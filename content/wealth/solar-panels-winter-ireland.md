<p class="cs-kicker">THE QUICK ANSWER · IRELAND · UPDATED OCTOBER 2026</p>
<p><strong>Yes, solar panels work in winter in Ireland. But they produce far less electricity in December and January than in late spring or summer.</strong> A published PVGIS-based Dublin reference profile, scaled to a 4 kWp system, suggests roughly <strong>136 kWh in December</strong>, <strong>151 kWh in January</strong> and <strong>502 kWh in May</strong>. These are long-term modelled averages, not guaranteed bills savings or a forecast for this coming winter.</p>

Winter is the question that can stop an Irish homeowner from buying solar panels: *What is the point of generating electricity when the sun barely comes out and the heating bill is highest?*

The answer is about the **whole year**, not one December week. Solar electricity is generated in winter daylight—even with cloud cover—but the shorter days, lower solar angle and more frequent shading reduce total production. A battery can shift output from afternoon to evening; it cannot move the excess electricity from June into January.

The question is therefore **how much solar you can use or export over 12 months, what it costs to install and how much you still need to buy from the grid in winter.**

## 1. How much electricity can solar panels produce each month in Ireland?

This comparison scales a publicly published **Dublin PVGIS-based 10 kWp monthly reference** to 4, 6 and 8 kWp. The source assumes a **south-facing array, 35° tilt and 14% system losses**, with a multi-year climate average. Compound has recalculated and visualised the values; we did **not** query the PVGIS API independently for this article.

## 2. Will panels actually lower your electricity bill in December and January?

Yes, **if the electricity offsets grid imports or is exported for payment**. But a kWh generated is not automatically a kWh deducted from your bill at the full retail rate.

Consider a **4 kWp Dublin reference array** and a household using an illustrative **650 kWh of electricity per month** across December, January and February:

## 3. What are the actual winter savings in euros?

Here is an example with **30 c/kWh** avoided grid-import cost and **18 c/kWh** export payment. These are **assumptions for illustration**, not verified current supplier rates. Suppose half of December's approximately **136 kWh** is used directly and half exported:

- About 68 kWh used in the home × €0.30 = **€20.40** in avoided unit charges.
- About 68 kWh exported × €0.18 = **€12.24** in export income.
- Combined December electricity value = **about €32.64**.

If you managed to use **all** 136 kWh, the energy-only value at 30 c/kWh would be **€40.80**. If all of it were exported at 18 c/kWh, it would be **€24.48**. These are limiting examples, not predictions of a typical home's self-consumption fraction.

The economics depend on your **day rate, night rate, export credit, when appliances run and how much solar is available**. Our [Solar Export Rate Comparator](/solar/export-rates/) checks import prices and export payments together.

## 5. Why winter output falls even though solar panels still work in cold weather

**Shorter days.** Winter gives each panel fewer hours of daylight in which to generate electricity.

**Lower sun angle.** The sun sits lower in the sky, so roof geometry and nearby obstacles become more important.

**Shading.** Chimneys, dormers, neighbouring roofs and trees may obstruct sunlight for longer periods when the sun is low.

**Weather variability.** A bright cold day can produce useful output; a dark rainy day may produce very little. A monthly climate average is not a guaranteed minimum.

**Snow or frost.** Irish conditions vary. Snow can obstruct panels, while frost and cold temperatures alone are not evidence that a panel has stopped generating. The cover image shows an illustrative snowy European roof, not a typical Irish-weather prediction.

If your house has a split roof, see our [East- vs West-Facing Solar Panels guide](/wealth/east-west-facing-solar-panels-ireland/) for the interaction between roof direction and the timing of demand.

## 6. Would installing 6 or 8 kWp instead of 4 kWp solve winter bills?

A larger array generally raises potential winter production, **provided the extra panels have comparably productive roof space**. In the Dublin reference, scaling the December total produces about:

| System size | Modelled December output | Theoretical maximum share of 650 kWh December demand |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 4 kWp | 136 kWh | 21% |
| 6 kWp | 205 kWh | 32% |
| 8 kWp | 273 kWh | 42% |

Those percentages are **ceilings based solely on monthly energy totals**: they assume perfect timing and every solar kWh consumed at home. They are not projected self-consumption rates.

Going bigger also costs money, and increased summer generation might be exported at a lower unit value than imports avoided. Choose size based on **annual usage, daily patterns, roof potential, quote price and the import/export tariff split**. Read [4 kWp vs 6 kWp vs 8 kWp in Ireland](/wealth/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need-ireland/) and compare installer assumptions in the [Solar Quote Calculator](/solar/compare-quotes/).

## 7. Does a home battery make Irish winter solar worthwhile?

A home battery can store some electricity generated in daylight and discharge it later, subject to usable battery capacity and charging/discharging losses.

What it **cannot** do is carry excess summer generation forward for months. If the roof generates very little on a dull December day, the solar battery has very little *solar* electricity to store. Some households can also charge a battery on an off-peak electricity tariff, but that is a separate tariff-arbitrage calculation rather than free solar power.

An illustrative 5 kWh battery that receives only 2 kWh of spare solar energy on a winter day **cannot create 5 kWh of solar supply**: the shortage is generation, not storage capacity. Export income forgone and the battery's installed cost also matter.

SEAI now lists a separate **€600 grant for qualifying batteries of at least 5 kWh**, subject to scheme conditions and approval *before work starts*. Check the [grant checker](/solar/grants/) and model the battery's *incremental* economics in the [Battery Payback Calculator](/solar/batteries/) rather than assuming every grant-backed battery is worthwhile.

## 8. Can solar panels make financial sense if December performance is poor?

Often, yes—but the answer must come from **annual net cash value**, not just the highest monthly output.

Here is a simplified **4 kWp illustrative investment**, with no battery:

| Input | Example assumption |
| --- | ---: |
| Installed price before grant | €9,000 |
| Illustrative eligible SEAI solar PV grant | €1,800 |
| Net cost after assumed grant | **€7,200** |
| Modelled annual Dublin 4 kWp output | ~3,930 kWh |
| Share consumed directly in the home | 35% |
| Avoided import unit price | 30 c/kWh |
| Export income unit rate | 18 c/kWh |
| Modelled annual energy-only benefit | **~€873** |
| Simple payback | **~8.3 years** |

Formula: annual modelled kWh × (35% × €0.30 + 65% × €0.18), followed by €7,200 ÷ annual value.

This is **not** a quote or a forecast. It assumes every modelled kWh is either self-consumed or compensated at the stated export rate, excludes system degradation, downtime, inverter replacement, servicing, insurance, time value of money and future tariff changes, and assumes grant eligibility. A 35% self-consumption fraction is an example—not a claim about the average Irish home.

The important result is structural: **winter production can be weak yet the year-round return can still be positive**. Conversely, an expensive oversized system may offer disappointing payback despite high annual production.

## 9. How to check your exact home in PVGIS

The [European Commission's PVGIS grid-connected calculator](https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/pvgis-online-tool_en) produces average monthly and annual output estimates using location, panel size, technology, slope, orientation and loss assumptions.

1. Find your house on the map, or select your nearest suitable location.
2. Enter **peak installed capacity (kWp)**, e.g. 4, 6 or 8.
3. Enter roof slope in degrees and orientation; PVGIS uses south = 0°, east = −90°, west = +90°.
4. Enter an appropriate system-loss assumption. **14%** is the reference used in the figures above, not a mandatory value.
5. Review the **monthly output chart** and year-to-year variation, then compare it with your smart-meter usage for those same months.
6. Check whether the installer has incorporated chimneys, tree shade, roof obstructions and inverter sizing. PVGIS's general horizon treatment is not a substitute for a site-specific shading study.

Ask for the **monthly kWh output, not just annual savings claimed in a brochure**. The source of each output assumption should be explained.

## 10. Five questions to ask an installer about winter solar

- Can you show **December, January and February** production individually—not merely a year's total?
- What are the roof-orientation, tilt, losses and **shading assumptions** behind those monthly estimates?
- How much electricity is likely to be **used in my home versus exported**, and what are the assumed import and CEG export rates?
- What would adding a battery cost **after the applicable grant**, and what incremental cash benefit does it add beyond solar-only?
- What happens to estimated payback if the installation quote rises by €1,000 or the export tariff falls?

## Frequently asked questions

### Do solar panels generate electricity on cloudy winter days?

Yes, panels can produce electricity using diffuse daylight, though output may be much lower than in bright conditions. Cloud cover and available daylight vary greatly from day to day.

### What is the worst month for solar panels in Ireland?

In the **specific published Dublin PVGIS-derived reference** used here, **December** produces the least monthly electricity, with roughly **136 kWh for 4 kWp**. Other sites and actual weather years can differ.

### Can you run a heat pump on solar in December?

Solar generation can offset some of a heat pump's consumption during daylight, but it should not be assumed to supply the full heating demand. Heating demand often rises just when solar output falls. See our [Heat Pumps in Ireland guide](/wealth/heat-pumps-ireland/) and model actual winter usage.

### Do I need a battery to benefit from solar in winter?

No. You can use solar electricity directly and may receive export payments for eligible surplus generation. Storage is an additional economic choice, not a prerequisite for PV output.

### How do I calculate solar panel payback in Ireland?

Compare the **net installed cost** with your annual electricity import savings plus export value, accounting for time of use and costs. Use the [Solar Payback Calculator](/solar-payback-calculator/) with your real quote and tariff, not a flat national savings promise.
