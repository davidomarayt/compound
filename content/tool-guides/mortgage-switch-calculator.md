## What this calculator is for

Switching a mortgage can reduce the interest rate, but a lower headline rate is not enough to prove that a switch is worthwhile. Legal and valuation costs, a fixed-rate break fee, cashback, the new mortgage term and the balance still outstanding after a few years can all change the economics.

This calculator therefore gives you two views:

- a **shorter comparison horizon**, which is useful when the entered rates are expected to apply for only a limited fixed-rate period; and
- a **scheduled lifetime comparison**, which shows what would happen if the entered rates remained unchanged for the full remaining terms.

The second view is deliberately labelled as a constant-rate illustration rather than a forecast.

## How to read the result

Start with the monthly repayment, but do not stop there.

A lower monthly payment can come from either a lower interest rate or a longer repayment term. If the alternative mortgage is stretched over more years, the payment can fall while the balance declines more slowly.

The most useful short-term outputs are:

- current-mortgage interest over the comparison horizon;
- alternative-mortgage interest plus net switching cost over that horizon;
- the difference between those financing costs; and
- the difference between the two remaining mortgage balances at the horizon.

Those figures let you see whether an apparently attractive monthly saving is being achieved by genuine interest reduction or partly by repaying principal more slowly.

## Worked example

Assume a borrower has **€250,000** outstanding with **20 years** remaining at **4.2%**.

The scheduled repayment is about **€1,541 per month**.

An alternative mortgage at **3.4%** over the same 20-year term is about **€1,437 per month**, a reduction of roughly €104 a month.

If the net switching cost is **€1,500** and both rates are assumed to hold for a five-year comparison horizon, the model estimates roughly:

- €48,078 of interest on the current mortgage during those five years;
- €38,637 of interest on the alternative mortgage;
- €40,137 when the €1,500 net switching cost is added to the alternative;
- about **€7,941 less interest and switching cost** over the five-year horizon; and
- an alternative balance about **€3,180 lower** after five years because the terms are the same and the lower rate allows more of each payment to reduce principal.

If the new mortgage term were lengthened, that last balance comparison could change materially even if the monthly payment looked better.

## Why the comparison horizon matters

Many Irish mortgage offers fix a rate for a limited period rather than for the entire mortgage term. CCPC notes that fixed-rate periods in the Irish market are often much shorter than the total mortgage term.

If you enter a three-year fixed rate but leave a 20-year mortgage term, treating 3.4% as though it were guaranteed for all 20 years would overstate the certainty of the lifetime result.

Advanced mode therefore lets you choose a comparison horizon.

For a fixed-rate-to-fixed-rate comparison, a sensible approach is to choose a horizon no longer than the period during which the rates being compared are reasonably known. If the new offer is fixed for five years, a five-year horizon can be more informative than pretending the rate is fixed for 20 years.

You can then rerun the calculator with different future-rate assumptions separately.

## Switching costs belong in the comparison

Moving lender can involve costs such as:

- solicitor or conveyancing fees;
- valuation fees;
- a BER assessment where relevant to a green mortgage;
- a fixed-rate early-repayment or break fee;
- other administrative costs.

Cashback or a switching incentive offsets those costs, but it should not be allowed to hide a more expensive mortgage.

The calculator defines **net switching cost** as:

**switching costs + break fee − cashback**

Unlike the previous simpler version of the tool, the calculation does not force this number to zero. If cashback genuinely exceeds the costs entered, the result can be negative, representing a net upfront incentive.

That still does not make the product cheaper over time. The rate and balance path remain more important.

## Simple cash-flow break-even has limits

The calculator also shows a simple break-even based on the monthly repayment saving.

If net switching cost is €1,200 and the repayment falls by €100 a month, the simple cash-flow break-even is about 12 months.

This is intuitive, but it is not a complete economic measure when the mortgage terms differ.

For example, a 30-year alternative can produce a much lower monthly payment than a 20-year current mortgage simply because the debt is being repaid more slowly. In that case, the monthly saving may generate a fast-looking break-even even though the borrower still owes substantially more after five or ten years.

That is why the horizon balance difference is shown beside the interest/fee comparison.

## Compare the same term first

The cleanest first comparison keeps the remaining term unchanged and varies the interest rate and switching costs.

That answers: **what does the lower rate itself do?**

Once you understand that scenario, deliberately test a longer or shorter new term if cash-flow flexibility is part of the objective.

This sequence prevents term extension from being mistaken for rate saving.

## Cashback deserves a separate stress test

CCPC cautions borrowers to look beyond cashback and compare the overall mortgage cost.

A useful process is:

1. run the real offer including cashback;
2. rerun it with cashback set to zero;
3. compare the interest and balance results over the fixed-rate horizon.

If the alternative still looks materially better without the incentive, the economics are being driven by the mortgage itself rather than by a one-off payment.

Also check the product terms for any cashback clawback or conditions.

## Your current lender may have another option

A switch does not always require moving lender.

If your loan-to-value ratio has improved because the balance has fallen or the property value has risen, or if the home's BER has improved, the existing lender may have another product or rate available.

A same-lender rate change can have different costs and administrative requirements from a full legal switch.

Use the calculator by treating that rate as the "alternative" and entering only the costs that genuinely apply.

## Fixed-rate break fees can change quickly

If you are leaving a fixed rate early, ask the current lender for an actual break-fee quote.

A break charge can depend on factors such as the amount repaid, time remaining in the fixed period and market interest-rate conditions. A generic estimate can therefore be misleading.

Advanced mode has a dedicated break-fee field so you can replace guesswork with the lender's current figure.

## Loan-to-value, BER and underwriting still matter

A mathematically attractive switch does not mean the new lender will approve it.

A new lender can assess income, credit history, repayment capacity, property value and documentation much like a fresh mortgage application. LTV and BER can also affect the range of products and rates available.

Use the rate from an offer or realistic quote where possible rather than the lowest rate seen in an advertisement.

## What can change the answer

The largest drivers are:

- mortgage balance;
- current and alternative rates;
- current and alternative terms;
- comparison horizon;
- legal, valuation and other switching costs;
- fixed-rate break fee;
- cashback or switching incentive.

Future rates after the comparison horizon are unknown. That is precisely why the tool keeps the shorter horizon separate from the lifetime constant-rate illustration.

## Common mistakes to avoid

Do not compare monthly payments alone. Do not lengthen the term without checking the remaining balance. Do not assume a cashback mortgage is cheaper. Do not omit a fixed-rate break fee. And do not interpret the lifetime difference as a prediction where either entered rate is only fixed for a few years.

Mortgage protection arrangements also need attention during a switch; do not cancel existing cover before replacement arrangements are confirmed.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a lower monthly repayment always better?
No. It can reflect a lower rate, a longer term, or both. Compare the remaining balance as well.

### What comparison horizon should I use?
For a fixed-rate comparison, use a period no longer than the period for which the entered rates are a reasonable assumption. Five years is the Basic-mode default.

### Does the tool include break fees?
Yes in Advanced mode, but you need to enter the actual or best available quote.

### Can cashback make net switching cost negative?
Yes. The calculator allows cashback to exceed the other upfront costs entered. That represents a net upfront incentive, not proof that the mortgage is cheaper overall.

### Does the lifetime result predict future mortgage rates?
No. It assumes the entered rates never change and is labelled accordingly.

### Should I ask my current lender for another rate?
Yes. A same-lender product change can be worth comparing with a full lender switch.

## Method and limitations

For each mortgage, the calculator uses the standard annuity repayment formula from the entered balance, rate and term.

It calculates:

**net switching cost = switching costs + break fee − cashback**

The simple monthly break-even divides positive net switching cost by the monthly repayment reduction where there is one.

For the comparison horizon, the calculator amortises each mortgage month by month at the entered rate and records:

- interest accumulated during the horizon;
- balance remaining at the horizon.

The alternative horizon cost shown is:

**alternative interest + net switching cost**

The horizon saving is:

**current-mortgage interest − alternative horizon cost**

The balance advantage is:

**current balance at horizon − alternative balance at horizon**

A positive balance advantage means the alternative mortgage leaves less debt outstanding at that point.

The scheduled lifetime difference compares total scheduled repayments plus net switching cost, assuming both entered rates remain constant for their full terms.

The model does not forecast future rates, model variable-rate changes, calculate APRC, determine mortgage approval, value mortgage protection, calculate tax effects, or reproduce lender-specific daily-interest rounding. It is a decision-support model, not a loan offer.
