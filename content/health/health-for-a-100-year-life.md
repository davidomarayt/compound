Think of something you would like to keep being able to do.

It might be lifting a bag onto a train, getting out to a match, cooking dinner or following the conversation around a busy table. It might be taking part with an aid, an adapted activity or another person's help. Those ordinary capabilities give a health plan a useful direction.

[Part 1](/live-to-100/) introduced the difference between a long life and the experience of those years. Here the question becomes practical: where is the evidence strong enough to guide everyday choices, where does individual care matter, and where should we resist a confident sales pitch?

No routine guarantees a hundredth birthday. There is, however, a great deal worth doing for health and daily life without that guarantee. The task is to find priorities that fit your body, circumstances and access to support.

## Start with capability, including support {#capability}

The WHO defines healthy ageing around the ability to do what people value. It includes the interaction between a person's capacities and the environment around them. Being completely free of disease is not a requirement. [WHO (1)](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/healthy-ageing-and-functional-ability).

That definition changes the conversation. A walking aid that makes a visit possible, treatment that makes pain more manageable or help preparing meals can belong in a good health plan. Independence need not mean doing everything without assistance.

Write down two or three activities that matter to you and anything making them harder. Bring a specific concern to the relevant professional: a GP, physiotherapist, occupational therapist, dietitian, pharmacist, dentist or another member of your care team. “I am avoiding the stairs because I feel unsteady” is a more useful starting point than a general wish to optimise ageing.

The priorities may change after an illness, operation or new diagnosis. A plan can make room for treatment, rehabilitation, comfort and enjoyment without treating a change in ability as a failure.

## Make room for movement, strength and balance {#movement}

Walking is valuable, but a rounded activity plan also asks about strength and balance. The HSE's adult guidance includes at least **150 minutes of moderate activity a week** and strength work on at least two days. Its older-adult guidance includes activities for balance and strength on at least three days. It also encourages less sedentary time and gradual progress. [HSE (2)](https://www2.hse.ie/living-well/exercise/increase-activity/).

These are population guidelines, not an entry test. If you are inactive, disabled, recovering or living with a condition that limits exertion, ask about an appropriate starting point and adaptation. Doing a manageable amount consistently may be a better beginning than attempting the full target immediately. A programme that causes sustained worsening needs review, not more determination.

<p class="eyebrow">THREE QUESTIONS FOR YOUR WEEK</p>
<p><strong>What gets me moving? What challenges my muscles? What helps me stay steady?</strong> The answers can overlap. Ask for guidance on suitable activities, technique and progression if you are uncertain.</p>
</aside>

The strongest reason to care about physical function is not a before-and-after photograph. In the **LIFE randomised trial**, 1,635 sedentary US adults aged 70–89 with physical limitations, but initially able to walk 400 metres, received either a structured activity programme or health education. Over an average 2.6 years, loss of the ability to walk 400 metres occurred in 30.1% of the activity group and 35.5% of the comparison group. That is a 5.4 percentage-point difference. [Pahor and colleagues (3)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24866862/).

This supports an appropriately designed programme for a studied group. It does not establish an extra number of years of life, or make the same exercises suitable for everyone. The trial involved support and a defined programme; copying a demanding online workout is a different proposition.

For everyday planning, think about what makes participation realistic: transport, a welcoming setting, cost, weather, access to a toilet, a companion or an activity available at home. These details can determine whether a good intention becomes something repeatable.

## Know what routine prevention is trying to prevent {#prevention}

Some risks are quiet. High blood pressure usually has no symptoms, so feeling well does not tell you whether it is raised. The HSE recommends checks, and notes that one high reading does not by itself establish hypertension; repeat or home monitoring may be needed. [HSE (4)](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/high-blood-pressure-hypertension/).

Ask your clinician which checks are appropriate given your age, family history, medicines and existing conditions. Blood pressure, cholesterol or blood glucose may be relevant, but there is no universal “longevity blood panel” prescribed here. A test is useful when its result can inform a sound decision in your circumstances.

If treatment has been recommended, ask what benefit is expected, what side effects to watch for and when it should be reviewed. Lifestyle measures and medicines can work together. Do not stop prescribed treatment because a supplement or wearable produces an encouraging number.

Our [blood pressure article](/health/lower-blood-pressure-naturally-what-works/) explores the evidence behind common lifestyle measures. Use that knowledge to support a conversation, rather than to choose a treatment target without clinical context.

## Eat for nourishment as circumstances change {#food}

A useful starting pattern is familiar food: vegetables and fruit, higher-fibre starchy foods, sources of protein, and dairy or suitable alternatives, with attention to the overall balance. The HSE's food guidance supports variety rather than reliance on a single supposedly exceptional ingredient. [HSE (5)](https://www2.hse.ie/living-well/healthy-eating/how-to-eat-well/).

An ordinary meal can do several jobs. Porridge with milk or a fortified alternative and fruit; beans on wholegrain toast; eggs, fish or tofu with vegetables and potatoes. These are meal ideas, not a diet prescription. Allergies, kidney disease, swallowing difficulties and other conditions can require different advice.

Later life does not always call for eating less. Unintended weight loss, reduced appetite or growing weakness deserve attention. The HSE identifies these among signs that can warrant assessment for malnutrition, and stresses that weight loss is not an inevitable consequence of old age. [HSE (6)](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/malnutrition/). Ask about help with the cause as well as the food: pain, shopping, cooking and eating alone may belong in the discussion.

Vitamin D is a specific Irish consideration. The HSE currently advises adults aged **65 and over to take 15 micrograms daily throughout the year**. For ages 13–64, its advice is 15 micrograms from 31 October to 17 March, with year-round use in specified circumstances, including darker skin, reduced sun exposure or pregnancy. Follow individual prescribing advice where it differs; more is not automatically better. [HSE (7)](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/).

The [macronutrients and micronutrients guide](/health/macronutrients-micronutrients-guide-ireland/) covers the detail without turning every meal into a calculation. A useful first question here is whether your current eating pattern is sufficient, enjoyable and manageable. If it is becoming difficult, seek help before assuming another restriction is the answer.

## Give sleep, smoking and alcohol their proper weight {#everyday}

The HSE describes **seven to nine hours** as typical adult sleep needs. Persistent daytime sleepiness, months of poor sleep or a problem affecting daily life are reasons to seek professional help. Shift work, caring, health conditions and medicines can complicate sleep; it is not simply a matter of choosing an earlier bedtime. [HSE (8)](https://www2.hse.ie/mental-health/issues/sleep-problems/).

Make the question practical: do you have a reasonable opportunity to sleep, and is that sleep restorative? A routine may help, but a recurring difficulty deserves assessment rather than endless self-monitoring. Our [adult sleep guide](/health/how-much-sleep-do-adults-need/) develops the everyday questions.

If you smoke, the HSE's **Quit** service offers routes to support, including stop-smoking advisers, clinics and information on nicotine replacement therapy. [HSE (9)](https://www2.hse.ie/living-well/quit-smoking/). Help belongs in the plan; you do not need to present quitting as a test of character or do it entirely alone.

Alcohol should not be added to a routine for supposed longevity benefits. The HSE states that there is no level of alcohol use that is safe for health and that drinking less reduces risk. Its page also notes a review of the evidence during 2026. [HSE (10)](https://www2.hse.ie/living-well/alcohol/health/improve-your-health/weekly-low-risk-alcohol-guidelines/). If you may be dependent or get withdrawal symptoms, talk to your GP before stopping abruptly: withdrawal can be serious. [HSE (11)](https://www2.hse.ie/living-well/alcohol/dependence/withdrawal-symptoms/).

## Look after your brain without promising protection {#brain}

Brain health belongs with the rest of health. WHO guidance describes dementia risk reduction through healthy behaviours, management of relevant conditions and attention to environmental risks. These are ways to reduce risk at a population level, not a guarantee that a particular person will avoid dementia. [WHO (12)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/dementia).

There is a difference between making a sensible effort and taking responsibility for every eventual outcome. Developing dementia does not demonstrate that someone failed to exercise, learn enough or maintain the right attitude. A family history or worrying change is a reason for an informed conversation, not a verdict.

Keep daily participation in view too. If hearing or vision difficulties make conversation, reading or getting out harder, ask about assessment and support. The immediate purpose can be clearer communication and a more workable day. It does not need to rest on a claim that an aid will prevent dementia.

Learning, music, reading and conversation can be worthwhile because you enjoy them. A commercial brain score is a different thing from evidence that an activity prevents disease. The [Happiness article](/happiness/happiness-for-a-100-year-life/) considers interests and connection without making them another performance test.

## Use screening and vaccination for their intended purpose {#screening}

Ireland has defined screening programmes, including BreastCheck, CervicalCheck, BowelScreen and Diabetic RetinaScreen, each with its own eligibility and process. The HSE also provides current vaccination guidance, including flu and COVID-19 information. [HSE directory (13)](https://www2.hse.ie/screening-and-vaccinations/). Check the current programme page, your invitation or your healthcare team; age ranges and seasonal arrangements can change.

Screening and assessment of symptoms are different. If something new or concerning develops, contact an appropriate clinician rather than waiting for the next screening round or treating a previous normal result as an explanation. The programme is one part of care.

At appointments, mention practical difficulties with taking medicines, attending follow-ups or understanding instructions. Ask for an explanation you can use, and bring a current medicines list. A plan has to work between appointments, not only look sensible while it is being discussed.

## Read longevity claims by looking at the outcome {#claims}

When a product promises to slow ageing, ask what was actually measured. A result in cells or mice is not a result in people. A change in a blood marker is not the same as fewer heart attacks, better function or longer survival. A testimonial does not tell you what would have happened without the product.

One useful example is a **2023 analysis from CALERIE**, a two-year calorie-restriction trial in healthy adults. It found an effect on one DNA-based pace-of-ageing measure, but not on two other ageing clocks. The authors explicitly said longer follow-up was needed to establish whether the changes translated into less disease or longer life. [Waziry and colleagues (14)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43587-022-00357-y). The result is a research finding, not a reason to prescribe calorie restriction to an older person at risk of undernutrition.

<aside class="series-note" aria-label="Questions to ask about a longevity claim">
<p class="eyebrow">BEFORE YOU BUY THE PROMISE</p>
<p><strong>In whom? Compared with what? For how long? With what harms?</strong> Look for human evidence, a meaningful comparison and outcomes that matter to patients. Ask whether the exact product and dose were studied, and who paid for the research.</p>
</aside>

An expensive test should also have a purpose. Ask how a result would change care, what a false alarm could lead to and whether there is evidence that acting on it improves outcomes. Curiosity is reasonable; certainty should be earned.

## Build a plan you can live with {#begin}

You do not need to change every part of your life at once. Choose one activity you want to keep doing, one concern that needs professional attention and one adjustment that could make your week more manageable. The right order depends on the problem in front of you.

If support is becoming necessary, include your preferences early. Where would you like care delivered? Who should be involved? What treatments or daily priorities matter most? The Decision Support Service explains how an **advance healthcare directive** can record treatment wishes for a future loss of decision-making capacity. [DSS (15)](https://www.decisionsupportservice.ie/advance-planning). This is a distinct process with formal requirements, not something this article can complete for you.

The [Wealth article](/wealth/wealth-for-a-100-year-life/) considers housing and the practical funding of care. The final part turns to the relationships and activities that make those arrangements worth having. Across all three, the aim is a life you can participate in, with appropriate help and room for enjoyment now.
