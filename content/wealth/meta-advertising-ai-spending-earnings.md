**Meta’s advertising engine is growing strongly. The harder question is how much of that success will reach shareholders after the infrastructure bill.**

Its results, released on 21 October, make the distinction unusually clear. A profitable company can still consume cash after investment. For shareholders, the challenge is to judge whether that investment creates future earning power at an acceptable price.

*Updated after publication of the SEC earnings release. This replaces our earlier preview at the same URL.*

## Meta earnings at a glance

| Metric | Q3 2026 |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | $64.601bn; +26% annually |
| GAAP operating income | $12.988bn |
| GAAP diluted EPS | $3.83 |
| Operating cash flow | $32.79bn |
| Capital expenditure, including finance-lease principal | $34.91bn |
| Free cash flow, non-GAAP | −$2.12bn |

*Source: Meta’s SEC 8-K, Exhibit 99.1; quarter ended 30 September 2026. US dollars.*

## The earnings headline needs an accounting explanation

A $9.89bn litigation charge depressed profit. Meta says excluding it would produce $7.40 EPS. The prior-year comparison also contains an exceptional tax charge.

Investors should resist both extremes: treating an unusual charge as proof that the operating business has collapsed, or removing it and behaving as though shareholders never bear its cost. Adjustments can improve comparisons without making the underlying obligation economically irrelevant.

That is why the reported result belongs beside any adjusted interpretation. A clean-looking earnings number is useful only if readers understand what has been excluded and why.

## Advertising growth has two drivers

Ad impressions rose 10% and average ad prices 14%. In its 21 October release, Meta guided Q4 revenue to $73–76bn and full-year capital expenditure to $135–145bn.

More impressions and stronger pricing offer different information. The former concerns the volume of advertising delivered; the latter reflects what advertisers pay across the mix of inventory. Neither measure alone isolates the contribution of AI.

For an investor, the important chain of evidence runs from better advertiser outcomes to repeat spending, then to cash generation after the cost of serving those advertisers. A confident management narrative should be tested against that chain rather than accepted as a substitute for it.

## Why the cash-flow deficit matters

Infrastructure spending generally hits cash before its full cost appears in earnings through depreciation. Rapid expansion can therefore make current accounting profit a poor guide to the amount available for distribution.

A cash deficit during investment is not automatically a failure. It does increase the importance of utilisation, pricing and useful asset lives. The economic question is whether the eventual cash returns compensate for the resources committed and the risk taken now.

*Filing update — 29 October 2026:* Meta’s [Q3 Form 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1326801/000162828026066773/meta-20260930.htm) adds a longer-term funding consideration. At 30 September, it reported approximately **$355.73 billion of operating and finance lease obligations for leases that had not yet commenced**. These cover infrastructure including data centres and energy facilities, with commencement dates extending through 2036 and lease terms of more than one year to 30 years. This is a future contractual obligation measure, not expenditure paid in Q3 or an annual spending forecast. It reinforces the need to assess committed capacity alongside current cash flow, without treating the entire amount as immediately payable.

## How much of Meta’s cash is available for general use?

*Research update — 11 November 2026, using the June and September filings.*

Meta’s September balance sheet reports $11.222 billion of cash and cash equivalents and $71.227 billion of marketable securities. Together they total **$82.449 billion**, matching the rounded $82.45 billion in its results release. Its separate cash-flow reconciliation includes another $13.132 billion of restricted cash and restricted cash equivalents. Keeping those categories distinct prevents an investor from overstating the resources management can deploy freely. [September filing, balance sheet and cash reconciliation](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1326801/000162828026066773/meta-20260930.htm)

| US$ billions, at the reporting date | 30 June 2026 | 30 September 2026 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Cash and cash equivalents | 15.462 | 11.222 |
| Marketable securities | 74.798 | 71.227 |
| Sum of the two balance-sheet lines | 90.260 | 82.449 |
| Restricted cash and equivalents in current assets | 0.702 | 0.597 |
| Restricted cash and equivalents in other assets | 13.107 | 12.535 |
| Total restricted cash and equivalents | 13.809 | 13.132 |

The first two lines fell by **$7.811 billion**, or about **8.7%**, between the reporting dates. Restricted cash and equivalents fell by $677 million. These are changes in balance-sheet stocks across three months, with marketable securities subject to valuation and portfolio movements. Each measure answers a different liquidity question from quarterly free cash flow. [June filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1326801/000162828026050705/meta-20260630.htm) · [September filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1326801/000162828026066773/meta-20260930.htm)

Within restricted cash equivalents, both filings identify **$10.80 billion held in escrow for multi-year infrastructure purchase agreements**. Meta expects release during 2028–2030 as the underlying purchase obligations are satisfied. The unchanged amount across these two reports means investors should treat this as an existing commitment when interpreting September’s figures. The release dates are management’s expectations, and satisfying an infrastructure obligation can direct money to the agreed purchase. It does not establish that the same money will become available for buybacks or dividends. [June, Note 4](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1326801/000162828026050705/meta-20260630.htm) · [September, Note 4](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1326801/000162828026066773/meta-20260930.htm)

For capital-allocation analysis, start with the $82.449 billion balance, then separately assess the timing of restricted funds, borrowing and future purchase commitments. Subtracting the $10.80 billion escrow from the headline balance a second time would understate that balance, because the escrow is already classified elsewhere. Adding it as freely available liquidity would overstate flexibility. This is a classification question with a direct consequence for estimates of how much further investment Meta can fund from existing resources.

The next useful disclosure is whether additional agreements require new cash to be ring-fenced, and how quickly existing restrictions are released against purchases. That would help distinguish a growing cash reserve from a growing pool of cash already assigned to infrastructure. The amount held in escrow alone establishes neither the profitability of the eventual assets nor a liquidity crisis.

## The new share class adds a governance question

Meta also announced non-voting Class C shares, planned for January 2027, with one new share per existing Class A or B share. At the announcement, payment dates remained to be set; the distribution itself leaves voting power unchanged.

A stock distribution does not mechanically create value. Shareholders should distinguish the division of their existing economic interest from any subsequent issuance to fund investments, compensation or acquisitions. Later issuance can affect ownership economics even when control remains concentrated.

That makes capital allocation and accountability inseparable. Investors need a view on both the prospective returns and who decides how much capital to commit.
