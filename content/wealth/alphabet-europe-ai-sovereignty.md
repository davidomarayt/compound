**Google wants European businesses to move faster on AI adoption. Europe also wants more control over the technology it depends on. For Alphabet, that tension is a commercial question as well as a political one.**

In a Financial Times interview published on 20 October, Google executive Kent Walker argued against European fears of US AI dominance and advocated collaboration with Europe. The [report](https://www.ft.com/content/2202b6a7-9645-4a0f-995b-40f3e939fbac) provides a timely starting point for examining who supplies Europe's AI—and who controls the terms.

Google has a commercial interest in broad adoption. European customers have an interest in useful services, competitive prices and the ability to change providers. Those objectives can overlap, but they are not identical.

## What does European AI sovereignty mean?

The European Commission’s [Cloud and AI Development Act policy page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/cloud-and-ai-development-act) identifies dependence on non-EU providers as a risk to autonomy and resilience. Its stated approach combines greater infrastructure capacity, a sovereignty assessment framework and support for adoption, while keeping most of the market open to international partners.

That is more nuanced than a blanket rejection of US technology. It asks how critical services remain dependable and controllable, alongside the question of which supplier offers the strongest product.

The policy page also identifies energy, land, financing and permitting as constraints on infrastructure expansion. Buying a European service does not, by itself, eliminate those physical limitations.

## Why this matters to Alphabet shareholders

For [Alphabet investors](/stocks/googl/), Europe presents both demand and conditions on how that demand can be served.

Our inference is that more AI adoption can create commercial opportunity, while requirements around control, portability or procurement may affect the cost and form of delivery. A supplier might need partnerships, additional operational arrangements or different contractual commitments. Their financial significance depends on the final applicable rules and individual contracts.

It would be premature to turn a political argument into a forecast of lost Google revenue. It would also be naive to assume that technical performance alone determines every public-sector purchasing decision.

## Search competition is a separate but related issue

The Commission’s [Google Search data-sharing guidance](https://digital-markets-act.ec.europa.eu/businesses-portal/data-access/alphabet-specification-proceedings-sharing-google-search-data_en) addresses access for eligible search competitors, including AI chatbots with search functions. This is a competition issue distinct from the sovereignty of a cloud service.

For investors, keeping the two separate is useful. A company can face questions about how it supplies cloud infrastructure and, independently, about access to data associated with a dominant search service. Combining all European regulation into one vague “AI crackdown” obscures the different mechanisms.

## The practical question for Irish businesses

An Irish organisation evaluating AI should ask more than where a provider is headquartered. Who can access sensitive information? What happens if service is interrupted? Can data and workflows move elsewhere? Which responsibilities remain with the customer?

These are procurement questions rather than a substitute for legal advice. Their relevance also depends on the application: drafting a low-risk marketing outline is different from operating a critical public service.

| Commercial attraction | Dependency question |
|---|---|
| Fast access to capable tools | Can the organisation switch providers? |
| An integrated technology stack | What is the cost of leaving it? |
| Global scale and investment | Who controls access and continuity? |
| Rapid product development | How are material changes governed? |

## The investment judgement

Europe’s choice is not simply to adopt everything or build everything itself. Different services can justify different combinations of international supply, domestic capability and contractual safeguards.

Alphabet’s opportunity is to make its technology valuable enough to win business while meeting those requirements economically. Its shareholders should watch customer adoption, contract conditions and the resources needed to comply—not just the tone of political exchanges.
