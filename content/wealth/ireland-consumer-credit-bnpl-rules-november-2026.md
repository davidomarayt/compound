**Ireland's revised consumer-credit rules start on Friday, 20 November 2026, extending the framework to more small loans, credit agreements up to €100,000 and products including Buy Now Pay Later.**

The Department of Finance announced the signed regulations on 16 November. The material update for borrowers is that the application date is now here: the revised EU Consumer Credit Directive applies from 20 November.

The changes do not make borrowing cheaper or turn a declined application into an entitlement to credit. They strengthen the information, assessment and review rights surrounding covered agreements.

## What changes from 20 November?

The [Department of Finance's announcement](https://www.gov.ie/en/department-of-finance/press-releases/t%C3%A1naiste-simon-harris-signs-new-regulations-enhancing-protections-for-borrowers/) identifies five main changes:

1. **More credit is covered.** The framework expands to loans below €200, covered agreements up to €100,000 and additional products including Buy Now Pay Later.
2. **Clearer information before signing.** Lenders must provide more standardised pre-contract information so consumers can compare the commitment.
3. **Stronger creditworthiness and affordability assessment.** The intention is to reduce irresponsible lending and over-indebtedness.
4. **A 14-day withdrawal right.** Consumers can withdraw from covered credit agreements up to €100,000, other than mortgages, within the first 14 days, subject to the applicable rules.
5. **Human intervention for automated decisions.** Where automated processing is used in a creditworthiness assessment, consumers have rights to an explanation, human intervention and review.

Mortgages remain under their separate mortgage-credit framework. Product scope, exclusions and transitional treatment still matter, so the date of the agreement and the actual contract should be checked.

## What this means for Buy Now Pay Later

Buy Now Pay Later is credit even when the checkout describes the instalments as interest-free.

Ireland already regulates BNPL providers. The [CCPC](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/borrowing-money/buy-now-pay-later) says providers have needed Central Bank authorisation since 2022 and are subject to consumer-protection rules. The 20 November framework broadens and standardises protection under the revised EU directive; it is not the first day on which BNPL becomes regulated in Ireland.

The practical improvement is that a very small agreement should not be dismissed as too minor for meaningful credit information merely because it is below €200.

Small instalments can accumulate. Imagine four purchases, each requiring a €50 payment in the same month:

> 4 × €50 = **€200 due that month**

That is the number a household budget needs to carry, regardless of whether each checkout looked manageable on its own.

## The 14-day withdrawal right is not free shopping

The right to withdraw concerns the **credit agreement**. It should not be confused with a blanket right to keep goods without paying or with the separate cancellation rights that can apply to an online purchase.

If you want to withdraw:

- use the lender's specified notice route within the deadline;
- keep a dated copy of the notice;
- ask the lender to confirm the amount and timing for repaying any credit already drawn; and
- deal separately with the retailer if goods are being returned.

Do not simply cancel a direct debit and assume the agreement has disappeared. The contract and the applicable rules govern repayment of money already advanced.

## Automated loan decisions: what “human review” can do

Digital lenders can assess an application quickly using automated processing. From 20 November, a consumer affected by automated creditworthiness processing has rights to an explanation, human intervention and review under the new framework.

That does not guarantee approval. A human reviewer can still conclude that the credit is unaffordable or that the lender's criteria are not met.

It can be useful where:

- income was categorised incorrectly;
- a closed debt appears current;
- irregular but reliable income was not understood;
- identity or address data were mismatched; or
- the applicant cannot tell which information drove the result.

Ask the lender what information was used, what can be corrected and how to request the review. Avoid sending sensitive documents through an unverified email address or messaging account.

## Affordability checks are not a safe-borrowing certificate

The [Central Bank's current rules](https://www.centralbank.ie/regulation/consumer-protection/consumer-protection-code/section-48-regulations/part-3-consumer-banking--credit--arrears-and-certain-other-financial-arrangements) already require regulated lenders to assess a personal consumer's likely ability to repay covered credit, hire-purchase, consumer-hire and BNPL debt.

A lender's approval does not establish that the payment is comfortable for your household. The assessment cannot know every future expense, income shock or competing goal.

Before accepting credit, calculate:

> total repayments + fees + interest − amount borrowed = **total cost of credit**

Then place every instalment on the same calendar. Compound's [loan repayment calculator](/loan-repayment-calculator/) can illustrate principal-and-interest loans, while the [debt repayment calculator](/debt-repayment-calculator/) can show how several balances compete for the same monthly cash.

## Small credit and the Central Credit Register

The new directive's scope and the Central Credit Register reporting threshold are different questions.

The [CCPC's BNPL guidance](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/borrowing-money/buy-now-pay-later) says a credit agreement of €500 or more may be reported to the Central Credit Register. The fact that sub-€200 credit receives wider consumer protection from 20 November does not automatically mean every small instalment is reported in the same way.

Missed payments can still lead to fees, blocked accounts, debt collection and difficulty obtaining future credit. Check the provider's reporting and late-payment terms before accepting the agreement.

## What information should you compare?

The monthly instalment is only one part of a borrowing decision.

| Check | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Amount borrowed | The cash or purchase price being financed |
| APR | A standardised annual measure that helps compare costs, where applicable |
| Total cost of credit | Interest and charges over the full agreement |
| Number and timing of payments | Whether several commitments collide on payday |
| Late fees and collection costs | What a missed payment could add |
| Fixed or variable rate | Whether the cost can change |
| Early-repayment terms | Whether paying sooner reduces the cost |
| Final or balloon payment | Whether a large amount remains at the end |

Interest-free does not necessarily mean consequence-free. A zero-interest agreement can still carry late fees, encourage overspending or reduce the cash available for rent, energy and other bills.

## Who enforces the new rules?

The Department says the **Central Bank of Ireland** is the competent authority for creditors, while the **Competition and Consumer Protection Commission** is responsible for credit intermediaries.

If something goes wrong:

1. complain to the provider in writing and keep the response;
2. identify whether the issue concerns the lender, retailer or credit intermediary;
3. check the Central Bank and CCPC registers where relevant; and
4. use the appropriate external complaints route if the provider does not resolve it.

The precise route can depend on the firm and product. The CCPC provides consumer information, while complaints about a regulated financial provider may ultimately fall within the Financial Services and Pensions Ombudsman's remit after the provider's process is completed.

## A five-minute check before clicking “Pay Later”

- Add every repayment due before your next two paydays.
- Compare the cash price with the total payable under credit.
- Read the late-fee and missed-payment section.
- Check whether the provider and any intermediary are authorised.
- Save the pre-contract information and agreement.
- If an automated refusal looks wrong, request an explanation and human review.

The new rules improve the framework around the decision. They do not remove the underlying trade-off: borrowing brings a future household bill into today's purchase.
