What would need to last if you had another seventy years ahead of you?

Imagine being 30 and reaching 100. Between those birthdays there could be several working chapters, friendships you have not yet made, places you have not yet called home and a long stretch beyond full-time employment. There would also be ordinary Tuesdays: making breakfast, paying bills, finding someone to talk to, deciding what to do with the afternoon.

That is the useful starting point for **Live to 100**, Compound’s four-part series on a longer life in Ireland. This opening article brings the questions together. The companion guides explore [Wealth](/wealth/wealth-for-a-100-year-life/), [Health](/health/health-for-a-100-year-life/) and [Happiness](/happiness/happiness-for-a-100-year-life/) in depth. Start here for the perspective, then follow the question that matters most to you.

**One hundred is a planning scenario, not a prediction.** Nothing in this series estimates your chance of reaching it. And being 30 does not put everyone at the same starting line: a person studying, raising children, recovering from illness or beginning again after a separation will have different choices. The point is to leave room for a future that may be longer, and less linear, than expected.

## Why this matters in Ireland {#ireland}

This is already a country with substantial later-life chapters. Two sets of official figures provide useful context, provided we keep their dates and meanings attached.

[live100:figures]

Life expectancy **at birth** describes the average lifespan a hypothetical newborn would have under the age-specific death rates of the reference period. It does not follow a real baby through the future. Remaining life expectancy **at a given age** is calculated from that age; you cannot obtain it by subtracting your age from the birth figure. Neither measure is a personal deadline or a probability of reaching 100. [Eurostat’s definition (3)](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:Life_expectancy).

The population figures answer a different question: who lives here now. They show why later life deserves attention in how we think about neighbourhoods, work, housing and care. They do not tell us that most people will become centenarians. No future population projection is needed to make that point.

## More years, and what those years contain {#healthspan}

Lifespan is the length of a life. Healthspan is a useful shorthand for the years spent in good health and with the capabilities that matter to us. The two are related, but asking only how long someone lives misses a great deal: whether they can hear a conversation, manage their daily routine, get outside or take part in something they enjoy.

Formal statistics use more precise measures. Eurostat’s **healthy life years** indicator combines mortality data with survey responses about health-related limitations in usual activities lasting at least six months. Its current explanatory article covers 2023 data. The survey is self-reported and excludes people in institutions such as care homes. This is not a count of perfectly healthy years, and subtracting it from life expectancy does not predict a fixed block of illness at the end of your life. [Eurostat’s methods and limitations (4)](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Healthy_life_years_statistics).

There is a more human distinction, too. Someone living with a long-term condition can still have a rich, enjoyable life. Someone who needs assistance still has preferences, relationships and things to contribute. The WHO’s approach to healthy ageing centres on people’s ability to do what they value, including with supportive environments; being entirely free of disease is not a requirement. [WHO (5)](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/healthy-ageing-and-functional-ability).

So the ambition is broader than avoiding diagnoses. It is to preserve options, adapt when circumstances change and make good support part of the plan.

## A life can have more than three chapters {#life-chapters}

Education, work, retirement: it is an easy timetable to picture. It can also be too narrow to describe a whole life. There may be study after a first career, a period of unpaid caring, a return to work, a change in hours or a new interest that becomes a serious commitment.

None of this means everyone should work longer. A demanding physical job, illness or caring responsibilities can make that suggestion unrealistic. For someone else, part-time work may offer structure, income and company they want to keep. The useful question is which arrangements could fit your circumstances, rather than which birthday should dictate the answer.

Take a deliberately simple example: stopping full-time work at 65 and reaching 100 leaves **35 years** between those ages. That is subtraction, not an Irish pension rule. The State Pension is generally available from 66 subject to eligibility; people born in or after 1958 can choose when to claim the contributory pension between 66 and 70. A chosen retirement date and access to a particular private pension are separate questions. [Department of Social Protection (6)](https://services.mywelfare.ie/en/topics/pensions-and-older-people/state-pension/).

## Four things worth making room for {#four-things}

### Money that leaves you choices

An ordinary day still costs money, whatever age you are. A longer planning horizon invites questions about how regular costs would be met, what happens after a setback and how much flexibility there is if work or health changes.

Inflation matters because a broad rise in prices reduces what a euro can buy. Your experience can also differ from the headline rate because your household buys a particular mix of goods and services. [European Central Bank (7)](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/ecb-and-you/explainers/tell-me-more/html/what_is_inflation.en.html). Thinking only in today’s prices can therefore make a future budget look more comfortable than it would feel.

A useful starting question is: which costs would continue if my wages stopped? Another is: what could change if I needed more help? There is no single savings target here. Someone renting alone, someone with a paid-off home and someone supporting another adult face different decisions. Investment returns, future prices and personal circumstances are uncertain.

[Part 2, Wealth](/wealth/wealth-for-a-100-year-life/), examines pensions, housing, care and the risks in a long financial plan. Compound’s [compound interest calculator](/compound-interest-calculator/) lets you explore how assumptions change an illustration. Its results are scenarios, not a guarantee of what you will earn or a verdict on what you ought to have saved.

### Physical and cognitive capability

Picture the activities you would like to keep: preparing a meal, following a conversation, walking to the shop, tending a garden or travelling to see a friend. This makes health less abstract than an annual list of numbers.

The WHO identifies regular physical activity, a balanced diet and avoiding tobacco as behaviours that can reduce chronic disease risk and support capacity. It also emphasises that ageing varies considerably between people. [WHO (8)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/ageing-and-health). These are reasons to care for your health, not evidence that a particular routine buys a specified number of extra years.

Think of capability as something to support and adapt. A person may use a walking aid, hearing aid, accessible transport or help at home and still pursue what matters to them. Asking for support can belong in a good plan from the outset.

[Part 3, Health](/health/health-for-a-100-year-life/), looks carefully at strength, cardiovascular and metabolic health, nutrition, sleep, brain health and prevention. Here, the task is simpler: identify something you value being able to do, and consider what appropriate support would help you keep doing it. Individual symptoms and treatment decisions belong with a qualified health professional.

### Relationships and a place in the community

Who could you call on a difficult afternoon? Who might notice you had not been around? These questions deserve space beside the pension statement and the health appointment.

Research connects social relationships with health outcomes, but its limits matter. A 2010 meta-analysis of 148 studies found an association between stronger social relationships and better survival during follow-up. It pooled populations from several regions and largely observational evidence; it does not prove that joining a club causes a particular person to live longer. [Holt-Lunstad and colleagues (9)](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316).

Connection also has value without a mortality statistic. It can mean being expected somewhere, sharing a joke, having someone to ask or feeling able to offer something yourself. It need not mean marriage, children or a large friendship group. Nor should a plan assume family members can provide unlimited care.

For a reader in Ireland, the setting might be a library, a local club, a community group, a faith community or an online group that feels welcoming. The practical reflection is which connections you would like to keep making room for. Compound’s article on [the Ploughing, community and wellbeing](/happiness/ploughing-championships-history-community-mental-health/) explores one familiar setting; [Part 4, Happiness](/happiness/happiness-for-a-100-year-life/), widens the lens.

### Purpose beyond a job title

If paid work changed tomorrow, what would you want to keep from it? Perhaps the challenge, the routine, the company or the feeling of being useful. Naming those things can help you imagine a later chapter with some of the same satisfactions in a different form.

Purpose need not be a grand mission or a second career. It might be learning an instrument, making something, helping a neighbour or keeping a modest commitment. It can also change: an activity that once fitted may become too demanding, while something quieter becomes meaningful.

Treat this as an invitation to explore, not another measure of productivity. Rest, enjoyment and receiving care belong in a good life too. Having interests and relationships does not guarantee protection from depression or other mental health conditions, and struggling is not evidence that someone failed to find enough purpose.

## Explore a hundred years, without a timetable {#timeline}

The five chapters below offer possible priorities, not deadlines. You may recognise a question in a different age group from your own. Learning, connection and adapting a home can matter at many ages; there is no required sequence of marriage, children, home ownership or retirement.

[live100:timeline]

Use the chapters as prompts rather than an audit. An older reader does not need a warning that the opportunity to begin has passed. A younger reader does not need another demand to optimise every decision. A useful question at either age is what would make the coming year more workable and more enjoyable.

## The place you live is part of the plan {#place}

Imagine two people who both want to meet a friend for coffee. One can use a nearby bus and an accessible café. The other depends on a car they can no longer drive. The intention is the same; the available options are not.

Housing, transport, care and employment arrangements shape what is practical. WHO guidance recognises that supportive physical and social environments can help people participate even when their own capacity changes. [WHO (8)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/ageing-and-health). Personal effort is only one part of that picture.

For your own reflection, consider the home as well as the home’s surroundings. Could you reach essential services? Where would company come from? What support might you need, and who would you feel comfortable asking? These are questions to revisit, not demands to move house or make irreversible decisions now.

## Make room for uncertainty, and for today {#begin}

No lifestyle guarantees a hundredth birthday. Genetics, illness, accidents, environment and chance can alter a life, including the life of someone who has taken great care of themselves. Earlier death or disability is not a failure of planning or effort. WHO describes ageing as varied and shaped by both personal and environmental factors. [WHO (8)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/ageing-and-health).

A longer horizon is useful when it broadens your choices. It becomes less useful if it turns the present into a waiting room for an imagined future. Spending time with someone now, enjoying a manageable activity or making everyday finances a little clearer may be worthwhile whether you live another five decades or a much shorter time.

Start with this reflection: **What would I like an ordinary day at 80 to look like, and what might help make that possible?** Think about where you would be, who you might see, what you would enjoy and the help you would want available. If you are already beyond 80, choose an ordinary future day that feels relevant to you.

Then choose one manageable step for financial resilience, one for physical capability and one for connection or purpose. Those could be understanding a regular expense, seeking help with an activity that has become difficult, or arranging time with someone whose company you enjoy. They are examples, not prescriptions.
