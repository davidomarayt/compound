## From grooming to “hardmaxxing”

### The ordinary part is not new

“Looksmaxxing” means trying to maximise physical attractiveness.

At the mild end, that can mean a haircut that suits your face, treating acne, improving dental health, exercising, sleeping properly, wearing clothes that fit and maintaining a healthy body composition.

Men have always done versions of this.

### What is new is the scoring system

Contemporary looksmaxxing turns appearance into a technical project.

Jaw projection, eye shape, facial thirds, canthal tilt, cheekbones, hairline, skin, height and body composition become variables. Communities rank people and attach labels to where they sit in a hierarchy of attractiveness.

The language often separates **“softmaxxing”** — grooming, fitness and style — from **“hardmaxxing”**, which can extend to cosmetic procedures, drugs and extreme attempts to alter the body.

### The extreme end is not just “more optimisation”

Medical specialists have begun warning explicitly about the trend.

Johns Hopkins has described a spectrum running from benign grooming advice to restrictive dieting, anabolic steroids, unregulated peptides and “bone smashing” — intentionally striking the face in an attempt to alter bone structure. The American Academy of Pediatrics has separately identified looksmaxxing as an emerging adolescent-health concern.

That distinction matters.

Treating sunscreen and deliberately injuring your face as points on the same continuum can make the dangerous end look like a logical next step rather than a categorical change in risk.

## Genetics matters — but not in the way social media often claims

### The evidence rejects both extremes

One extreme says almost anything can be changed with the right protocol.

The other says appearance is almost entirely fixed by genetics.

The research supports neither.

Facial morphology is **substantially heritable**. In a 3D twin study of 1,380 female twins, statistically significant heritability estimates ranged from **38.8% to 78.5%** for facial-form components and **30.5% to 84.8%** for scaled facial-shape components.

A separate study of 1,580 twin pairs and siblings estimated facial-attractiveness heritability at roughly **50% to 70%**, with facial masculinity-femininity around **40% to 50%**.

### Heritability is not destiny

A heritability estimate is a **population statistic**.

It does not mean “70% of your face is genetic”. It also does not mean the remainder can be freely engineered. It describes how variation within a studied population is associated with genetic differences under those conditions.

The practical point is simpler: some visible traits are highly modifiable, some partly modifiable, and others are constrained by adult skeletal structure and development.

<div class="looks-layers">
<div><span>MORE MODIFIABLE</span><h3>Presentation</h3><p>Haircut, facial hair, clothing, grooming, photography and styling.</p></div>
<div><span>PARTLY MODIFIABLE</span><h3>Soft tissue & condition</h3><p>Body composition, muscularity, skin condition, dental appearance and some hair-loss outcomes.</p></div>
<div><span>MORE CONSTRAINED</span><h3>Underlying structure</h3><p>Adult skeletal proportions, jaw dimensions, eye spacing, facial height and many inherited morphological traits.</p></div>
</div>

That is where useful advice and pseudoscience separate.

Improving the layers you can realistically influence is very different from being told that enough discipline will reconstruct an adult skull.

## Body fat really can change how men are perceived

### One 2025 study found a peak — not “lower is always better”

A 2025 study in <em>Personality and Individual Differences</em> used 15 DXA-derived male body images and asked 283 participants across China, Lithuania and the UK to rank them for attractiveness.

The relationship with body-fat percentage was **curved, not linear**.

The estimated peak was **13.0% in the UK sample, 13.2% in China and 14.2% in Lithuania**.

Shoulder-to-waist ratio also mattered, with estimated peaks around 1.56–1.58. In the authors’ modelling, adiposity explained more variation when the two measures were considered together.

### An earlier experiment landed in the same broad region

In a 2016 experiment, participants manipulated male body images to maximise either attractiveness or apparent health.

Those figures do **not** justify telling an individual man to reach 13%, 14% or 16% body fat.

Measurement methods differ. Preference studies are not health guidelines. Individuals vary in fat distribution, muscularity, age, ethnicity and appearance.

The useful conclusion is narrower: **the available evidence does not say attractiveness rises indefinitely as body fat approaches zero.**

<strong>Do not turn a preference study into a target.</strong>

## Why losing excess fat can make a jawline look sharper

### Facial adiposity is visible

A review of the literature found that people could estimate body mass from facial cues with a pooled correlation of **r = 0.71**.

Researchers identified facial width-to-height ratio, perimeter-to-area ratio, **cheek-to-jaw-width ratio** and the lower-face outline among the cues associated with body mass.

That helps explain why a face can look substantially different after fat loss.

Experimental work summarised in the same review found that participants transformed male faces toward facial-adiposity levels corresponding to BMIs of **22.46** in one study and **23.79** in another when asked to optimise attractiveness.

Those are not “ideal BMI” prescriptions. They are results from image-transformation experiments.

### The same body-fat percentage can produce different faces

Two men can reach the same body-fat percentage and have very different jawlines.

Fat distribution differs. So does the bone beneath it.

A study involving 922 participants found that jawbone prominence contributed meaningfully to judgments of male masculinity and attractiveness.

That makes jaw definition partly a **soft-tissue question** and partly a **skeletal question**.

Losing excess fat can change the first. It cannot guarantee the second.

## Can an adult jaw be “trained” into another shape?

### Mewing: big claims, thin evidence

“Mewing” — maintaining a particular tongue posture — is promoted online as a way to reshape the jaw or midface.

A recent systematic review of conservative mechanical facial-rejuvenation techniques found a **paucity of evidence** that mewing produces significant aesthetic or functional changes.

### Jaw trainers: evidence is also limited

A published report following two users of a commercial jaw exerciser for about three months found no noticeable improvement in jaw appearance.

The authors’ literature review also found limited evidence that these devices reduce a double chin, tighten skin or meaningfully improve jaw contour.

That does not prove every possible facial exercise has zero effect. Small muscular changes and functional rehabilitation are separate questions.

It does mean that claims of substantial adult skeletal remodelling deserve a much higher evidential bar than a before-and-after photograph.

### “Bone smashing” is not an aesthetic technique

Deliberately injuring facial bones in the hope that healing will make them more attractive is not evidence-based.

Medical organisations warning about looksmaxxing have highlighted fracture, deformity and other injury risks.

## When self-improvement becomes self-surveillance

### Looksmaxxing is not the same thing as body dysmorphic disorder

Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) involves persistent preoccupation with perceived defects or flaws that are not observable, or appear slight, to other people.

It commonly includes repetitive behaviours such as mirror checking, comparing, reassurance seeking, camouflaging or repeatedly examining a feature.

A major recent clinical review estimates BDD prevalence at around **2% in community adult samples in high-income countries** and notes that roughly **two-thirds of cases begin before age 18**.

Most people who care about their appearance do not have BDD.

But some looksmaxxing mechanics overlap with behaviours clinicians watch for: repeated comparison, numerical scoring, zooming in on perceived defects, reassurance seeking and escalating interventions after temporary relief.

### Social comparison is not psychologically neutral

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis found that greater online social comparison was associated with more body-image concerns and eating-disorder symptoms, and with lower positive body image.

For boys and men, muscularity-focused content is especially relevant.

A 2025 study of **1,553 boys and men** in Canada and the United States found that more frequent exposure to muscular bodies, muscle-building supplements and muscle-building drugs on social media was associated with probable muscle dysmorphia, even after accounting for total time on social media.

The study was observational. It cannot prove the content caused the symptoms; people already preoccupied with muscularity may also seek out more of it.

But it undermines the idea that an appearance-focused feed is neutral simply because it is labelled “self-improvement”.

## The male ideal has become lean, muscular — and measurable

The cultural ideal for men is no longer simply “big”.

Research increasingly describes a **lean-muscular ideal**: low enough body fat for visible definition, combined with enough muscle to signal size and strength.

That combination can create competing pressures. Adding muscle takes time, training and adequate energy intake. Staying extremely lean can make the pursuit more restrictive.

### The comparison group has changed

Social media does not show the median face or body.

It can select from millions of people for unusually favourable genetics, years of training, professional lighting, cosmetic work, filters, editing and carefully controlled photographs.

The algorithm shows what holds attention.

Looksmaxxing then gives that comparison a technical vocabulary. A vague feeling — “I do not look as good as him” — becomes “my gonial angle is wrong”, “my canthal tilt is negative”, or “my midface is too long”.

Technical language can describe real anatomy. It can also make subjective judgments feel like diagnoses.

## So what actually has the highest return?

The evidence points toward a much less exotic hierarchy than the internet often suggests.

A man with higher facial adiposity may see a major change in apparent jaw definition after losing excess fat.

A lean man with a relatively narrow or retrusive mandible may see very little jaw change from becoming leaner still.

The first issue is largely soft tissue. The second is largely structural.

That distinction matters because the wrong diagnosis can create an endless loop: when extra fat loss does not create the expected jaw, the person assumes he needs to become even leaner.

The evidence does not support that conclusion.

<h3>Optimise what is realistically modifiable. Do not confuse a limit with a failure.</h3>
<p>Body composition and presentation can change a great deal. Adult skeletal anatomy changes far less. Genetics influences both the starting point and the range of visible outcomes. None of those facts turns attractiveness into an objective score.</p>

## The paradox of the optimisation economy

Looksmaxxing is compelling because it contains enough truth to make weaker claims sound plausible.

Body fat can change the face. Muscle can change the body. Skin can improve. Hair matters. Teeth matter. Style matters. Some medical and cosmetic treatments work.

Genetics also matters — sometimes substantially.

Put those facts together and a sensible message is simple: **improve the things that are healthy and realistically modifiable, then recognise that human faces vary.**

But that message has a commercial problem.

It ends.

A system built around ratings, analysis, coaching, products and repeated optimisation works better when the customer remains slightly dissatisfied.

That does not mean every creator or service intends to create insecurity. It does mean the incentives deserve scrutiny.

<div class="looks-closing">
<p>Michelangelo completed <em>David</em> more than five centuries ago. The sculpture became an enduring image of an idealised male form.</p>
<p>The internet has added something Michelangelo did not: <strong>a scorecard that can refresh every day.</strong></p>
<p>And if the score is always capable of moving, optimisation has no finish line.</p>
