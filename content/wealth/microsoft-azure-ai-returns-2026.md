Microsoft combines recurring software relationships with a capital-intensive cloud business. That combination can be powerful: established customer relationships can support new services, while cloud infrastructure can expand what those customers buy. But recurring revenue does not make every new investment equally profitable.

The central question for a long-term shareholder is whether Microsoft can keep growing cash earnings per share while funding the infrastructure its products require. Use the [Microsoft company page](/stocks/msft/) for updated reported results and the interactive share-price chart.

## How does Microsoft make money?

Microsoft's products span Microsoft 365, Azure, business applications, Windows, gaming, LinkedIn and other services. Its 2025 annual report describes Microsoft 365 growth in terms of users and revenue per user, while Azure provides cloud infrastructure and platform services. These product descriptions are more durable than reporting labels: always check the latest filing for segment definitions and restated comparisons. [Source: Microsoft annual report](https://www.microsoft.com/investor/reports/ar25/index.html).

| Business economics | What supports growth | What investors should test |
|---|---|---|
| Productivity subscriptions | Users, product adoption and spending per user | Renewal strength and the cost of delivering extra features |
| Cloud infrastructure | Customer workloads and consumption | Utilisation, pricing and capital required |
| Business applications | Customer adoption and broader usage | Whether expansion creates durable incremental profit |
| Consumer and other products | Devices, engagement and transactions | Cyclicality and the contribution to group cash flow |

## Why switching costs matter—and where they stop

A business with software embedded in daily work may be reluctant to migrate systems, retrain staff and change security processes. This is an analytical reason existing enterprise relationships can be valuable. It is not evidence that customers will accept any price or buy every add-on.

Separate customer retention from product expansion. An organisation can renew its core subscription while declining an additional AI service. The stronger investment case requires evidence that customers pay for additional value, not just that an installed base exists.

For each new product, ask who makes the purchase decision, what measurable benefit the customer receives and whether that benefit persists at renewal. Trial usage and paid, repeatable demand are different stages.

## Azure: growth must earn a return on infrastructure

Cloud growth can require servers, networking, buildings and power commitments before the resulting revenue arrives. Compare demand with capacity utilisation and the cash committed to serving it. A backlog or customer commitment can be useful evidence, but it is not equivalent to cash already collected or profit already earned.

Read the definition of capital expenditure used in each disclosure. Cash purchases of property and equipment, assets obtained through finance leases and management's broader investment measures can differ. Comparing unlike definitions can manufacture an apparent improvement or deterioration.

Also use like-for-like reporting periods. If Microsoft changes its segment structure or the scope of a product metric, use the company's recast history where available. Do not splice an old definition and a new definition into a growth series without explanation.

## AI: distinguish monetisation from defensive spending

There are several possible outcomes. AI features might support higher revenue per customer, increase cloud consumption, or improve internal productivity. They might also become expected features whose costs rise faster than the extra revenue they generate.

The analytical test is incremental profit after the cost of providing the service and maintaining the infrastructure. An impressive number of users is not enough. Ask whether new revenue is genuinely additional, whether it replaces an existing product and whether the economics improve as usage expands.

## Free cash flow and depreciation

A common starting point for free cash flow is operating cash flow minus cash capital expenditure. Review lease payments and other investment commitments as well. That calculation is an analytical measure, not a substitute for the complete cash-flow statement.

Depreciation spreads equipment costs across accounting periods. Cash may leave before the expense fully reaches earnings. If assumed equipment lives increase, reported earnings can improve without an equivalent immediate increase in cash. Conversely, a heavy investment year can depress free cash flow even if the projects eventually earn attractive returns.

The task is to evaluate the return over the investment cycle, while avoiding an assumption that all current spending is temporary growth expenditure.

## Valuation: the price paid still matters

Here is a **hypothetical illustration**, not a Microsoft forecast. A company earns $10 per share and trades at $300, or 30 times earnings. Five years later, earnings have grown 10% annually to about $16.11. At a 20-times multiple the share price would be about $322: only roughly 1.4% annual price growth before dividends, taxes and fees.

The example shows why strong business growth can coexist with modest shareholder returns. Test both future cash earnings and the valuation investors might assign to them. Keep assumptions about buybacks and share-based compensation consistent with the diluted share count.

## What would strengthen or weaken the investment case?

The case strengthens if customers repeatedly pay more for valuable services while incremental cash returns remain attractive. It weakens if spending accelerates but utilisation, margins or renewal economics fail to follow. Competition and regulatory constraints can alter the benefits of bundling and distribution, so treat those advantages as hypotheses to monitor rather than permanent entitlements.

## Microsoft investor checklist

- Track software customer growth separately from spending per customer.
- Compare cloud growth on consistent metric definitions.
- Reconcile cash capex, leases and management's investment commentary.
- Look for paid AI adoption and renewal evidence.
- Review cash generation after investment and changes in diluted shares.
- Stress-test returns at a lower valuation multiple.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Microsoft purely an AI investment?

No. Its established software, cloud and other businesses matter. AI should be assessed within the whole company's economics rather than as a separate story that makes existing costs irrelevant.

### Is higher capital expenditure automatically bad?

No. The outcome depends on utilisation, pricing, asset life and incremental returns. Spending alone proves neither future success nor failure.

### Is Azure growth the same as Microsoft profit growth?

No. A product's revenue growth cannot be substituted for consolidated profit growth. Costs, product mix, investment and the rest of the group all matter.
