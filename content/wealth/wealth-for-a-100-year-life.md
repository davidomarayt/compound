The useful question is what your money needs to make possible.

A warm home. Food you enjoy. A way to visit people. The option to reduce work if your health changes. Help with tasks you can no longer manage alone. These are less dramatic than a target pension pot, but they give a financial plan its meaning.

In [the opening article](/live-to-100/), we treated a hundred-year life as a scenario rather than a prediction. This second part asks what that scenario changes about money. The answer is not that everyone needs the same enormous sum. It is that a plan may need to support several different versions of your life, with room for uncertainty between them.

That matters whether you are beginning to save, restarting after a difficult period or already drawing a pension. The first step is to understand what is available and what it must cover. General guidance cannot determine which pension, investment or retirement date suits an individual household.

## Begin with the life, then the budget {#budget}

Start with current spending in euros you recognise. Separate regular essentials, spending you could adjust and occasional costs that are easy to overlook. A boiler replacement does not happen every month, but it still belongs somewhere in the plan. So do dental care, travel to appointments and the cost of keeping in touch with people.

Then imagine several chapters. The first years after full-time work might involve more travel or caring for relatives. A later chapter might involve less travel but more help at home. These are possibilities, not a universal pattern of retirement spending. Someone with a disability may already be paying for support; a renter may need a continuing housing budget that a mortgage-free homeowner does not.

Work in household terms while keeping each person's position visible. If you share costs with a partner, ask how the budget would look if one income stopped. Do not assume that bills fall by half when a household becomes smaller. If you live alone, make that the starting point rather than treating it as an exception to somebody else's model.

## Put the different pensions on separate lines {#pensions}

A retirement date, a State Pension start date and access to a workplace or personal pension are different decisions. Write down the rules for each actual arrangement you have, including older pots from previous jobs. Ask providers for current statements and an explanation of when benefits can begin, what charges apply and what happens on death.

For the **State Pension**, current Irish rules generally start at 66, subject to eligibility. The contributory pension depends on your social insurance record and is not means tested. People born in or after 1958 can choose a contributory pension start date between 66 and 70. The non-contributory pension has a means test and separate conditions. MyWelfare recommends checking your contribution record and considering Pension Caring Supports where relevant. [Department of Social Protection (1)](https://services.mywelfare.ie/en/topics/pensions-and-older-people/state-pension/).

Knowing the rules is more useful than assuming you will receive a maximum payment. A period abroad, time caring or a missing employment record can make a contribution statement worth examining well before retirement. Ask the Department to clarify an uncertain record. Do not build a decades-long budget around an unconfirmed entitlement.

For eligible **employee pension contributions**, Revenue limits income-tax relief by age and earnings. The percentages are 15% under 30, then 20%, 25%, 30%, 35% and 40% across the age bands 30–39, 40–49, 50–54, 55–59 and 60 or over. The annual earnings ceiling used for this calculation is €115,000. These are relief limits, not recommended savings rates or a promise that everyone can afford to use them. [Revenue (2)](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/tax-relief-limits.aspx). Our [pension tax relief guide](/wealth/pension-tax-relief-ireland-how-to-claim/) explains the claiming process.

**MyFutureFund** adds another arrangement to check. For participants in 2026–2028, the employee and employer each contribute 1.5% of gross pay, with a 0.5% State top-up, subject to the scheme's €80,000 annual earnings threshold for contributions. Scheduled rates rise in later phases. These are amounts going into the fund, not investment returns. [Official contribution booklet (3)](https://assets.gov.ie/static/documents/1b66f3e6/MyFutureFundContributions.pdf). Check your actual enrolment and payroll position before assuming which arrangement applies.

## Keep purchasing power in the picture {#inflation}

A pension statement may show future euros. Your shopping, heating and housing bills will also be in future euros. Comparing a future income with today's spending, without adjusting either, can make a plan look stronger than it is.

These are calculations, **not forecasts**: €1,000 × (1 + annual rate)<sup>30</sup>, rounded to the nearest euro. Actual prices vary over time and between households. The point is to ask whether a projection is expressed in today's purchasing power or future cash amounts, and whether income is assumed to increase too.

The [compound interest calculator](/compound-interest-calculator/) can help you explore assumptions. Try less favourable inputs as well as attractive ones. A smooth line is a feature of the calculation; it is not a description of how markets or a household's income will behave.

## A long horizon still needs accessible money {#risk}

Money needed soon has a different job from money intended for much later. Before committing funds, consider what you might need for an interruption to earnings, an urgent repair or a change in care. The CCPC advises thinking about access, the capacity to absorb loss and an emergency reserve, alongside investment goals. Taxes and charges can materially reduce returns. [CCPC (4)](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/saving-and-investments/investments/thinking-of-investing).

During retirement, ask an adviser to show what happens if investments fall while withdrawals continue. It is possible to need cash at an inconvenient time. A projection should make clear how spending would be met and what could change in a difficult period. Ask for the assumptions in writing, including inflation, costs, tax and how long the illustration runs. A single favourable outcome is not enough to understand the decision.

Irish tax treatment also matters. Revenue reduced the individual tax rate on income and gains from specified Irish investment funds and equivalent offshore funds from 41% to **38% from 1 January 2026**. That is not a universal rate for every investment or pension. [Revenue (5)](https://www.revenue.ie/en/tax-professionals/ebrief/2026/no-0162026.aspx). The product, jurisdiction, account and your circumstances matter; a generic international investing article cannot settle those questions.

There is no recommended portfolio or withdrawal percentage in this guide. A useful professional conversation should explain the trade-offs, the total cost of advice and products, and what you would do if the plan stopped fitting. Check the adviser and provider on the Central Bank's register, as the CCPC recommends, and ask whether the particular product is regulated too. [CCPC (4)](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/saving-and-investments/investments/thinking-of-investing).

## Treat housing as a place to live {#housing}

A home's value on paper is only one part of its role. Ask whether it is affordable to heat, maintain and insure, whether its layout works, and whether you can reach shops, healthcare and people you want to see. The right question may be about a handrail, a bus route or a smaller maintenance burden, rather than an investment return.

For homeowners, paying off a mortgage does not remove every housing cost. Before overpaying, compare the benefit with your need for accessible savings and check the lender's conditions. Compound's [mortgage overpayment guide](/wealth/mortgage-overpayments-100-euro-ireland/) explains why the rate, term, permitted overpayments and any charge belong in the calculation.

For renters, an honest later-life budget includes rent. It should also acknowledge the difficulty of finding suitable accommodation and the disruption a move can cause. Long-term planning is not a substitute for secure housing or adequate income. These constraints are real, and they change the options available.

Downsizing might release money, but it is not automatically a ready-made solution. Consider transaction and moving costs, the price of the replacement home, accessibility and the relationships you would be moving away from. A financially tidy move may still be a poor fit for the life you value. Discuss possibilities early enough to compare them without treating a move as inevitable.

## Make care a conversation before it becomes a crisis {#care}

Care is both a financial question and a question about preferences. Who would you want involved in decisions? What help would make home life workable? What can relatives realistically offer alongside jobs, their own health and other responsibilities? A loved one's willingness to help should not become an invisible, unlimited line in the budget.

Ireland's **Fair Deal** scheme supports approved long-term nursing home care after care-needs and financial assessments. It does not cover all care or all extras: short-term care and additional charges for some services fall outside it. Funding is not backdated to cover a period before approval. [HSE (6)](https://www2.hse.ie/services/schemes-allowances/fair-deal-scheme/about/).

The financial assessment considers income and assets, with different rules for single applicants and couples. The treatment of the home has specific provisions, including a three-year cap; this should not be read as a cap on every contribution or on all care costs. Use the HSE's assessment guidance and seek case-specific advice before transferring property or making assumptions about an inheritance. [HSE (7)](https://www2.hse.ie/services/schemes-allowances/fair-deal-scheme/financial-assessment/).

The **HSE Home Support Service** is separate. It is mainly for people aged 65 or over who need help to remain at, or return, home, with some younger people eligible. The service is free, does not require a medical card and does not assess income. Support depends on needs; extra support beyond what the HSE offers may have to be paid for privately. [HSE (8)](https://www2.hse.ie/services/home-support-service/home-support-service-for-older-people/). Ask what is available in your circumstances rather than assuming a particular number of hours.

## Leave room for work to change {#work}

Some people would like to continue earning in a different way. Others need to stop, or cannot find work that accommodates their health or responsibilities. A long-life plan should allow for both possibilities.

If a change in hours is feasible, sketch the whole effect: take-home income, pension contributions, commuting, caring and the use of the time released. Ask an employer about actual options and your pension provider about the consequences. A gradual transition may suit one person; a clear ending may suit another.

Learning can also belong in a financial plan without a guaranteed payoff. Before paying for retraining, examine the total cost, the time required and evidence of the work it could lead to. A longer possible working life is not a reason to accept an expensive promise uncritically.

And notice what work provides beyond money. The routine and company may need a new home when paid work ends. [Part 4, Happiness](/happiness/happiness-for-a-100-year-life/), explores that transition; it deserves attention alongside the income calculation.

## Make the plan understandable to someone else {#administration}

A plan that only one person can understand is difficult to help with. Keep an organised record of accounts, pensions, insurance, regular bills and the relevant professional contacts. Store it securely and make clear to a trusted person how it could be found when needed. This does not require sharing passwords or giving someone authority they do not legally have.

The Decision Support Service explains two distinct arrangements: an **enduring power of attorney** can cover specified property, financial and personal welfare decisions if you lose capacity to make them; an **advance healthcare directive** records wishes about treatment. They have different purposes and formal requirements. [DSS (9)](https://www.decisionsupportservice.ie/advance-planning). Explore them while you can set out your own preferences.

A will addresses what happens after death and is a separate matter. Family rights, debts and the way assets are held can affect an estate, so use appropriate legal advice rather than a note of wishes alone. [CCPC (10)](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/planning-for-the-future/wills-and-inhertitance). Revisit arrangements when family or financial circumstances change.

## One manageable review {#begin}

Choose a first task that answers a real uncertainty. That might be obtaining a contribution statement, locating an old pension, listing regular expenses or asking about a care assessment. Completion matters more than constructing an impressive spreadsheet.

At a fuller review, ask: Which income is confirmed? Which costs are missing? What depends on optimistic assumptions? What happens if work stops earlier, a partner dies or support is needed? What would I be willing to change, and what would I most want to protect?

If the immediate problem is debt or making essential payments, start there. **MABS offers free, independent and confidential money advice and budgeting support.** [MABS (11)](https://www.mabs.ie/about/). A long horizon should not make today's pressure feel like a personal failure.

The purpose of this work is to give your future self more understandable choices. Those choices connect directly with [health and capability in Part 3](/health/health-for-a-100-year-life/): where you live, what support is available and whether everyday life leaves room to look after yourself.
