<p><em>News analysis · 26 September 2026. Budget 2027 is due on 6 October, so the 2027 tax bands are not yet final.</em></p>

A single PAYE worker in Ireland currently starts paying the **40% rate of Income Tax on earnings above €44,000**.

That threshold is low enough that a worker can enter Ireland's higher income-tax band while earning **less than the average wage**.

That is the basis for renewed claims that Irish workers hit the "top tax rate" earlier than many European peers.

The claim captures something real — Ireland's income-tax system becomes steep quite quickly as earnings rise — but it can also be misleading if it is read as saying Ireland has the highest overall tax burden in Europe.

It does not.

The more accurate picture is:

> **Ireland taxes low and lower-middle earnings relatively lightly, then increases the marginal burden sharply as income rises.**

The European Commission describes Ireland as having a **low overall labour-tax burden but a highly progressive labour-tax system**.

Those two facts can exist at the same time.

## When does the 40% tax rate start in Ireland?

For 2026, Revenue's standard Income Tax bands are:

| Taxpayer | Income taxed at 20% | Income above this taxed at 40% |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Single person | €44,000 | Above €44,000 |
| Single parent qualifying for the SPCCC | €48,000 | Above €48,000 |
| Married/civil partnership, one income | €53,000 | Above €53,000 |
| Married/civil partnership, two incomes | Up to €88,000 combined, subject to the transferable-band rules | Balance |

For a single worker, that means the first **€44,000** of taxable employment income sits in the 20% band and the balance is taxed at 40%.

Crucially, crossing €44,000 does **not** mean the worker suddenly pays 40% Income Tax on their entire salary.

Only the slice above the band is taxed at 40%.

If a single worker earns €45,000, for example:

- €44,000 falls in the 20% band;
- €1,000 falls in the 40% band;
- tax credits are then deducted from the Income Tax bill.

## Why does €44,000 feel like such a low threshold?

Because it is below the level of average full-time earnings.

KPMG noted after Budget 2026 that Ireland's entry point to the 40% band remained **below the average wage** and argued that wage growth without matching increases to tax bands would push more workers into the higher rate.

That process is sometimes called **fiscal drag** or **bracket creep**.

If wages rise by 4% but the tax band does not move, a larger share of a worker's salary can end up in the higher band even if their purchasing power has barely improved.

Budget 2026 left the €44,000 Income Tax threshold unchanged.

## How early does Ireland reach its top income-tax rate compared with Europe?

Cross-country comparisons are difficult because European tax systems are structured very differently.

Some countries have:

- several income-tax bands;
- local or municipal income taxes;
- large employee social-insurance contributions;
- flat-rate income taxes;
- tax-free allowances rather than Irish-style credits;
- different treatment for married couples and children.

A useful historical comparison from Tax Foundation Europe measured the income at which the top income-tax rate applied as a multiple of each country's average wage.

In that dataset, Ireland's top-rate threshold was about **0.71 times the average wage**.

That was lower than many European progressive-tax systems, including Italy, Finland, Greece, the Netherlands, Portugal and Germany.

But it was not literally the earliest in Europe. Flat-tax systems can apply their single rate from the first euro of taxable income, while countries such as Belgium also reached their top statutory rate at a relatively low multiple of average earnings.

So the strongest version of the headline — that Ireland simply has the earliest top tax rate in Europe — is too broad.

The more defensible conclusion is:

> **Ireland moves from its standard Income Tax rate to its higher rate unusually early relative to average earnings compared with many Western European peers.**

## The bigger issue: 40% Income Tax is not the full marginal tax rate

Income Tax is only one deduction from an Irish payslip.

At a salary just above €44,000, a typical Class A PAYE worker can face on the next euro earned:

- **40% Income Tax**
- **3% USC**
- **4.2% employee PRSI** for most of 2026

That produces a combined marginal deduction of roughly **47.2%** before considering pension relief or unusual personal circumstances.

From **1 October 2026**, the main employee PRSI rate rises to **4.35%**, taking that combined marginal rate to about **47.35%** in this income range.

Above the €70,044 USC threshold, the standard USC rate rises from 3% to 8%.

At that point, the headline marginal deduction for a standard Class A worker can exceed **52%**:

**40% Income Tax + 8% USC + 4.35% PRSI = 52.35%**

That does not mean the worker loses 52.35% of their entire salary.

It means that, once those thresholds are crossed, roughly that proportion can be deducted from an **additional euro of earnings**.

## What happens to an extra €1,000 of salary?

Take a single PAYE worker already above the €44,000 Income Tax threshold but below the 8% USC threshold.

Ignoring pension deductions and other reliefs, an additional €1,000 of gross salary can attract approximately:

| Deduction | Approximate amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Income Tax at 40% | €400 |
| USC at 3% | €30 |
| PRSI at 4.2% | €42 |
| **Total deductions** | **€472** |
| **Approximate amount retained** | **€528** |

After the October 2026 PRSI increase, the retained amount would be slightly lower.

This is why the €44,000 threshold matters economically even though the 40% rate only applies to the income above it.

For a promotion, overtime, extra shifts or a pay rise, the important number is often the **marginal rate** — how much of the next euro you actually keep.

## But Ireland's overall tax burden on workers is not unusually high

This is the important counterpoint.

The European Commission's 2026 Country Report says Ireland's **labour-tax wedge was well below the EU average at all earnings levels** in 2025.

The tax wedge measures the gap between what it costs an employer to employ someone and the worker's take-home pay, including Income Tax and social contributions.

Ireland's system is therefore unusual in its shape:

- the tax burden on low earners is relatively light;
- the burden rises steeply as income increases;
- the difference between low- and high-earner taxation is among the largest in the EU.

The Commission explicitly describes this as evidence of Ireland's **strongly progressive tax system**.

So two seemingly contradictory claims can both be correct:

**1. Irish workers can enter the higher Income Tax band comparatively early.**

**2. Ireland's overall labour-tax burden can still be below the EU average.**

They are measuring different things.

## Why tax credits matter

Ireland also uses tax credits extensively.

A standard single PAYE employee in 2026 can generally receive:

- **€2,000 Single Person Tax Credit**
- **€2,000 Employee Tax Credit**

That €4,000 is deducted directly from the Income Tax bill.

This is one reason looking only at the 40% band can overstate the tax burden for someone earning around the threshold.

Tax credits reduce the worker's **average tax rate**, even though they do not stop the next euro above the band from being taxed at the higher marginal rate.

## Could Budget 2027 move the threshold?

Yes.

The Government has been considering an increase in the standard-rate band as part of Budget 2027.

Recent reporting has suggested a possible move from **€44,000 to at least €46,000** for a single worker.

That has not yet been formally confirmed.

If the band increased by exactly €2,000, the arithmetic for someone earning at least €46,000 is straightforward:

- €2,000 that would otherwise have been taxed at 40% would instead be taxed at 20%;
- the difference is 20 percentage points;
- maximum Income Tax saving: **€400 a year** for an individual who fully benefits from the band increase.

A two-income jointly assessed couple could potentially benefit by more, depending on both incomes and how their rate bands are allocated.

The final Budget 2027 tax package will be announced on **6 October 2026**.

## What would moving the band to €46,000 change?

A €46,000 threshold would delay the point at which a single worker starts paying 40% Income Tax.

But it would not change the 40% rate itself.

Nor would it automatically change USC or PRSI.

That distinction matters.

There are several different ways a government can reduce tax on employment:

- widen the 20% Income Tax band;
- increase tax credits;
- cut an Income Tax rate;
- change USC bands or rates;
- change PRSI rates;
- introduce targeted reliefs.

Each produces a different distribution of gains across incomes.

For example, widening the standard-rate band primarily helps workers who already earn enough to reach the higher band. Increasing a tax credit can reach a broader section of Income Tax payers.

## The clearest way to think about Ireland's tax system

The Irish system is not simply "high tax" or "low tax".

It is **highly progressive**.

Workers at lower earnings levels face a relatively low tax wedge by European standards.

But once incomes move into the middle and upper parts of the earnings distribution, the marginal rate increases quickly.

That is why the €44,000 threshold has become such an important Budget 2027 issue.

It is not because someone earning €44,001 suddenly pays 40% of their salary in Income Tax.

It is because **the next euro of earnings begins to face a much steeper combination of Income Tax, USC and PRSI at an income level that is no longer particularly unusual in the Irish labour market**.

## Bottom line

For a single PAYE worker in 2026:

- the 20% Income Tax band runs to **€44,000**;
- income above that enters the **40% band**;
- combined marginal deductions can be around **47%** in the next income range;
- the overall Irish labour-tax burden remains **below the EU average**, according to the European Commission;
- Ireland's system is therefore better described as **low-tax at lower earnings and steeply progressive as incomes rise**.

Budget 2027 may move the threshold to around **€46,000**, but the final decision will not be known until 6 October.
