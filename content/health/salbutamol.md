**Salbutamol is a fast-acting reliever inhaler that widens the airways.** It can ease wheezing, coughing and breathlessness caused by narrowing of the airways in asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). In Ireland it is supplied on prescription. Ventolin and Salamol are examples of brand names. [HSE salbutamol guide](https://www2.hse.ie/medicines/salbutamol-inhaler/)

> **Call 112 or 999 immediately if you are struggling to breathe or your asthma symptoms are worsening despite your reliever.** Follow your asthma action plan and the [HSE’s emergency guidance](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/asthma/asthma-attacks/).

## The Science Behind the Drug

Salbutamol activates **beta-2 adrenergic receptors** on the smooth muscle that wraps around the airways. The signal tells that muscle to relax. As the muscle relaxes, the opening inside the airway widens and air can move more easily. This is called **bronchodilation**. The HPRA’s Ventolin Evohaler prescribing information describes an onset within five minutes and an effect lasting roughly four to six hours in reversible airway obstruction. [HPRA prescribing information, section 5.1](https://assets.hpra.ie/products/Human/24543/Licence_PA1077-049-010_03032026105901.pdf)

### Beta-2 receptors, a fast airway response and inhaler technique

Salbutamol activates beta-2 receptors in airway smooth muscle. That raises cyclic AMP and reduces the force with which the muscle contracts. The airway can open relatively quickly if the medicine reaches it. Tremor and a fast heartbeat are examples of effects that can occur beyond the lung. [HSE source](https://www2.hse.ie/medicines/salbutamol-inhaler/)

## What is the difference between a reliever and a preventer?

A salbutamol reliever opens the airways quickly when symptoms occur. A steroid preventer, also called a **controller**, helps lower airway inflammation over time. Some people use a combination inhaler for both maintenance and relief under a plan known as MART. Check the name and instructions on your inhaler and use the schedule in your own asthma action plan. [HSE asthma treatment guidance](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/asthma/treating-asthma/)

## What if you need it more often?

If your reliever is becoming a regular part of the day or your asthma is waking you at night, ask your GP or pharmacist to review your symptoms and inhaler technique. The HSE specifically advises checking in if you need salbutamol **more than four times in 24 hours** or wake with asthma symptoms **once a week or more**. For an acute attack, follow your action plan and the emergency guidance above. [HSE salbutamol guide](https://www2.hse.ie/medicines/salbutamol-inhaler/)

## What side effects can salbutamol cause?

Shaky hands, headache or a faster heartbeat can occur. Ask a pharmacist or GP if symptoms are persistent or worrying. Mention any other medicines you take, especially a beta blocker such as propranolol, so your prescriber can check the combination. ## What the evidence measures

Relief of tightness is one outcome. Frequency of symptoms, urgent visits and attacks measures a different aspect of control. Regular reliance on a reliever can be a reason to review the broader plan and inhaler technique.

## Bring the inhaler to a technique check

| What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Device | demonstrate how you take a normal puff |
| Record | share symptom frequency and when you needed a reliever |


## Can an inhaler problem look like a medicine problem?

A depleted device, technique problems or a mismatch between device and breathing pattern can affect delivery. Demonstrating actual use helps the clinician decide what needs to change.
