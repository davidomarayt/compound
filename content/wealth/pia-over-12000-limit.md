**If you pay more than €12,000 into a PIA in one tax year, the extra money would be taxed at 1% for every month it stays in the account.** That works out at roughly 12% a year on the excess, so it is the most expensive money in the account. A change the Dáil agreed on 25 November also makes the provider take the excess out within 30 days of the end of the month. In practice, a mistake should cost about one month's charge plus any selling costs.

PIA is the common name. The Finance (No. 2) Bill 2026 calls it an *Individual Investment Account* (IIA).

**Status, checked 27 November 2026:** these are proposed rules. The Dáil passed the Bill on 25 November. The Seanad stages and signing into law are still to come, and the Seanad could still change it. No provider is taking applications. The 1 July 2027 opening target depends on enactment and on providers being ready. Follow the [PIA launch tracker](/pia/#pia-launch-tracker) for each confirmed step.

## The limit counts what goes in, not what stays in

Section 747H sets the **contribution limit** at **€12,000 for each year of assessment** (tax year). Section 747L(2)(d) counts contributions **"without regard to any amounts withdrawn"**. That has two consequences:

- Taking money out does not free up room. If you pay in €12,000, withdraw €4,000 and later put €4,000 back in the same year, you have contributed €16,000.
- The limit is about payments, not value. Growth on your investments never counts towards it.

Amendment 18, agreed on 24 November, widened the meaning of **contribution**. It now covers money paid in "for the purposes of" buying eligible investments **or** "the operation of the account". So if you pay in extra money to cover a provider's fee, that payment counts too.

## How the 1% monthly charge works

Section 747R covers what it calls an **excess contribution year**. The steps are:

1. At the end of each month, the provider looks at what you have contributed so far that tax year.
2. The first month in which that total goes over €12,000 is the **excess month**.
3. For that month, and every later month while any excess is still in the account, you are charged **1% of the outstanding excess amount** on the last day of the month (747R(4)).
4. This charge applies **even if your account is worth less than €50,000** (747R(3)(b)). It is separate from the normal 1% yearly charge on value above €50,000.
5. Money you withdraw, including money the provider removes, reduces the outstanding excess. The reduction counts from the last day of the month the withdrawal is made (747R(6) and the new 747R(12)).

The charge is handled like the ordinary investment account tax. The provider collects it, and it can sell investments to pay it (747S).

## The new 30-day removal rule

Before 25 November, the committee text set the monthly charge. It also told providers to take reasonable steps to keep accounts within the rules (747L(3)), but it set no deadline for taking an excess out. **Amendment 45**, agreed at Report Stage, adds a new section 747R(9) to (12). In plain terms:

- If there is an outstanding excess on the last day of a month, the provider must remove it **"without undue delay and in any event not later than 30 days"** after that date.
- This applies **"whether or not that amount arose from a contribution accepted by that provider"**. That covers an excess that came across with an account transferred from another provider.
- The provider can sell investments to raise the money. It pays you the amount less **fees, costs or charges "reasonably and necessarily incurred"** in selling.
- It must then write to you with the amount removed, the date, and any costs it deducted.
- The rule is paused while an account is being transferred between providers. It does not apply if the account has already been closed.

Providers also have a strong reason to stop excess payments at the door. Under 747R(8), a provider that accepts an over-limit contribution **without reasonable excuse** faces a **€3,000 penalty** for that account and year.

## Worked examples

These are hypothetical. They use a full tax year, such as 2028, to avoid any question about how the rules apply in the 2027 launch year. They assume the Dáil-passed text becomes law unchanged.

### Example 1: a standing order that runs one month too long

Niamh pays €1,000 a month from January and a €2,000 bonus in March. By the end of October she has paid in **€12,000**. She forgets to cancel the standing order, and it pays another €1,000 on 5 November. The provider lets it through, and she cancels the standing order straight after.

| Date | Contributed so far this year | Outstanding excess | Charge for the month |
|---|---|---|---|
| 31 October | €12,000 | €0 | €0 |
| 30 November | €13,000 | €1,000 | 1% × €1,000 = **€10** |
| Provider removes €1,000 on 18 December | — | — | — |
| 31 December | €13,000 | €1,000 − €1,000 = €0 | **€0** |

The cost is **€10 of tax**, plus any dealing costs if the provider had to sell to raise the €1,000. The removal still counts as a withdrawal. It does not give her any new room for the rest of the year.

### Example 2: why the removal rule matters

Imagine the same €1,000 excess had simply stayed in the account. The charge would be €10 every month: **€120 over a year**, or 12% of the excess. Compare that with the ordinary charge on a €60,000 account, which is 1% × (€60,000 − €50,000) = **€100 a year**. The Dáil amendment means this running cost should now be limited to the month or two before the provider acts.

### Example 3: paying an extra €60 for fees

Conor has already paid in his full €12,000. His provider's €60 yearly fee falls due, and he pays in **€60** to cover it rather than letting the provider sell some investments. Under the new definition, that €60 is a contribution, so he is €60 over the limit. The charge for that month is 1% × €60 = **€0.60**, and the provider must then take the €60 back out. The sum is small. The lesson is that, under the new definition, any money you pay in uses the same €12,000.

Our reading, not confirmed by any provider: a fee taken from cash or investments already in the account is not a new payment in, so it should not use your allowance. Check each provider's terms when they are published.

## Practical steps once accounts open

- **Track money paid in, not the balance.** Your statement balance includes growth. The limit only counts payments.
- **Check your standing orders in the autumn.** €1,000 a month uses the full €12,000 by December. Any one-off top-up during the year means a later standing order will go over.
- **Count re-deposits.** Money you put back after a withdrawal counts again.
- **Moving provider mid-year?** Your contributions so far, and any outstanding excess, move with the account (747X(2)). Do not restart your count with the new provider.
- **Closing and reopening is not a reset.** If you close an account, you cannot open another one in the same tax year (747L(4)).

## What could still change

The Seanad has not yet debated the Bill. The Minister has signalled a further change there on information sharing between Revenue and providers when an account is opened. Regulations under section 747AA will set the form of the provider's removal notice. Until the Act is signed and commenced, treat every figure here as proposed.
