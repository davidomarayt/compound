<p><em>News analysis · 29 September 2026. The €16 Living Wage discussed below is a voluntary cost-of-living benchmark, not Ireland’s statutory minimum wage. Government confirmed the €14.94 minimum wage for 2027 in Budget 2027; the 2026 statutory rate remains €14.15 until the change takes effect.</em></p>

Ireland’s Living Wage Technical Group says the hourly rate needed for a single full-time worker to afford a minimum acceptable standard of living should rise to:

**€16 an hour.**

That is up from **€15.40** for 2025/26, an increase of 60 cent or about **3.9%**.

But the most important point is what the €16 figure **is not**.

It is **not a new legal minimum wage**.

Ireland’s statutory National Minimum Wage remains **€14.15 an hour in 2026**.

Separately, Budget 2027 confirmed the adult National Minimum Wage will rise by **79 cent to €14.94 an hour from January 2027**, following the Low Pay Commission’s recommendation.

There are three useful numbers to distinguish:

- **€14.15** — the legal National Minimum Wage in 2026;
- **€14.94** — the Government-announced adult minimum wage for January 2027;
- **€16.00** — the Living Wage Technical Group’s new cost-of-living benchmark.

For workers, the difference between them is not trivial.

On a 39-hour week, moving from €14.15 to €16 would amount to roughly **€3,752 more gross pay over a 52-week year**.

These are gross figures before Income Tax, USC, PRSI and any other deductions.

The Department of Enterprise gives the current €14.15 minimum wage as equivalent to **€28,696.20 a year** on this 39-hour, 52-week basis.

## How much more is €16 than the current minimum wage?

The gap between €14.15 and €16 is:

**€1.85 an hour.**

At 39 hours per week, that becomes:

- **€72.15 more gross pay per week**;
- about **€312.65 more gross pay per month** on an annualised basis;
- about **€3,751.80 more gross pay per year**.

The €16 Living Wage benchmark is therefore about **13.1% above** the current statutory minimum wage.

The comparison with the announced January 2027 rate of €14.94 is narrower.

At €16 rather than €14.94, a 39-hour worker would receive:

- **€41.34 more gross pay per week**;
- about **€179.14 more per month**;
- about **€2,149.68 more per year**.

That is before tax.

## Why has the Living Wage risen to €16?

The Living Wage Technical Group says minimum living costs increased by an average of **3.1%** over the past year for the single-adult household on which the rate is modelled.

The main pressures were:

- rising **rents**;
- higher **household energy costs**;
- and, for the first time, the effect of **My Future Fund auto-enrolment pension contributions** on the calculation.

The Living Wage is intended to answer a different question from the National Minimum Wage.

The National Minimum Wage is a legally enforceable wage floor set through Government policy.

The Living Wage asks:

> **What hourly gross wage would a full-time single adult without dependants need to afford a socially acceptable minimum standard of living?**

The Living Wage Technical Group’s model is based on Minimum Essential Standard of Living research and includes the interaction between living costs, Income Tax, USC, PRSI and relevant supports.

Its technical methodology assumes a full-time worker is employed for **39 hours per week**.

## Is €16 now the legal minimum wage?

No.

An employer generally does **not** become legally obliged to pay €16 simply because the Living Wage Technical Group has published the rate.

The statutory National Minimum Wage remains **€14.15 an hour** for an adult worker in 2026, subject to the separate age-based rates and sector-specific rules that can apply.

Budget 2027 confirmed the Low Pay Commission’s recommended **5.6% increase** for 2027. The adult statutory minimum wage is due to rise **from €14.15 to €14.94 an hour from 1 January 2027**. The €16 Living Wage remains a separate, voluntary benchmark.

## What will the announced €14.94 minimum wage add to pay?

The announced increase is **79 cent an hour**, effective January 2027.

For someone working 39 hours each week, that is:

**€30.81 extra gross pay per week.**

Across 52 weeks:

**€1,602.12 extra gross pay per year.**

The annual gross figure would rise from about:

**€28,696 → €30,298.**

Again, the increase in take-home pay will be smaller than the gross increase because Income Tax, USC and PRSI can change as earnings rise.

That distinction becomes especially important when household costs rise rapidly.

A statutory minimum wage can increase while still remaining below the amount the Living Wage methodology estimates is needed to meet its defined standard of living.

## How the gap narrows in January 2027

At today’s €14.15 minimum wage, the gap to €16 is:

**€1.85 per hour.**

When the January 2027 adult minimum wage rises to €14.94, the gap falls to:

**€1.06 per hour.**

That would leave the statutory minimum around **6.6% below €16**.

The confirmed increase will narrow the gap materially, but will not eliminate it.

## What about employers?

Higher wages increase payroll costs, particularly for businesses with large numbers of employees at or close to the minimum wage.

Ahead of the Budget, the **Small Firms Association** asked the Government to reject the proposed minimum-wage increase. The Government subsequently announced the increase.

It argues that smaller businesses are already facing higher labour-related costs and has called for measures including a temporary PRSI rebate.

The SFA notes that the National Minimum Wage has risen from **€9.15 in 2016 to €14.15 in 2026**.

That is an important part of the policy debate because the cost to an employer is not limited to the employee’s headline hourly wage.

Employer PRSI and other employment costs can also rise with payroll.

The debate illustrates why wage policy involves both worker living standards and business costs; the January 2027 rate has now been announced.

## Do minimum-wage increases push up prices?

New ESRI research published on 22 September provides useful Irish evidence.

Researchers examined eight minimum-wage increases between 2016 and 2025.

For **seven of the eight increases**, the study found **no statistically detectable impact on prices**.

The exception was the unusually large January 2024 increase, when the minimum wage rose **12.4%**, from €11.30 to €12.70.

That increase was associated with a **2.5% rise in prices across a small number of minimum-wage-intensive categories**, including:

- hairdressing;
- takeaway coffee;
- takeaway meals;
- restaurant meals.

Those categories represented about **11% of total consumer spending**, according to the ESRI.

The researchers therefore concluded that the overall inflation impact of small-to-moderate minimum-wage increases in Ireland is likely to be limited.

That does not mean a wage increase is costless for every individual business.

It means the historical Irish data did not show a broad inflation effect for most of the increases studied.

## What about jobs?

A separate ESRI study published in March 2026 looked at whether past minimum-wage increases made minimum-wage employees more likely to become unemployed or inactive.

It found **no evidence that recent minimum-wage increases increased the likelihood of minimum-wage workers losing their jobs** in the six-month period after an increase.

The researchers also cautioned that the period examined was one of strong economic growth and low unemployment.

So the result is evidence about what happened during that period, not a guarantee that every future increase will have the same effect under every economic condition.

## One complication: hourly pay is only part of low pay

Another recent Irish study makes this story more complicated.

Research reported by RTÉ found that many low-paid workers are not necessarily paid the statutory minimum hourly rate.

Instead, low weekly earnings can result from **too few working hours**.

That distinction matters.

A worker earning €16 an hour for 20 hours a week would have gross weekly pay of:

**€320.**

A worker earning €14.15 for 39 hours earns:

**€551.85.**

So an hourly wage benchmark cannot by itself solve low household income where working hours are irregular or insufficient.

The Living Wage methodology explicitly assumes **regular, consistent full-time employment** for this reason.

## What happens next?

There are now two separate tracks.

### The €16 Living Wage

The Living Wage Technical Group has published €16 as its new benchmark for the year ahead.

Employers that voluntarily use the Living Wage benchmark can take the new figure into account in wage setting.

### The 2027 National Minimum Wage

Budget 2027 announced that the adult National Minimum Wage will increase to **€14.94** from **1 January 2027**. Until that date, the 2026 adult minimum wage remains **€14.15 an hour**.
