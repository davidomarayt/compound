## Hourly wage to annual salary: €15, €20, €25 and €30 an hour

## What is €50,000 a year per hour in Ireland?

At **39 paid hours a week over 52 paid weeks**, €50,000 annual gross salary works out at **€24.65 an hour** (rounded). At 37.5 paid hours a week it is **€25.64/hour**, and at 40 hours a week it is **€24.04/hour**. Those are very different hourly equivalents for the same annual salary because the weekly hours differ.

## How to convert salary to an hourly rate

For a gross annual salary, the basic conversion is:

**hourly rate = annual salary ÷ (paid weeks per year × paid hours per week)**

To go in the other direction:

**annual gross salary = hourly rate × paid hours per week × paid weeks per year**

For example, €52,000 across 52 paid weeks and 40 paid hours a week is €25 gross per hour. Use paid hours and paid weeks: for a normal salaried job with paid annual leave, reducing the denominator to exclude that paid leave would overstate the hourly equivalent.

## What the time-conversion outputs mean

The calculator also shows a **fortnightly gross figure**, the average number of paid hours represented by each working day, and the share of a full 168-hour week represented by paid work. Advanced mode estimates the effective gross equivalent when you routinely work additional unpaid hours. It assumes the entered additional hours occur during each of the entered paid weeks; the result is for personal comparisons only.

These are conversion tools, not measures of workload or quality of life. Two jobs with the same gross hourly equivalent can still be very different once commuting, unpaid overtime, shift patterns, annual leave, employer pension contributions and other benefits are considered.

## How to read the result

The result is a **gross pay conversion**, not take-home pay. The hourly rate depends strongly on how many paid weeks, working days and hours you assume.

## Worked example

A €52,000 salary across 52 weeks is €1,000 gross per week. At five days a week that is €200 per working day, and at 40 hours a week it is €25 gross per hour.

## Irish context

Employees receive more than the cash salary alone: paid annual leave, public-holiday entitlement, employer PRSI, possible pension contributions, sick-pay policies and other benefits can have economic value. A contractor hourly rate therefore should not be compared with an employee hourly equivalent without adjusting for those differences.

## What can change the answer

Unpaid leave, overtime, bonuses, shift premiums and actual hours worked can all change the effective hourly rate. Someone regularly working 50 hours on a 40-hour contract has a lower effective rate than the calculator's 40-hour assumption.

## Common mistakes to avoid

Do not compare a contractor rate directly with this employee gross hourly figure. Do not forget paid leave. And do not interpret the gross hourly equivalent as the amount arriving in your bank account.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the calculator include tax?
No. Results are gross amounts before Irish Income Tax (PAYE), USC, PRSI, and any pension or other deductions. Use the [Take-Home Pay Calculator](/take-home-pay-calculator/) for estimated net pay.

### Can I convert €20 an hour into an annual salary?
Yes. Switch to **Hourly pay → annual salary**, enter €20, then choose your paid weekly hours and annual paid weeks. At 39 hours and 52 weeks this is €40,560 gross per year, or €3,380 gross per month.

### What does €50,000 a year equal per hour?
At 39 paid hours per week and 52 paid weeks, €50,000 is about €24.65 gross per hour. At 40 hours it is about €24.04 gross per hour.

### Can I include unpaid overtime?
In Advanced mode, enter additional unpaid working hours per week to estimate a lower effective gross hourly equivalent. Keep it separate from your contractual hourly rate and do not use it to assess legal wage compliance.

### What weeks should I use?
Use the paid weeks represented by the salary. For a standard year-round salary, 52 is usually the natural starting point.

### Should lunch breaks count as hours?
Use the actual paid-hours basis relevant to the comparison.

### Does it include bonus?
Only if you add bonus into the annual salary amount.

## The hidden value of an employee hour

An employee's economic hourly value is often higher than salary divided by hours because paid time off and employer-funded benefits sit outside the visible hourly figure.

For example, an employee receiving paid annual leave, public holidays and a 5% employer pension contribution can be better compensated than a contractor or casual worker earning the same nominal hourly rate without those benefits.

The reverse can also happen when salaried employees routinely work unpaid overtime. If a €60,000 salary is contracted at 39 hours but the person regularly works 50, the effective hourly rate is materially lower than the contract-based result.

For job comparisons, calculate at least two versions: the **contracted hourly equivalent** and the **real-world hourly equivalent** based on the hours you actually expect to work.

Then add the cash value of benefits separately. This creates a much fairer comparison than using salary alone.

## Paid leave and the 52-week denominator

For a normal salaried role where annual leave is paid, **52 paid weeks** is usually the appropriate denominator for the gross hourly equivalent. Reducing the input to 48 weeks simply because the employee receives four weeks of paid annual leave would spread the same annual salary across too few paid weeks and overstate the hourly figure.

The working-hours input should represent paid working hours in a normal week. Unpaid breaks should generally be excluded. The result is still a contractual gross-pay equivalent rather than a valuation of the entire employment package.

## Turn salary into a fair job-to-job comparison

An hourly equivalent becomes most useful when two roles have different schedules. A €70,000 salary based on 37.5 paid hours a week is not the same time proposition as €70,000 based on 45 regular hours, even before commuting or benefits are considered.

Start with the contractual gross hourly equivalent, then build a second comparison using the hours you realistically expect to work. Keep those numbers separate. The contractual figure answers “what does the employment contract imply?” while the real-world figure answers “what does this role pay for the time it actually consumes?”

### Add total reward without pretending benefits are cash salary

Employer pension contributions, health insurance, bonus, paid professional fees and other benefits can materially change the package. Rather than inflating the salary input, calculate the salary-based hourly figure first and then add the annual value of benefits as a separate total-reward layer.

For example, a role with a lower salary but a meaningful employer pension contribution can be economically closer to a higher-paid role than the headline salary suggests. The reverse can also be true where a higher salary comes with longer hours and little employer-funded benefit.

Use the [Pension Projection Calculator](/pension-projection-calculator/) when you want to model the long-term value of pension contributions instead of treating the employer contribution as if it were immediately spendable cash.

### Commute time is not paid working time, but it is still your time

A normal commute should not be added to contractual working hours when calculating the employer's hourly pay rate. It can, however, be useful in a **personal opportunity-cost comparison** between jobs.

If one role requires ten hours a week of commuting and another is largely remote, calculate both the official hourly equivalent and an optional “time committed” equivalent that includes travel. Label the second number clearly so it is not confused with a legal or contractual hourly rate.

The same principle applies to unpaid preparation, regular out-of-hours calls or predictable overtime: keep the contractual calculation clean, then create a separate real-world scenario.

### Salary versus contracting

Do not compare a PAYE hourly equivalent directly with a contractor's invoice rate. A contractor rate may need to cover unpaid leave, gaps between engagements, insurance, pension funding, accounting, equipment and other business costs. The [Contractor vs Salary Calculator](/contractor-vs-salary-calculator/) is built for that broader comparison.

For two PAYE jobs, this calculator is a strong first pass. For PAYE versus self-employed or company contracting, use the hourly conversion only as one input into the wider economic comparison.

## Method and limitations

For annual salary input, the calculator divides the entered gross salary by paid weeks and paid hours. For hourly input, it multiplies the rate by those hours and weeks, then divides the resulting annual total into monthly, fortnightly, weekly and daily equivalents. An Advanced comparison includes optional unpaid extra weekly working hours in the denominator; it is not a statutory wage-rate assessment. This tool does not calculate tax, overtime entitlement, benefits or employment rights.
