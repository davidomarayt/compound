## From grooming to “hardmaxxing”

“Looksmaxxing” is an internet term for trying to maximise physical attractiveness. At its mildest, that can mean ordinary self-care: a haircut that suits your face, treating acne, improving dental health, exercising, sleeping properly, wearing clothes that fit and maintaining a healthy body composition.

That version is not especially novel. Men have always groomed, trained, dressed and tried to present themselves well.

What makes contemporary looksmaxxing different is the **system built around it**.

Faces are broken into components. Jaw projection, eye shape, facial thirds, canthal tilt, cheekbones, hairline, skin, height and body composition become variables. People are ranked against one another. Communities use numerical scales and labels to describe where someone sits in a hierarchy of attractiveness.

The language also separates “softmaxxing” — lower-risk grooming, fitness and style — from “hardmaxxing”, which can include cosmetic procedures, drugs and extreme attempts to alter the body.

In 2026, paediatric and medical specialists have begun warning explicitly about the trend. Johns Hopkins described a spectrum from benign grooming advice to restrictive dieting, anabolic steroids, unregulated peptides and “bone smashing” — intentionally striking the face in an attempt to alter bone structure. The American Academy of Pediatrics has separately identified looksmaxxing as an emerging adolescent-health concern.

That range matters. Treating sunscreen and hitting your face with a hard object as points on the same optimisation continuum can make the extreme end look like a logical next step rather than a categorical change in risk.

## There is now a business built around the score

The commercial layer is easy to see.

As of October 2026, Looksmaxxing.com sells a **$79.99 facial analysis and plan** that promises an “objective rating”, a breakdown of strong and weak features and a prioritised improvement plan. It also sells one-to-one coaching for **$49.99 an hour**. Its forum reports more than 80,000 registered members.

QOVES, another prominent facial-aesthetics service, markets a **$150 analysis** that measures more than 160 facial markers and returns a personalised report. Its own material says the service cannot move bone structure or promise a result — an important caveat in a market where measurement can easily be mistaken for control.

Current looksmaxxing apps add another layer. One iPhone app lists paid tiers including **$19.99 monthly premium** and a **$69.99 lifetime option**.

None of that means every service is fraudulent. A good stylist, dermatologist, dentist, barber, trainer or regulated clinician can offer genuinely useful advice.

The more interesting question is the **business incentive created by permanent optimisation**.

## The uncomfortable truth: genetics really does matter

The strongest version of the looksmaxxing sales pitch says almost anything can be improved if a man finds the correct protocol.

The strongest version of the fatalistic “blackpill” response says appearance is almost entirely fixed by genetics.

The research supports neither extreme.

Facial morphology is **substantially heritable**. In a large 3D twin study using 1,380 female twins, statistically significant heritability estimates for facial-form components ranged from **38.8% to 78.5%**, while scaled facial-shape components ranged from **30.5% to 84.8%**. Facial size, several nose dimensions, lip prominence and inter-ocular distance were among the most strongly heritable traits.

A separate genetically informative study of 1,580 twin pairs and siblings estimated the heritability of facial attractiveness at roughly **50% to 70%**, with facial masculinity-femininity around **40% to 50%**.

Those numbers require careful interpretation. Heritability is a population statistic. It does **not** mean that “70% of your face is genetic”, nor that the remaining 30% can be freely engineered. It describes how much of the variation observed in a particular population and environment is statistically associated with genetic differences.

The practical conclusion is less dramatic but more useful: **some visible traits are highly modifiable, some are partly modifiable, and others are constrained by skeletal development and genetics.**

This is where good advice and pseudoscience separate. Improving the layers you can realistically influence is very different from being told that enough discipline will reconstruct an adult skull.

## Body fat does change how men are perceived

One part of the looksmaxxing conversation is supported by surprisingly direct evidence: **body composition can materially change perceived attractiveness**.

A 2025 study in <em>Personality and Individual Differences</em> used 15 DXA-derived male body images and asked 283 participants across China, Lithuania and the UK to rank them for attractiveness.

The relationship between attractiveness and body-fat percentage was not simply “lower is better”. It was **peaked**.

The estimated peak was **13.2% body fat in the Chinese sample, 14.2% in Lithuania and 13.0% in the UK**. Peak BMI values were 23.4, 23.0 and 26.6 respectively. Shoulder-to-waist ratio also mattered, with peaks around 1.56–1.58, but adiposity explained more variation when both were modelled together.

An earlier 2016 experiment reached a similar broad region using a different method. Participants manipulated male body images to maximise either attractiveness or apparent health. The average body fat selected was **15.89% for attractiveness** and **14.75% for perceived health**.

Those studies do not justify telling an individual man to reach 13%, 14% or 16% body fat. Measurement methods differ, preference studies are not health guidelines, and individuals vary in fat distribution, muscularity, age, ethnicity and appearance.

But they do challenge an important idea in extreme looksmaxxing: **the available evidence does not say attractiveness increases indefinitely as body fat approaches zero.**

## Why losing excess fat can make a jawline look sharper

The body-fat finding also helps explain a common observation: a man's face can look substantially different after losing excess body fat.

Facial adiposity is visible.

A review of the literature found that people could estimate body mass from facial cues with a pooled correlation of **r = 0.71**. Researchers have identified changes in facial width-to-height ratio, perimeter-to-area ratio, **cheek-to-jaw-width ratio** and the outline of the lower face as cues associated with body mass.

In experimental work summarised by that review, participants transformed male faces toward facial-adiposity levels corresponding to BMIs of **22.46** in one study and **23.79** in another when asked to optimise attractiveness.

That does not mean a BMI of 22.46 produces an ideal face. BMI is an imperfect proxy and these were image-transformation studies. But it does show that soft tissue around the cheeks and lower face can influence how facial structure is perceived.

This also explains why two men at the same body-fat percentage can have very different jawlines. Fat distribution differs. So does the bone beneath it.

A study involving 922 participants found that jawbone prominence contributed meaningfully to judgments of male masculinity and attractiveness. That is relevant to looksmaxxing because a visible jawline is partly a **soft-tissue question** and partly a **skeletal question**.

Losing excess fat can change the first. It cannot guarantee the second.

## There is no evidence that an adult jaw can simply be “trained” into another shape

This is where looksmaxxing often moves from plausible optimisation into weakly supported claims.

“Mewing” — maintaining a particular tongue posture — is frequently promoted online as a way to reshape the jaw or midface. A recent systematic review of conservative mechanical facial-rejuvenation techniques found a **paucity of evidence** that mewing produces significant aesthetic or functional changes.

Jaw-training products face a similar problem. A published report following two users of a commercial jaw exerciser for about three months found no noticeable improvement in jaw appearance, while the authors' literature review found limited evidence that such products reduce a double chin, tighten skin or meaningfully enhance jaw contour.

That does not prove every possible facial exercise has zero effect. Small muscular changes and functional rehabilitation are different questions.

It does mean that claims of substantial adult skeletal remodelling deserve a much higher evidential bar than an influencer before-and-after photograph.

“Bone smashing” is in another category entirely. Deliberately injuring facial bones in the hope that healing will make them more attractive is not an evidence-based aesthetic technique. Medical organisations warning about looksmaxxing have highlighted fracture, deformity and other injury risks.

## When self-improvement becomes self-surveillance

The most important risk in looksmaxxing may not be any single bad technique.

It is the possibility that appearance becomes a project that can never be finished.

Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) involves persistent preoccupation with perceived defects or flaws in appearance that are not observable, or appear slight, to other people. It commonly involves repetitive behaviours such as mirror checking, comparing, reassurance seeking, camouflaging or repeatedly examining a feature.

A major recent clinical review estimates BDD prevalence at around **2% in community adult samples in high-income countries** and notes that roughly **two-thirds of cases begin before age 18**.

Looksmaxxing is not the same thing as BDD. Most people who care about their appearance do not have a psychiatric disorder.

But some of its mechanics overlap uncomfortably with behaviours clinicians watch for: repeated comparison, numerical scoring, zooming in on perceived defects, searching for reassurance and escalating interventions after temporary relief.

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis found that greater online social comparison was associated with greater body-image concerns and eating-disorder symptoms, and with lower positive body image.

For boys and men, the muscularity side is particularly relevant. A 2025 study of **1,553 boys and men** in Canada and the United States found that more frequent exposure to muscular bodies, muscle-building supplements and muscle-building drugs on social media was associated with probable muscle dysmorphia, even after accounting for total time spent on social media.

That study was observational. It cannot prove the content caused the symptoms. People already preoccupied with muscularity may also seek out more of that content.

But it undermines the idea that appearance-focused feeds are psychologically neutral simply because the content is labelled “self-improvement”.

## The male ideal has changed shape — and become measurable

The cultural ideal for men is no longer simply “big”.

Research increasingly describes a **lean-muscular ideal**: low enough body fat for visible definition, combined with enough muscle to signal size and strength.

That combination is difficult because the two goals can pull behaviour in opposite directions. Adding substantial muscle usually requires time, training and adequate energy intake; staying extremely lean can make the pursuit more restrictive.

Social media adds another pressure: the reference group is no longer the men someone sees in ordinary life.

It can be a feed selected from millions of people for unusually favourable genetics, professional lighting, low body fat, years of training, cosmetic work, filters, editing and highly controlled photographs.

The algorithm does not show the median face. It shows the content most likely to hold attention.

Looksmaxxing then gives that comparison a technical vocabulary. A vague feeling — “I do not look as good as him” — can become “my gonial angle is wrong”, “my canthal tilt is negative”, or “my midface is too long”.

Technical language can be useful when it describes real anatomy. It can also make subjective judgments feel like diagnoses.

## So what actually has the highest return?

The evidence points toward a much less exotic hierarchy than the internet often suggests.

For a person with modifiable issues, ordinary interventions can matter: a healthy body composition, resistance training, an appropriate haircut, grooming, treatment for acne or hair loss where indicated, dental care, sleep and clothing that fits.

Those changes can improve appearance without requiring a theory that every face has an objective score.

Beyond that, the returns become much more individual.

A man with higher facial adiposity may see a major change in apparent jaw definition after losing excess fat. A lean man with a relatively narrow or retrusive mandible may see very little jaw change from becoming leaner still. The first problem was largely soft tissue. The second is largely structural.

That distinction is critical because the wrong diagnosis can create an endless loop: when additional fat loss does not create the expected jaw, the person assumes he needs to become even leaner.

The scientific evidence does not support that conclusion.

<span>THE EVIDENCE-BASED VERSION OF “LOOKSMAXXING”</span>
<h3>Optimise what is realistically modifiable. Do not confuse a limit with a failure.</h3>
<p>Body composition and presentation can change a great deal. Adult skeletal anatomy changes far less. Genetics influences both the starting point and the range of visible outcomes. None of those facts turns attractiveness into an objective score.</p>

## The paradox of the optimisation economy

Looksmaxxing is compelling because it contains enough truth to make the weaker claims sound plausible.

Body fat can change the face. Muscle can change the body. Skin can improve. Hair matters. Teeth matter. Style matters. Some medical and cosmetic treatments work.

Genetics also matters — sometimes substantially.

Put those facts together and a sensible message would be: **improve the things that are healthy and realistically modifiable, then accept that human faces vary.**

But that message has a commercial problem. It ends.

A system built around ratings, analysis, coaching, products and repeated optimisation works better when the customer remains slightly dissatisfied.

That does not mean every creator or service intends to create insecurity. It does mean the incentives deserve scrutiny.

Michelangelo completed <em>David</em> more than five centuries ago. The sculpture became an enduring image of an idealised male form.

The internet has added something Michelangelo did not: a scorecard that can refresh every day.

And if the score is always capable of moving, optimisation has no finish line.
