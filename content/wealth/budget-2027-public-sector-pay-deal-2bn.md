<p><em>News analysis · 27 September 2026. The reported €2 billion figure is a Budget provision under discussion, not an agreed pay award. The final public-service pay agreement — if one is reached — will depend on negotiations between Government and unions.</em></p>

Reports this weekend say the Government is preparing to provide **up to €2 billion in Budget 2027 for a new public-sector pay agreement**, as industrial action across parts of the public service moves closer.

That is a large number.

But it is important to separate three different things:

1. **money set aside in the Budget;**
2. **the pay terms eventually negotiated;**
3. **the cost that actually falls in 2027.**

They are not the same.

At the time of writing, **no successor public-service pay agreement has been concluded**.

The previous agreement expired on **30 June 2026**, and the dispute over how new negotiations should begin has already led several unions to ballot for industrial action.

## What is being reported?

The Irish Independent reported on 27 September that an **extra €2 billion** is being set aside in the Budget for public-sector pay increases.

Earlier in the week, Minister for Public Expenditure Jack Chambers told the Oireachtas Committee on Budgetary Oversight that **provision would be made in Budget 2027 for a new public pay deal**, even though no agreement had yet been reached.

So the direction is now clear:

**Budget 2027 is expected to contain substantial room for public-service pay.**

What is not clear is the final negotiated percentage increase, how long a new agreement would run, or when each increase would take effect.

## Why is this happening now?

The last public-service pay agreement ran from **1 January 2024 to 30 June 2026**.

According to RTÉ, it provided for pay increases of **up to 10.25% over two-and-a-half years**.

When that agreement expired, no replacement was ready.

Public-service unions have argued that workers need a new pay deal reflecting cost-of-living pressures. Government has said it wants a deal that is fair but also affordable and sustainable.

The disagreement over the starting point for negotiations has become an industrial-relations dispute in its own right.

## What industrial action is planned?

Several unions have already backed industrial action.

Fórsa members voted strongly in favour of action, while the INMO and other unions have also secured mandates.

The Public Services Committee of the Irish Congress of Trade Unions has planned **one-day strike action for 14 October**, with a further stoppage on **21 October if necessary**, according to RTÉ.

Before then, work-to-rule action is due to begin in a number of sectors from **30 September**.

That includes sections of local government and health services.

The timing matters because Budget 2027 is scheduled for **6 October** — between the start of work-to-rule action and the first planned national strike day.

## What does €2 billion actually buy in public-sector pay?

One useful benchmark comes from Department of Public Expenditure figures reported by The Irish Times in June.

At that point, the estimated cost of a **1% increase in public-service pay was about €370 million a year**.

Using that figure purely as a rough full-year illustration:

| Illustrative general pay increase | Approximate full-year cost |
| --- | ---: |
| 1% | €370m |
| 2% | €740m |
| 3% | €1.11bn |
| 4% | €1.48bn |
| 5% | €1.85bn |
| 5.4% | ~€2.0bn |

This does **not** mean workers are about to receive a 5.4% pay rise.

A real agreement can be structured very differently.

It may include staged increases, flat-rate payments, percentage increases, lower-paid-worker provisions, local bargaining or increases that only take effect partway through the year.

That means a deal whose eventual annualised cost exceeds €2 billion could still cost less than €2 billion in its first Budget year — and vice versa depending on the design.

## Why the timing of a pay rise changes the Budget cost

Imagine a hypothetical 3% increase costing about **€1.11 billion in a full year**.

If the entire increase applied from 1 January, most of that cost would hit the 2027 Budget.

If it started in July, only around half of the first-year cost would fall in 2027.

If increases were staged — for example one part early in the year and another later — the cash cost in 2027 would differ again.

That is why the reported €2 billion allocation should not be reverse-engineered into a single percentage pay rise.

## How does this compare with Budget 2026?

Budget 2026 included **€1.2 billion for public-service pay agreement adjustments**, according to the Department of Public Expenditure.

The newly reported figure of up to €2 billion for 2027 would therefore be materially larger.

But the comparison is not exact.

The €1.2 billion in Budget 2026 largely related to implementing an agreement whose terms were already known.

The 2027 provision is being discussed before the successor agreement has been finalised.

That creates more uncertainty around how much of the envelope will eventually be required.

## Is €2 billion already guaranteed to be spent?

No.

The safest interpretation is that the Government is **making fiscal room for a possible agreement**.

That is prudent from a budgeting perspective because a public-service pay deal creates recurring expenditure.

If the final deal costs less than the amount provided, the full allocation may not be required for pay.

If negotiations produce a more expensive agreement, Government would have to find additional room, phase increases differently or make other spending choices.

The final Budget documents and any subsequent pay agreement will determine the actual numbers.

## Why public-sector pay matters so much to the Budget

Public-service pay is one of the State's largest recurring expenditure commitments.

The Irish Times reported in June that there were around **417,000 full-time public-service employees**, and that the public pay bill represents roughly **one-third of public expenditure**.

That means even relatively small percentage changes have large Exchequer effects.

A 1% increase sounds small at individual level.

Across hundreds of thousands of employees, it becomes a roughly **€370 million annual commitment**.

And unlike a once-off payment, a permanent salary increase generally becomes part of the base for future years.

## What would a pay rise mean after tax?

The headline percentage increase is not the same as the amount that lands in a worker's bank account.

The net gain depends on:

- income level;
- income-tax band;
- tax credits;
- USC;
- PRSI;
- pension contributions;
- and any other payroll deductions.

For example, two workers receiving the same gross 3% pay increase can receive different net gains if one remains in the 20% income-tax band and another has part of the increase taxed at 40%.

## Does the €2bn reduce room for other Budget measures?

Potentially, yes — because permanent public-service pay rises are part of current expenditure.

Budget 2027's Summer Economic Statement set an overall package of **€8.5 billion**, including **€5.9 billion in additional current spending**, **€1.1 billion in additional capital spending** and **€1.5 billion in tax measures**.

Not all of the €5.9 billion is available for new political choices.

Large parts are needed to maintain existing services, meet demographic pressures, fund already-promised measures and absorb higher costs.

A sizeable public-pay provision therefore becomes one of the major demands on the current-spending envelope.

That does not by itself determine which other measures will or will not happen.

It means the final Budget must accommodate the pay agreement alongside health, education, childcare, social protection and other spending priorities.

## What happens next?

There are now three important dates to watch:

**30 September:** work-to-rule action is due to begin in several parts of the public service.

**6 October:** Budget 2027 is due to be delivered.

**14 October:** unions have planned a one-day strike if the dispute has not been resolved, with another strike pencilled in for 21 October if necessary.

The existence of a Budget provision may make a deal easier to finance.

It does not itself resolve the negotiation.

Government and unions still have to agree the structure, timing and size of any new pay settlement.

## The bottom line

The reported **€2 billion** figure is significant because it shows the State is preparing financially for a substantial public-service pay agreement.

But it should not be read as a €2 billion cheque that has already been agreed with unions.

At present:

- the previous agreement has expired;
- industrial action is scheduled;
- Budget 2027 is expected to make room for a new deal;
- and the actual pay increases remain subject to negotiation.

The next useful number will not simply be the Budget allocation.

It will be the **percentage increases, payment dates and duration of the final agreement** — because those are what determine both the cost to the Exchequer and the change in individual workers' pay packets.
