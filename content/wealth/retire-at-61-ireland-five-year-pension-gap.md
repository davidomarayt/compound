Retirement ambitions and retirement finances do not always match. A survey of 1,000 Irish employees conducted by iVOX for SD Worx, reported by RTÉ on 24 November, found that workers wanted to finish before 61 on average but expected to work beyond 65. Some 62% expressed concern about having insufficient resources to stop at their intended age.

The survey captures expectations, not actual future retirement ages. But it raises a useful planning question: what would it cost to stop working at 61 instead of 66?

## The five-year income bridge

Ireland's State Pension (Contributory) is available from age 66, subject to social insurance eligibility. Someone stopping at 61 needs another source of income for approximately five years. This might be savings, an accessible occupational or personal pension, a partner's income or part-time work.

Consider a household that needs the following annual spending after tax, with no other income during those five years.

| Annual spending | Five-year spending requirement |
|---|---:|
| €20,000 | €100,000 |
| €25,000 | €125,000 |
| €30,000 | €150,000 |
| €40,000 | €200,000 |

These calculations assume constant nominal spending, no investment gains or losses and no tax on withdrawals. They are not recommended pension fund sizes. Inflation, taxes, housing and medical expenses, and income after 66 all affect the actual requirement.

If the €30,000 spending assumption increases by 2% annually, the total over five years is approximately €156,121 before allowing for investment returns.

## Part-time work can reduce the bridge

For someone requiring €30,000 a year, an illustrative €12,000 annual after-tax income from part-time work reduces the annual funding gap to €18,000. Over five years that is €90,000 rather than €150,000, assuming constant amounts. This does not account for changes to benefits or pension entitlements.

## Saving for the bridge

Starting from zero, accumulating €90,000 in 15 years with no investment growth requires €500 a month. A €150,000 target over the same period requires about €833 a month. The [savings goal calculator](/savings-goal-calculator/) lets you test your own target, timeframe and return assumption. Investment returns might reduce contributions required, but they are uncertain and losses shortly before retirement could delay a planned exit.

These targets are separate from the resources needed for later life. Early retirement may also mean fewer years of pension contributions and a longer period of withdrawals.

## Check pension access rather than assuming it

The State Pension start date is not the same as the access date for a private or occupational pension. Scheme rules and individual circumstances govern access. See [Compound's pension access guide](/wealth/when-can-i-access-pension-ireland/) and ask the relevant provider for a retirement benefit estimate.

The contributory State Pension amount depends on the social insurance record; [Compound's State Pension guide](/wealth/state-pension-ireland/) explains the current rates and PRSI rules. Request a Contribution Statement through MyWelfare and check how stopping employment early could affect the eventual payment. Eligible people may choose a claim date between 66 and 70 under the applicable rules.

## A practical retirement plan

Calculate annual spending after tax, including housing, transport and irregular bills. Subtract income you reasonably expect before 66. Then repeat the exercise for the period after 66, using an appropriate State Pension estimate rather than assuming the maximum rate.

The [Compound retirement income calculator](/retirement-income-calculator/) can help explore assumptions. Stress-test higher spending, lower returns and the possibility of living much longer than expected.

The survey's most useful lesson is that desired retirement age should be translated into a funding plan well before the final years of employment.
