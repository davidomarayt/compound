A home battery solves a real solar problem.

Your panels often generate most strongly around the middle of the day. Your home may need the most electricity in the morning and evening.

A battery can move some of that electricity from one part of the day to another.

What it cannot do is make the economics automatically better.

If the battery is expensive, your export payment is generous, or you already have a very cheap night tariff, a battery can increase self-consumption while **lengthening the simple payback period**.

The useful question is therefore not:

> Should a solar home have a battery?

It is:

> **What is each stored kilowatt-hour worth in my home compared with exporting it or buying electricity later?**

## The short answer

For many Irish homes:

**Solar PV can make financial sense without a battery.**

A battery becomes more attractive when:

- a large amount of solar would otherwise be exported;
- evening or peak-rate electricity is expensive;
- the battery can be charged cheaply off-peak and discharged at expensive times;
- your household uses substantial electricity after sunset;
- you cannot shift much of your demand into sunny hours;
- or you value the additional flexibility enough to accept a longer financial payback.

An EV does not automatically make a battery necessary.

In fact, a very cheap EV night rate can sometimes make it financially better to **export daytime solar and charge the car at night**.

That sounds counter-intuitive, but the arithmetic is straightforward.

## First: what support is available in 2026?

SEAI's domestic Solar PV Grant remains capped at **€1,800 in 2026**.

The current grant structure is:

- €700 per kWp for the first 2 kWp;
- €200 per additional kWp from 2 to 4 kWp;
- maximum grant: €1,800.

A 4 kWp or larger eligible installation therefore receives the maximum €1,800 solar grant.

The home must have an MPRN, must have been built and occupied before 2021, must not previously have received solar PV funding at that MPRN, and the SEAI grant application must be approved **before works begin**.

[SEAI: Solar electricity grant](https://www.seai.ie/grants/home-energy-grants/individual-grants/solar-electricity-grant).

As of 21 September 2026, SEAI's individual home-energy grant list includes the solar PV grant but does **not list a separate standalone domestic battery-storage grant**.

That means the battery part of a quote should be assessed on its own economics rather than assuming a separate battery grant will reduce the price.

## Ireland gets useful solar generation—but not evenly through the year

Solar panels do not need Mediterranean sunshine to work.

SEAI says a well-located **3 kW** home solar PV system can generate around **2,600 kWh a year**.

Its solar guidance also notes that around **75% of annual solar PV production in Ireland occurs from May to September**.

That seasonal imbalance matters.

A battery moves energy by hours, not by seasons.

It can move midday electricity into the evening. It cannot save a large surplus from July and give it back to you in December.

[SEAI: electricity from solar](https://www.seai.ie/renewable-energy/solar-energy/electricity-from-solar).

## A worked 5 kWp household

To make the trade-off visible, consider an illustrative household.

These are **assumptions**, not current market quotes:

| Assumption | Example |
| --- | ---: |
| Solar system size | 5 kWp |
| Annual solar yield assumption | 850 kWh per kWp |
| Annual solar generation | 4,250 kWh |
| Household electricity use | 4,200 kWh/year |
| Installed solar quote before grant | €10,000 |
| SEAI solar grant | €1,800 |
| Net solar investment | €8,200 |
| Day/import electricity value | 30c/kWh |
| Export payment | 18c/kWh |
| Direct solar self-use without battery | 35% |
| Battery installed cost | €5,000 |
| Battery round-trip efficiency | 90% |
| Share of surplus solar captured by battery | 60% |

The 850 kWh/kWp yield is deliberately an illustrative midpoint. SEAI gives examples around this range, but the real output depends on location, orientation, pitch, shading, inverter losses and weather.

Your installer's design estimate should replace it.

## Scenario 1: solar only

At 35% direct self-use:

**4,250 kWh × 35% = 1,487.5 kWh**

is used directly in the home.

The remaining:

**2,762.5 kWh**

is exported in this simplified example.

### Value of the electricity

Directly used solar:

**1,487.5 × €0.30 = €446.25**

Exported solar:

**2,762.5 × €0.18 = €497.25**

Total annual energy value:

**€943.50**

On an €8,200 net investment:

**€8,200 ÷ €943.50 ≈ 8.7 years**

That is the simple payback under these assumptions.

It ignores financing cost, degradation, future tariff changes, maintenance and the time value of money.

Use the [Solar Payback Calculator](/solar-payback-calculator/) for your own figures.

## Scenario 2: add a battery

Now assume the battery captures 60% of the solar that would otherwise have been exported.

Battery input:

**2,762.5 × 60% = 1,657.5 kWh**

At 90% round-trip efficiency, approximately:

**1,491.8 kWh**

comes back out for later use.

The rest is lost through charging/discharging and conversion losses.

### What is that stored solar actually worth?

Without a battery, 1 kWh of surplus solar could be exported for **18c**.

Put that same 1 kWh into a 90%-efficient battery and only about **0.9 kWh** comes back.

If that later avoids electricity costing 30c/kWh:

**0.9 × 30c = 27c**

So the gross financial gain from storing rather than exporting that 1 kWh is:

**27c - 18c = 9c**

before considering battery degradation or the capital cost of the battery.

That is the key battery equation.

## The battery increases annual value—but can worsen payback

Under the worked assumptions, solar + battery produces annual energy value of roughly:

**€1,093**

versus:

**€944**

for solar alone.

The battery has increased annual value by only about:

**€149 a year**

from solar shifting alone.

But the upfront investment has risen from:

**€8,200**

to:

**€13,200**

because of the €5,000 battery.

### Simple payback

Solar only:

**about 8.7 years**

Solar + battery:

**about 12.1 years**

So in this particular example, the battery improves self-consumption and annual savings but **slows the overall payback**.

That distinction matters.

A technology can save additional electricity without producing the best return on the additional capital.

## The export rate changes everything

Suppose your export rate is 18c/kWh and the battery is 90% efficient.

For each 1 kWh sent into the battery, its later value is:

**0.9 × avoided import price**

To beat exporting at 18c:

**0.9 × import price > 18c**

Therefore:

**import price > 20c/kWh**

At exactly 20c:

**0.9 × 20c = 18c**

The battery has created no gross energy-arbitrage advantage over exporting.

Above 20c, storing begins to win on this narrow comparison.

Below 20c, exporting can be more valuable.

That threshold does **not** include battery wear or battery purchase cost, so the true economic hurdle is higher.

The CRU requires suppliers to compensate eligible microgenerators for metered electricity exported to the grid under the Clean Export Guarantee arrangements. The actual rate is set by the supplier and appears on the bill.

[CRU: microgeneration](https://www.cru.ie/consumer-information/microgeneration/).

## This is why using your own tariff matters

A household on:

- 35c evening electricity;
- 12c night electricity;
- 15c export;

has very different battery economics from a household on:

- 24c flat-rate electricity;
- 20c export.

The physical battery is the same.

The financial value of shifting a kilowatt-hour is not.

That is why Compound's [Solar + EV + Battery Optimiser](/solar-ev-battery-optimiser/) asks separately for import, export and night rates.

## Can a battery make money by charging at night?

A battery does not have to charge only from solar.

SEAI explicitly notes that home batteries can charge from the grid when renewable generation is plentiful and electricity prices are low, then discharge later when electricity is more expensive.

[SEAI: be energy smart](https://www.seai.ie/plan-your-energy-journey/for-your-home/smart-living/be-energy-smart).

Consider an illustrative tariff:

- night electricity: 10c/kWh;
- later avoided day/peak electricity: 30c/kWh;
- battery round-trip efficiency: 90%.

Put **5 kWh** into the battery overnight.

Cost:

**5 × 10c = €0.50**

Useful electricity returned:

**5 × 90% = 4.5 kWh**

Value if it avoids 30c electricity:

**4.5 × 30c = €1.35**

Gross spread:

**€1.35 - €0.50 = €0.85**

for that cycle before battery wear, degradation, tariff restrictions and any other losses.

If that exact opportunity existed every day, the arithmetic would be approximately €310 a year.

That should **not** be treated as a forecast.

Real battery availability changes with solar charging, household demand, capacity limits, seasonal conditions and tariff structures.

But it demonstrates why a battery can have value beyond simply storing solar.

## An EV changes the problem again

SEAI recommends shifting EV charging toward periods of strong solar production where practical and using off-peak charging where daytime solar charging is not convenient.

[SEAI: smart energy technologies](https://www.seai.ie/plan-your-energy-journey/for-your-home/smart-living/smart-energy-technologies).

But the cheapest option depends on your actual tariffs.

Assume an EV:

- travels 15,000 km a year;
- uses 18 kWh/100 km at the battery;
- has 10% charging losses;
- does 80% of charging at home.

Approximate home charging demand:

**about 2,400 kWh/year**

Now suppose you can move 720 kWh of that charging into sunny hours.

### If the alternative was 30c daytime charging

Using 720 kWh of solar directly avoids:

**720 × 30c = €216**

But if that solar could otherwise have been exported at 18c:

**720 × 18c = €129.60**

Net financial improvement:

**€86.40 a year**

### If the alternative was a 10c EV night rate

Solar charging saves:

**720 × 10c = €72**

But gives up:

**€129.60**

of export income.

Net difference:

**minus €57.60**

In that tariff example, exporting the solar and charging the EV at night is financially better.

That does not mean night charging is always best.

It means:

> **Do not value solar EV charging against the wrong electricity rate.**

If your EV would otherwise charge on a cheap night boost tariff, use that rate in the comparison.

## Does an EV make a home battery less necessary?

Sometimes.

An EV is itself a very large flexible electrical load.

If the car is home during sunny hours, it can absorb surplus solar directly without first putting that electricity through a stationary battery.

That avoids one conversion step and the capital cost of using the home battery for that particular purpose.

If the car is away all day and only returns in the evening, the interaction changes.

A home battery may then help move solar production into the evening—but a cheap night EV rate may still be a better way to charge the vehicle itself.

The right arrangement might therefore be:

**solar → household by day**  
**surplus solar → export or home battery**  
**battery → expensive evening household demand**  
**EV → cheap night-rate electricity**

rather than trying to run every electrical load from the battery.

## Four configurations worth comparing

The [Solar + EV + Battery Optimiser](/solar-ev-battery-optimiser/) compares four broad setups:

### Solar only

Lowest capital cost of the four.

Makes most sense where:

- you can use a good share of solar directly;
- export payment is reasonable;
- and you do not need additional storage flexibility.

### Solar + smart EV charging

Useful where the car is at home during solar hours or where you can schedule charging intelligently.

The important comparison is against the **rate the EV would otherwise use**.

### Solar + battery

Can increase self-consumption and reduce expensive imports later in the day.

Its incremental return depends heavily on:

- battery price;
- efficiency;
- export rate;
- avoided import rate;
- and how often enough surplus exists to charge it.

### Solar + EV + battery

Potentially the most flexible setup.

It is also the highest-capital-cost setup.

More technology does not guarantee the shortest payback.

The optimiser therefore compares the configurations rather than assuming the most complex one is best.

## When does a battery look stronger financially?

A battery case becomes stronger when:

### Your evening electricity is expensive

If stored solar replaces high peak-rate electricity, each delivered kWh is worth more.

### Your export tariff is relatively low

The opportunity cost of not exporting falls.

### You have substantial midday surplus

A battery cannot store solar you do not generate.

### You have evening household demand

A battery is more useful when there is a real load available later to consume the stored electricity.

### You can exploit a large night-to-day tariff spread

Grid charging can add another source of value where tariff rules and battery capability allow it.

### The battery price is competitive

This sounds obvious, but battery economics are extremely sensitive to installed cost.

A €3,500 battery and a €7,000 battery storing the same number of useful kWh have very different paybacks.

## When can a battery look weak financially?

The case is weaker when:

- your solar export rate is high;
- your night import rate is extremely low;
- your evening demand is modest;
- you can already shift washing, hot water or EV charging into sunny hours;
- the battery is oversized for your actual surplus;
- or the battery quote is high relative to the extra annual value.

A battery can still be chosen for reasons other than simple payback.

The point is to separate those reasons from a claim that it will necessarily produce the best financial return.

## Battery size matters as much as battery ownership

A battery that is too small may regularly fill and still export substantial surplus.

A battery that is too large may spend much of the year partly empty because there is not enough surplus or later demand to cycle it.

Ask for:

- **usable capacity**, not only nominal capacity;
- round-trip efficiency;
- maximum charge/discharge power;
- warranty duration;
- cycle or throughput warranty conditions;
- inverter compatibility;
- whether grid charging is supported;
- and how the energy-management software decides when to charge and discharge.

The correct battery is a system-design question, not simply “5 kWh versus 10 kWh”.

## Solar sizing matters too

SEAI advises sizing solar with the home's electricity demand in mind and maximising the share used on site where practical.

A very large array may generate more annual electricity but also export a much larger proportion.

That can still be economically sensible if the installed cost per kWp is attractive and export compensation is good.

Again, self-consumption percentage is not the only measure that matters.

A system with a lower self-consumption percentage can still produce more total financial value.

## Why smart-meter data is more valuable than an annual bill

Annual consumption tells you **how much** electricity you use.

Battery economics also require knowing **when** you use it.

SEAI notes that smart meters record usage in half-hour intervals, and ESB Networks data can help households understand demand and export patterns.

[SEAI: smart meters and tariffs](https://www.seai.ie/plan-your-energy-journey/for-your-home/smart-living/smart-meters-and-tariffs).

Before buying a battery, useful questions include:

- How much electricity do you use from 5pm to bedtime?
- How much solar do you export on a typical spring or summer day?
- How often would the battery actually reach full charge?
- How often would it fully discharge?
- What rate are you paying during the hours the battery would replace?
- What rate are you being paid for exports?

Those answers are far more valuable than a generic claim that a battery “raises self-consumption to X%”.

## A 4,200 kWh benchmark is only a starting point

The CRU's typical domestic electricity-consumption benchmark used in supplier annual-cost comparisons is **4,200 kWh a year**.

That is useful for examples.

It is not a prediction for your house.

A home with:

- a heat pump;
- an EV;
- electric showers;
- a tumble dryer;
- home working;
- or electric space heating

can differ materially.

The [Solar Payback Calculator](/solar-payback-calculator/) therefore lets you replace the benchmark with your own annual consumption.

## Three quotes to ask an installer for

Rather than asking only for “solar with battery”, request:

1. **solar only**;
2. **the same solar array + smaller battery**;
3. **the same solar array + larger battery**.

For each quote, ask the installer to show:

- annual generation estimate;
- assumed household self-consumption;
- annual export;
- battery throughput;
- battery losses;
- estimated bill saving;
- export income;
- installed price before and after grant;
- and the tariff assumptions used.

If the payback model does not state the import and export rates, it is difficult to assess.

## The most important number may be the battery's incremental payback

People often calculate:

**total solar + battery cost ÷ total annual savings**

That tells you the combined-system payback.

It does not tell you whether the battery itself improved the economics.

A useful second calculation is:

**additional battery cost ÷ additional annual value created by the battery**

In our worked example:

- battery cost: €5,000;
- additional annual value from solar shifting: about €149.

That gives an incremental simple payback of more than **33 years** for the battery **from solar shifting alone**.

That is why the separate night-tariff use case can matter so much.

If the battery creates value from both solar shifting and grid arbitrage, its economics change.

But those benefits should be calculated separately rather than bundled into a vague “battery saves more” claim.

## The practical conclusion

For many Irish households, the sensible order is:

### First: make solar itself work

Check:

- roof suitability;
- installed price;
- likely annual generation;
- grant eligibility;
- daytime electricity demand;
- and export payment.

### Second: understand your load shape

Use smart-meter data where available.

### Third: compare the battery as an incremental investment

Ask what the battery adds in:

- avoided imports;
- lost exports;
- night-rate arbitrage;
- and flexibility.

### Fourth: account for the EV separately

Do not assume solar charging beats a cheap EV night tariff.
