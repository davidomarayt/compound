Making friends as an adult can feel strangely difficult.

At school, college or in your first jobs, other people are built into the structure of the week. You repeatedly see the same faces without having to organise it.

Then adulthood fragments.

People move.

Couples settle into routines.

Children change schedules.

Work becomes remote.

Friends emigrate.

A commute replaces the time that used to be spent hanging around.

And suddenly friendship has to be **deliberately scheduled**.

That can make a normal social problem feel like a personal failure.

It is not rare.

According to the Central Statistics Office, **17.2% of people aged 25 to 49 in Ireland reported feeling lonely at least sometimes in 2025**, up from 14.6% in 2024. This was the highest loneliness rate among the age groups reported in that particular CSO analysis.

That does not mean all of those people lacked friends.

Loneliness is about the gap between the connection you want and the connection you feel you have.

But the number is a useful reminder: if adult friendship feels harder than it used to, you are dealing with something many other adults are dealing with too.

## Why making friends becomes harder in adulthood

Friendship needs opportunity.

You need to encounter people, encounter them again, have enough shared context to talk, and eventually choose to spend time together outside the original setting.

Childhood and education create those conditions automatically.

Adult life often does not.

That means the solution is rarely to "be more outgoing".

A quieter person with repeated contact can build deeper friendships than a highly sociable person who meets a different group every week.

The more useful target is:

**create repeated, low-pressure contact with people you might actually like.**

## Friendship quality matters more than collecting contacts

A systematic review of 38 studies on adult friendship and wellbeing found that friendship quality and socialising with friends were consistently associated with wellbeing.

The review also highlighted the importance of **effort to maintain friendships**.

That is intuitive but important.

Friendship is not just the moment you meet someone.

It is the follow-up.

The second coffee.

The message after the match.

Showing up again next Tuesday.

Remembering what was happening in someone's life.

A contact becomes a friend through accumulated interactions.

That is useful because it means you do not need to find an instant best friend.

You need to find environments where a relationship has time to compound.

## The best places to meet people have one thing in common

They repeat.

Think of the difference between:

- a one-off concert;
- and a weekly running club.

Both contain other people.

Only one makes repeated interaction likely.

The HSE recommends community activities, clubs, courses, walking groups, volunteering, Men's Sheds and other recurring local activities as ways to build social contact.

The important word is **recurring**.

Good adult-friendship environments tend to have three characteristics:

1. **You see the same people repeatedly.**
2. **There is something to do besides make conversation.**
3. **There is a natural reason to return.**

That is why sports clubs, classes, volunteering, choirs, community groups, local clean-ups, book clubs, gaming groups and professional communities can work better than simply going somewhere crowded.

## Start with your actual interests

Do not join a club because it sounds like the sort of place adults are supposed to make friends.

Join something you can imagine continuing even if you do not immediately meet anyone.

That gives the activity its own value.

If you like football, play football.

If you like lifting, choose a gym or class with some social repetition.

If you like books, find a book club.

If you have always wanted to learn Irish, pottery, photography or guitar, a course creates built-in repeated contact.

The HSE specifically recommends thinking about hobbies you enjoy or things you have wanted to try.

This is not because hobbies magically cure loneliness.

It is because they create **shared context**.

Conversation is easier when there is already something to talk about.

## The hardest step is often moving from acquaintance to friend

Adults can accumulate acquaintances for years.

Someone at work.

A person you see at the gym.

Another parent.

A neighbour.

A teammate.

The relationship stays in its original container unless someone moves it outside.

That usually requires a small amount of social risk.

Instead of waiting for a dramatic invitation, make the next step small.

"Are you getting a coffee after this?"

"Are you watching the match Saturday?"

"I'm going for a walk Sunday morning if you fancy it."

"A few of us should grab a pint after training some week."

The objective is not to make the invitation impressive.

It is to make it easy to say yes.

## Use the two-invitation rule

One awkward feature of adult friendship is that everyone is busy.

A declined invitation can mean:

- they are genuinely unavailable;
- they forgot;
- they have childcare;
- they are tired;
- they are already committed;
- or they are not particularly interested.

You usually cannot tell from one answer.

A practical rule is to invite someone twice on separate occasions.

If they decline both times and never suggest an alternative, stop pushing.

This gives the relationship a fair opportunity without turning friendship into pursuit.

## Existing friendships are often the highest-return place to start

"Make new friends" is not always the best first move.

Sometimes the social network already exists but has become dormant.

Look through the people you genuinely liked over the past few years.

Who did you lose touch with because life became busy rather than because the friendship ended badly?

A simple message can reopen a relationship:

"I realised we haven't caught up in ages. Fancy a coffee next week?"

You do not need a clever reason.

A neglected friendship may be easier to rebuild than a completely new one.

This is especially relevant if your social circle thinned after moving, changing jobs, having children or returning from abroad.

## Friendship needs frequency before it needs intensity

When people feel lonely, there can be pressure to make every interaction meaningful.

That can make socialising harder.

Not every coffee has to become a deep conversation.

Not every new person has to become a close friend.

Regular weak ties can matter too: the person at the coffee shop, a neighbour, the same people at a class.

Over time, some of those interactions deepen.

Let friendship develop at an ordinary pace.

## What if you are shy or socially anxious?

You do not have to become extroverted.

Choose environments where conversation has structure.

A class is easier than a networking event.

A walking group is easier than standing in a room trying to make small talk.

Volunteering gives you a task.

Sport gives you a shared goal.

You can also set a tiny target:

- say hello to one person;
- ask one follow-up question;
- stay ten minutes longer;
- learn one person's name.

If anxiety is strong enough that you repeatedly avoid social situations you want to take part in, it may be worth discussing that separately with a GP or mental health professional.

The goal is not to bully yourself through distress.

## Does joining groups actually reduce loneliness?

The intervention evidence is encouraging but not magical.

A 2024 umbrella review covering 29 systematic reviews of randomised trials found **small-to-moderate beneficial effects overall**.

Social interventions appeared most promising for social isolation, while psychological interventions appeared more promising for loneliness.

But results varied considerably and study quality was mixed.

That distinction makes sense.

If someone has too little contact, creating genuine opportunities for social contact may help.

If someone has regular contact but still feels profoundly disconnected, simply adding more people may not solve the underlying problem.

Our [guide to loneliness in Ireland](/happiness/loneliness-ireland-how-common-what-helps/) goes deeper into that difference.

## A four-week friendship plan

If your social life feels stuck, do not set the goal "make friends".

That is too large and partly outside your control.

Set behavioural goals instead.

### Week 1: choose one recurring environment

Pick one activity you can attend at least weekly.

Commit to four appearances before judging whether it is "working".

### Week 2: become recognisable

Introduce yourself.

Learn two names.

Arrive a little early or stay a little later instead of disappearing immediately.

### Week 3: create one small invitation

Ask one person to extend the interaction slightly: coffee, a walk, food after training, another event.

### Week 4: maintain

Message someone you met.

Return to the group.

Reconnect with one old friend.

The purpose of the month is not to manufacture a best friend.

It is to create **social momentum**.

## Where to find things in Ireland

The HSE points people towards:

- local libraries;
- Education and Training Boards;
- community and local news sites;
- sports and walking groups;
- volunteering;
- Men's Sheds;
- local clubs and courses.

The HSE also highlights **social prescribing**, where a link worker can connect someone with suitable activities and supports in their area.

You can contact a social prescribing service yourself or ask a health professional such as your GP about referral.

This can be particularly useful when the obstacle is not knowing what is available locally.

## Friendship after children

Having children can simultaneously increase the number of people you encounter and decrease the time available for actual friendship.

Parent-and-child groups, sports clubs, school communities and neighbourhood routines can create repeated contact, but it still takes an intentional step to move from "other parent" to friend.

Keep invitations simple and compatible with real life.

A coffee during a child's activity may be more realistic than arranging an elaborate night out.

Friendship does not have to look the way it did at 22.

## Friendship after moving or returning to Ireland

A move resets the repetition that built your old network.

Expect rebuilding to take time.

Join things sooner than feels necessary.

Do not wait until you feel lonely enough that every new interaction carries enormous importance.

If you have returned to Ireland after years abroad, old friendships may also have changed.

People may still care about you while having completely different routines.

Treat returning as a partial rebuild rather than expecting everything to restart exactly where it stopped.

## When loneliness needs more than a busier calendar

The HSE advises seeking support if loneliness becomes overwhelming, contributes to persistent low mood or anxiety, causes you to withdraw further, or leaves you feeling unable to cope.

A GP can help you work out whether loneliness is part of a broader mental health problem and connect you with appropriate support.

The HSE also lists counselling, psychology and mental health services, including options that can be accessed without a GP referral.

## The real goal is not more people

It is enough connection.

For one person, that might mean a large social circle.

For another, it might mean two close friends, a football team and regular family contact.

There is no correct number.

Adult friendship becomes easier to think about when you stop asking:

**"Why don't I have enough friends?"**

and start asking:

**"Where can I create repeated contact with people I might genuinely enjoy knowing?"**

Then you show up again.

That is how most adult friendships begin.
