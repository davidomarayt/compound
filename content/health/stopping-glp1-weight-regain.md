<p class="glp-note">Evidence checked 14 September 2026. This guide concerns adult weight-management treatment. It is not a stopping, tapering or restarting schedule.</p>

Starting a medicine comes with instructions. Stopping can feel much less clearly mapped out.

Perhaps the cost has become difficult to sustain. Perhaps side effects are interfering with everyday life, or pregnancy is being planned. Perhaps treatment has helped and the question is whether it is still needed. Those are different situations, but all deserve a conversation about what happens next.

The research gives one clear reason to have that conversation: **people commonly regain weight after semaglutide or tirzepatide is withdrawn**. It does not establish that everyone regains everything, or that an individual has failed if their weight increases. The useful question is how to keep care in place when the treatment changes.

## What “two-thirds regained” actually means

The widely reported figure comes from the STEP 1 extension. Researchers followed a subset of 327 adults from a trial of semaglutide 2.4 mg for overweight or obesity; participants did not have diabetes. Medication and the structured lifestyle intervention both ended after 68 weeks.

In the semaglutide group, average weight loss was 17.3% at that point. Over the following year, participants regained about two-thirds of the weight previously lost. The reported average net reduction from their original weight was still 5.6% at week 120. Several cardiometabolic improvements moved back towards baseline. [Wilding and colleagues, STEP 1 extension](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35441470/)

Two-thirds refers to **the previous loss**, not two-thirds of someone's body weight. For a simple illustration, regaining two-thirds of a 15 kg loss would mean regaining 10 kg. That arithmetic explains the phrase; it is not a forecast for a person who has lost 15 kg.

This was an exploratory extension in a subset of participants, with lifestyle support withdrawn too. It cannot cleanly separate the effects of ending each part of care. The study was funded by Novo Nordisk. Those limitations belong alongside the headline.

## A stronger test: continue treatment or switch to placebo

In SURMOUNT-4, 670 adults without diabetes were randomised after 36 weeks of tirzepatide: continue 10 mg or 15 mg, as tolerated, or switch to placebo for 52 weeks. Lifestyle counselling continued in both groups.

By week 88, total average weight loss from the original baseline was **25.3% with continued treatment** and **9.9% after switching to placebo**. [Aronne and colleagues, SURMOUNT-4](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2812936)

The reported **14% regain** uses weight at week 36 as its denominator. It cannot simply be subtracted from the initial percentage loss. The chart uses one reference point throughout.

Randomisation strengthens the evidence. However, participants had already reached this stage on treatment; they do not represent every starter. Eli Lilly funded the trial.

## Semaglutide shows the same broad pattern

STEP 4 provides another randomised comparison. After a 20-week semaglutide run-in, 803 adults without diabetes continued semaglutide 2.4 mg or switched to placebo, with lifestyle intervention in both groups.

During the next 48 weeks, the continued-treatment group lost a further 7.9% on average; the placebo group gained 6.9%. Both changes are measured from week 20, not from the original pretreatment weight. [Rubino and colleagues, STEP 4](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2777886)

That supports the finding that continued medication helps maintain weight reduction. It still does not compare every possible approach to stopping. The trial selected people able to reach the treatment stage required for randomisation, and Novo Nordisk funded it.

Taken together, these studies make regain something to plan for. They do not justify treating it as inevitable in exactly the same amount for everyone.

## Does this mean treatment has to last forever?

These trials cannot answer a lifetime question. They followed people for defined periods, using particular doses and study conditions. None establishes the best duration for every patient, or a personalised taper that reliably prevents regain.

The World Health Organization's December 2025 guidance conditionally supports long-term GLP-1 treatment for adults with obesity, excluding pregnant women. It explicitly notes gaps around long-term use, maintenance and discontinuation, alongside costs and access. Structured dietary and physical-activity support may also be offered, but the certainty of evidence for that additional recommendation is low. [WHO guidance](https://www.who.int/news/item/01-12-2025-who-issues-global-guideline-on-the-use-of-glp-1-medicines-in-treating-obesity)

Long-term treatment should therefore mean ongoing review, not an assumption that the prescription can never change. Equally, having reached a target weight does not by itself answer whether the treatment's benefits are still needed.

An honest review can hold both ideas: a medicine may be doing useful work, and continuing it may be difficult, unsuitable or no longer the preferred option for that person.

## Build the follow-up plan before the last prescription

These are useful questions to take to the prescriber. They are a conversation guide, not a universal monitoring protocol.

If money is the deciding factor, say so before the prescription runs out. The [Irish funding guide](/health/glp1-hse-medical-card-ireland/) explains the schemes and their limits. It cannot promise an alternative will be funded, but it can help make the conversation more specific than “I cannot keep paying.”

If side effects are driving the decision, their severity matters. Use our [side-effects and warning-sign guide](/health/glp1-side-effects-when-to-seek-help/) to distinguish symptoms for a routine review from those needing urgent care. A planned appointment is not a reason to delay urgent assessment.

## Three situations that need their own advice

**Treatment for diabetes.** The withdrawal studies above excluded diabetes. Their weight findings are not a diabetes-management plan. Ozempic is authorised for type 2 diabetes, so stopping it requires attention to the condition it is treating, not just body weight. [EMA: Ozempic](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/human/EPAR/ozempic)

**Planning pregnancy.** Product-specific timing matters. Wegovy information says semaglutide should be discontinued at least two months before a planned pregnancy; Mounjaro information specifies at least one month for tirzepatide. Discuss this with the prescriber in advance, including any diabetes care. [Wegovy product information](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/product-information/wegovy-epar-product-information_en.pdf), [Mounjaro product information](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/product-information/mounjaro-epar-product-information_en.pdf)

**Restarting after a gap.** Do not assume the old dose is the right starting point. Wegovy's product information says a reduced starting dose should be considered when reinitiating after multiple missed doses. Ask for product-specific advice rather than treating a leftover pen as a restart plan. [EMA: Wegovy, section 4.2](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/product-information/wegovy-epar-product-information_en.pdf)

The last injection does not have to be the last point of contact. Agreeing the next appointment, the reasons to seek help sooner and the support available gives the next stage some structure. That remains worthwhile whether weight stays steady, rises or falls.
