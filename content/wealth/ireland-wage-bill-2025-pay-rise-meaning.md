**Ireland's total Compensation of Employees reached €165.2 billion in 2025, up almost €6.9 billion from 2024, according to new CSO research published on 17 November 2026.**

That works out as a **4.35% increase** in the national wage bill.

It does **not** mean that the average worker received a 4.35% salary increase.

The measure combines gross wages, overtime, bonuses, benefits in kind and employer social contributions across the whole economy. It also changes when the number of workers or hours worked changes.

For households, the release is most useful as a map of where employee compensation sits in the economy — not as a ready-made benchmark for an individual pay claim.

## How the €165.2bn figure changed

The [CSO retrospective](https://www.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-ceriwb/compensationofemployees-reviewingirelandswagebill2025/aretrospectiveofirelandswagebill/) reports total Compensation of Employees of:

- **€165.158 billion in 2025**; and
- **€158.269 billion in 2024**.

The difference is:

> €165.158bn − €158.269bn = **€6.890bn**

Relative to 2024:

> €6.890bn ÷ €158.269bn = **4.35%**

This is current-price national-accounting data. It has not been adjusted here for inflation, employee numbers or hours worked.

## What “Compensation of Employees” actually includes

The CSO measure is broader than take-home salary. It includes:

- gross wages and salaries;
- overtime and bonuses;
- benefits in kind, such as certain company-provided benefits; and
- social contributions paid by employers, including relevant pension contributions.

It is measured before the employee's Income Tax, USC, PRSI and other deductions.

That is why the total cannot be divided casually by a workforce estimate and presented as an “average salary”. Workers can have different hours, employment patterns and non-cash compensation, while the national total includes employer costs that never appear as spendable net pay.

## Why a 4.35% wage-bill rise is not your pay rise

The national total can increase through several channels at once:

1. more people are employed;
2. employees work more paid hours;
3. hourly pay or annual salaries rise;
4. overtime and bonuses increase;
5. employer pension or social contributions rise; or
6. employment shifts towards higher-paying sectors.

The CSO says the longer-term increase reflects both price changes and rising labour input, which is largely linked to total hours worked.

Suppose an employee earns €50,000. Applying 4.35% mechanically would produce:

> €50,000 × 4.35% = **€2,175**

That would imply a hypothetical salary of €52,175. It is only a worked illustration of the percentage. The CSO release does not say this employee is due €2,175, nor that comparable roles rose by that amount.

## Where the wage bill is concentrated

The [CSO key findings](https://www.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-ceriwb/compensationofemployees-reviewingirelandswagebill2025/keyfindings/) identify Public Administration, Education & Health as the largest broad sector:

| Broad sector | 2025 employee compensation | Share of total |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Public Administration, Education & Health | €45.8bn | 27.8% |
| Professional, Admin & Support Services | €24.1bn | 14.6% |
| Industry excluding Construction | €22.0bn | 13.3% |

These figures measure total compensation, not a typical salary in each sector. A sector with many employees can have a large wage bill even when individual pay is moderate.

The structural change over 30 years is notable. Professional, Admin & Support Services rose from 4.7% of the total in 1995 to 14.6% in 2025. Industry excluding Construction fell from 23.6% to 13.3%, even though its euro wage bill increased.

## What the gender figure does — and does not — show

The CSO attributes **58.1% of total employee compensation, or €96.0 billion, to male employees** in 2025. The female share was about 41.9%.

This is not a like-for-like gender pay-gap measure. The aggregate is affected by:

- the number of men and women employed;
- full-time and part-time patterns;
- hours worked;
- occupation and seniority;
- sector mix; and
- cash and non-cash compensation.

The sector breakdown illustrates that mix. Female employees accounted for 64.3% of compensation in Public Administration, Education & Health, but 10.3% in Construction.

The release is evidence about the distribution of the national wage bill. It does not establish what two people doing the same job were paid.

## High compensation and multinational sectors

Employees in foreign-owned multinational-dominated sectors represented a growing share of compensation in the higher bands. In the **€200,001 to €300,000** band, the CSO attributes 36.5% of compensation to those sectors.

Again, that is a share of compensation within a band, not a claim that 36.5% of all multinational employees earn between €200,001 and €300,000.

The distinction matters for salary comparisons. National technology or finance headlines can be a poor benchmark for a role in a different occupation, county or employer type.

## How to use the release in a pay review

The 4.35% national increase can provide economic context, but a stronger salary case combines it with role-specific evidence:

- changes in responsibilities and measurable output;
- current pay bands or a collective agreement;
- credible vacancies for comparable roles;
- scarce qualifications or shift requirements;
- retention and replacement costs; and
- inflation over the relevant review period.

Compare total compensation, not only basic salary. Employer pension contributions, shift premium, overtime, bonus, health insurance, annual leave and commuting costs can materially change the value of two offers.

## What the data say about national income

Compensation of Employees was equal to **38.4% of Gross National Income** in 2025. Against modified Gross National Income, or GNI*, the share was **49.5%**.

GNI* is designed to reduce some distortions from globalised activity in Ireland. The gap between the two ratios is a reminder that headline national accounts and household experience are not interchangeable.

The practical takeaway is simple: Ireland's employee-compensation pool grew substantially in 2025, but the national total cannot tell you what happened to your own hourly rate or take-home pay. Use it as context, then bring the comparison back to your role, hours, sector and complete remuneration package.
