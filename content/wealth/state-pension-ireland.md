The State Pension is one of the most important numbers in an Irish retirement plan.

It is also easy to oversimplify.

You may hear that the State Pension is **€299.30 a week** and assume that is what everyone gets at 66.

It is not.

The amount depends on whether you qualify for the **State Pension (Contributory)**, the **State Pension (Non-Contributory)**, your PRSI record, caring periods, when you choose to claim and — for the non-contributory pension — your means.

<div class="cs-intro">
<p class="cs-kicker">Quick answer</p>
<p><strong>The maximum State Pension (Contributory) rate in 2026 is €299.30 a week for someone under 80.</strong> That is €15,563.60 over 52 weeks.</p>
<p>You generally need at least <strong>520 reckonable contributions</strong> to qualify and <strong>2,080 contributions</strong> for the maximum rate under the Total Contributions Approach.</p>
<p>If you do not qualify for the contributory pension, or only qualify for a reduced rate, the means-tested State Pension (Non-Contributory) may be available. Its maximum 2026 rate is <strong>€288 a week</strong> for someone aged 66 to 79.</p>

The contributory pension can also include an increase for a qualified adult where the conditions are met.

For 2026, the maximum qualified-adult increase attached to the contributory pension is **€199.40 a week for a qualified adult under 66** and **€268.40 a week for a qualified adult aged 66 or over**.

Those additions are subject to their own rules and means assessment.

The **State Pension (Contributory)** is based on your social insurance record. Savings, investments or other income do not reduce it simply because you have more money.

The **State Pension (Non-Contributory)** is a social-assistance payment. It is means tested and requires you to be legally and habitually resident in Ireland.

If you qualify for only a low contributory rate, it can be worth applying for both. The Department says that where a person qualifies for both, it will award the higher payment.

## How many PRSI contributions do you need?

There are two numbers worth remembering.

### 520 contributions: the general minimum

In general, you need at least **520 full-rate reckonable contributions** — effectively 10 years — to qualify for the State Pension (Contributory).

That does not mean 520 contributions gets you the full pension.

It means you have crossed the general qualification threshold.

### 2,080 contributions: the full-rate TCA benchmark

Under the **Total Contributions Approach (TCA)**, **2,080 contributions** — the equivalent of 40 years at 52 contributions a year — produces the maximum personal rate.

A simple way to think about the TCA is:

> **reckonable contributions ÷ 2,080 = approximate share of the maximum TCA pension**

For example, 1,560 reckonable contributions is 75% of 2,080.

The actual Department calculation can include paid contributions, eligible credits and caring supports, so your Contribution Statement matters more than a rough estimate.

## The State Pension calculation changed again in 2026

Ireland is gradually removing the old **Yearly Average** method.

By 2034, new State Pension (Contributory) awards will be calculated using the Total Contributions Approach.

During the transition, the Department compares calculation methods and awards the more favourable result.

For someone drawing down the pension in **2026**, the two calculations are:

1. **Method 1:** TCA only.
2. **Method 2:** 80% of the Yearly Average result plus 20% of the TCA result.

You receive whichever method gives the higher pension.

This matters because two people with the same number of total contributions can have different work histories.

The old Yearly Average method can sometimes favour people with particular contribution patterns. The transition is designed to move the system toward counting total lifetime contributions instead.

## Get your Contribution Statement before guessing

The most useful practical step is to request your **Contribution Statement** through MyWelfare.

It shows the Department's record of:

- paid PRSI contributions;
- credited contributions;
- caring periods;
- Long-Term Carers Contributions;
- and contributions that are reckonable for pension purposes.

Do not wait until age 65 to look at it.

A missing employment period, an unrecorded caring period or a misunderstanding about which PRSI class counts can materially change your expected pension.

If retirement is still years away, identifying a gap early gives you more time to understand whether further contributions, voluntary contributions or caring supports can improve the position.

## What if you took time out to care for children or another person?

Time out of paid work does not automatically mean the period is lost for pension purposes.

Ireland has several **Pension Caring Supports**.

### HomeCaring Periods

HomeCaring Periods can help people who spent time caring for:

- children under 12;
- a child over 12 who required an increased level of care; or
- an adult who required an increased level of care.

These periods can help fill gaps when the TCA is used.

### Homemaker's Scheme

The Homemaker's Scheme can disregard certain years spent caring when a Yearly Average calculation applies.

Because the Yearly Average method is being phased out, this remains especially relevant during the transition years.

### Long-Term Carers Contributions

A major reform allows eligible full-time carers to receive **Long-Term Carers Contributions**.

Where a person has accumulated at least **1,040 weeks — 20 years — of qualifying full-time care**, those contributions can count as full-rate contributions for State Pension (Contributory) purposes.

The 20 years do not have to be consecutive.

For someone with a long caring history, this can be the difference between a reduced pension and a much stronger entitlement — and in some cases can establish entitlement where none existed before.

## Can you defer the State Pension until 70?

For people born on or after **1 January 1958**, the State Pension (Contributory) can be started on a chosen date between age **66 and 70**.

Deferring can help in two ways.

First, the pension rate may be **actuarially increased** because you start it later.

Second, if you continue working and paying full-rate PRSI, the additional contributions can improve your contribution record.

That means deferral is not merely a choice between "four years of payments now" and "a higher payment later".

For someone short of 520 contributions, the years from 66 to 70 may even help establish entitlement.

For someone between 520 and 2,080, extra contributions may increase the underlying rate before the deferral uplift is considered.

### But deferral is not automatically better

Deferring means giving up pension payments you could otherwise have received.

The decision depends on:

- your health and expected longevity;
- whether you are still working;
- your PRSI record;
- your tax position;
- other pension income;
- household cash flow;
- and how much the later weekly rate increases.

Once your State Pension (Contributory) starts, you cannot simply reverse the start date and go back to building PRSI entitlement under the deferral rules.

Treat it as a retirement-planning decision, not an automatic optimisation.

## Can you work and still receive the State Pension?

Yes.

You can continue to work after starting the State Pension (Contributory).

The important PRSI rule is that, from 2024, a person who defers the contributory pension can remain liable for PRSI until they are awarded the pension or reach age 70.

Once the State Pension (Contributory) has started, those later work contributions do not operate in the same way as the pre-drawdown record.

There was also an employment-law change in 2026.

From **29 June 2026**, eligible employees whose contractual retirement age is below the State Pension age of 66 gained a new right to notify their employer that they do not consent to retire at that lower contractual age, subject to the legislation's conditions.

That does not abolish every retirement age, but it reduces the gap that could previously arise between a contractual retirement date and access to the State Pension.

## What if you worked in the UK or another country?

Do not assume that years worked outside Ireland are irrelevant.

EU social-security rules and bilateral agreements can allow social insurance records from other countries to be considered when establishing entitlement.

The UK is particularly important for Irish workers because many people have contributions in both systems.

Depending on the circumstances, you may receive a pro-rata Irish State Pension and a separate pension entitlement from another country.

If your Irish record alone appears short of the qualification threshold, overseas work history is one of the first things to flag on your application.

## How does the State Pension (Non-Contributory) means test work?

The non-contributory pension is designed for people aged 66 or over who do not qualify for a sufficient contributory pension and meet the residence and means conditions.

The maximum rate in 2026 is:

- **€288 a week** for ages 66–79;
- **€298 a week** from age 80.

Your actual rate can be lower because the payment is means tested.

The Department looks at assessable income and capital under the scheme rules.

The family home is treated differently from ordinary capital, and not every euro of savings is treated as weekly income on a one-for-one basis.

The practical point is this:

> **Do not rule yourself out because you have some savings.**

The Department's own guidance shows that a single person can hold a meaningful level of capital and still qualify for at least some non-contributory pension.

If you have a weak PRSI record, apply and let the formal means test determine the result.

## Is the State Pension taxable?

Yes.

Both the contributory and non-contributory State Pensions are **liable to Income Tax**.

They are not subject to **USC or PRSI**.

If the State Pension is your only income, your tax credits and age-related exemptions may mean that little or no Income Tax is actually payable.

If you also receive an occupational pension, PRSA/ARF income, employment income or other taxable income, the State Pension can affect the overall tax calculation.

Revenue receives State Pension information from the Department of Social Protection and adjusts the tax treatment accordingly.

Read our [Pensions in Ireland guide](/wealth/pensions-in-ireland/) for the wider picture of State, workplace and personal pension income.

## Is the State Pension enough to retire on?

For many households, no.

At the 2026 maximum contributory rate, one pension provides about **€15,564 a year** before any Income Tax.

Two people each receiving the full under-80 rate would receive about **€31,127 a year** combined.

That may cover a significant part of essential spending for a mortgage-free household.

But retirement costs vary enormously.

Housing, transport, healthcare, home maintenance, travel, family support and long-term care can all push spending well above the State Pension.

The better question is:

> **What annual spending do we expect, and how much of it will the State Pension cover?**

Private pensions and other assets then need to fill the gap.

## How to apply for the State Pension

The Department recommends applying **six months before** the date you want the pension to start.

The quickest route is through **MyWelfare** using a verified MyGovID.

You can also use the paper application process.

For the contributory pension, the application asks for the date you want payments to begin.

For eligible people born on or after 1 January 1958, that can be any chosen date between ages 66 and 70.

A sensible pre-application checklist is:

1. Request your Contribution Statement.
2. Check for missing employment or PRSI periods.
3. Check whether caring periods are recorded correctly.
4. Gather details of work in other countries.
5. Decide whether age 66 is the right start date or whether deferral deserves analysis.
6. If your contributory record looks weak, apply for the non-contributory pension as well.
7. Apply around six months before your intended start date.

## A simple State Pension planning example

Imagine someone reaches 66 in 2026 with a long but incomplete PRSI record.

They should not jump straight from "I do not have 40 years" to "I will not get the State Pension".

Instead:

- confirm whether they have at least 520 reckonable contributions;
- check all credits and caring periods;
- compare the TCA calculation with the transitional 2026 blended calculation;
- check overseas contribution history;
- consider whether working longer could improve the record;
- and compare any reduced contributory rate with the non-contributory means-tested option.

That is why the Contribution Statement is so important.

The pension is determined by the record and the rules — not by a rough count of years you remember working.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the State Pension age in Ireland in 2026?

The State Pension is available from **age 66**. Eligible people born on or after 1 January 1958 can choose to defer the State Pension (Contributory) and start it on a chosen date up to age 70.

### How much is the State Pension in Ireland in 2026?

The maximum State Pension (Contributory) rate for someone under 80 is **€299.30 per week**. The maximum State Pension (Non-Contributory) rate for ages 66–79 is **€288 per week**.

### How many years of PRSI do I need for the Irish State Pension?

In general, you need at least **520 reckonable contributions**, equivalent to 10 full years, to qualify for the State Pension (Contributory). Under the Total Contributions Approach, 2,080 contributions is the benchmark for the maximum rate.

### Is the State Pension means tested?

The State Pension (Contributory) is **not** means tested. The State Pension (Non-Contributory) **is** means tested.

### Can I get the State Pension if I have a private pension?

Yes. A private pension does not stop you receiving the State Pension (Contributory) if you qualify through your PRSI record. Other income and assets can affect the means-tested State Pension (Non-Contributory).

### Can I claim the State Pension and keep working?

Yes. You can continue working while receiving the State Pension (Contributory).

### Can carers qualify for extra pension contributions?

Yes. HomeCaring Periods, the Homemaker's Scheme and Long-Term Carers Contributions can help protect or improve pension entitlement, depending on the caring history and the calculation method used.

### Should I defer the State Pension to age 70?

Not automatically. Deferral can increase the eventual rate and may allow more PRSI contributions to build, but you give up payments in the meantime. The best choice depends on your record, health, employment, tax and other retirement income.

## The bottom line

For most people, there are four State Pension numbers to remember:

- **66** — the age the State Pension is available;
- **520** — the general minimum number of reckonable contributions for the contributory pension;
- **2,080** — the TCA benchmark for the maximum contributory rate;
- **€299.30** — the maximum weekly contributory rate for someone under 80 in 2026.

But the most useful action is not memorising the numbers.

It is checking your actual PRSI record.

Request your Contribution Statement, correct gaps early and treat the State Pension as one layer of a wider retirement plan.
