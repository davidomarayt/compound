A pension lump sum can be tax free in Ireland, but the phrase **"25% tax free"** is easy to misunderstand.

For a personal pension, Revenue says an individual can generally take **25% of the fund as a retirement lump sum**. But the amount that is actually tax free is subject to a separate **€200,000 lifetime limit across retirement lump sums from all sources**.

So 25% describes how a personal-pension lump sum can be calculated. It does not create an unlimited tax-free entitlement.

## The three retirement lump-sum tax bands

For relevant retirement lump sums, the current framework can be understood in three cumulative bands.

| Cumulative retirement lump sums | Tax treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Up to €200,000 | Tax free |
| €200,001 to €500,000 | 20% excess lump-sum tax |
| Above €500,000 | Taxed under PAYE at the individual's marginal rate |

These are cumulative lifetime thresholds, not limits that reset for each pension pot.

## Why the €200,000 limit is a lifetime limit

Imagine someone has three different pension arrangements accumulated across a career.

If they take a €100,000 retirement lump sum from one pension and later take another €100,000 from a second pension, they have used the €200,000 lifetime tax-free amount.

A later retirement lump sum does not receive a fresh €200,000 allowance simply because it comes from a different pension provider.

Revenue's rules take account of relevant retirement lump sums from all sources.

## Do previous lump sums count?

Yes.

Revenue's Pensions Manual explains that previous retirement lump sums received on or after **7 December 2005** are taken into account when determining how much of the lifetime tax-free amount and standard chargeable amount have already been used.

That makes record-keeping important, particularly for someone retiring with several pension arrangements or who has already crystallised part of a pension.

The calculator therefore asks for previous retirement lump sums before showing the remaining tax-free allowance.

## Worked example: €400,000 personal pension

A 25% retirement lump sum is:

**€400,000 × 25% = €100,000**

If there are no previous retirement lump sums, the full €100,000 sits within the €200,000 lifetime tax-free limit.

Estimated lump-sum tax: **€0**.

The remaining pension funds are subject to the separate retirement options and tax rules that apply to the arrangement.

## Worked example: €800,000 personal pension

A 25% retirement lump sum is:

**€800,000 × 25% = €200,000**

With no previous retirement lump sums, this uses the full lifetime tax-free amount.

Estimated tax on the €200,000 retirement lump sum: **€0** under the current lifetime limit.

This is the example many people have in mind when they hear "25% tax free".

But it stops being that simple once previous lump sums exist or the current lump sum exceeds €200,000.

## Worked example: €1.2 million fund after a previous €100,000 lump sum

Twenty-five per cent of €1.2 million is **€300,000**.

Assume the person has previously received €100,000 of relevant retirement lump sums.

Before the new payment:

- €100,000 of the €200,000 tax-free lifetime amount has already been used;
- €100,000 remains.

For the new €300,000 lump sum:

- first €100,000 uses the remaining tax-free allowance;
- next €200,000 falls in the cumulative €200,001–€500,000 band;
- that €200,000 portion is taxed at 20%.

Estimated tax on that current lump sum: **€40,000**.

Estimated net current lump sum: **€260,000**.

## What happens above €500,000?

Once cumulative retirement lump sums exceed €500,000, Revenue taxes the excess under PAYE at the individual's marginal Income Tax rate.

Revenue's public guidance currently describes this as the higher rate of 40%. Its Pensions Manual provides the more technical description: amounts above the cumulative threshold are taxed under Schedule E at the individual's marginal rate.

That distinction matters in unusual cases, so Compound's Advanced mode allows the marginal PAYE assumption to be changed rather than pretending every taxpayer's circumstances are identical.

## Does every pension use the 25% rule?

No.

Revenue distinguishes personal pensions from occupational pension schemes.

For a personal pension, 25% of the fund is generally available as a retirement lump sum.

An occupational scheme can instead calculate retirement lump-sum entitlement by reference to **salary and service**.

That means someone with an occupational pension should not simply multiply the fund value by 25% and assume that is the scheme benefit.

The Advanced mode of the calculator allows a scheme-quoted lump sum to be entered directly.

## What happens to the rest of the pension?

Taking a retirement lump sum is only one retirement decision.

Revenue notes that the remaining personal-pension funds may, depending on the arrangement and conditions, be used for options such as:

- purchasing an annuity;
- transferring to an Approved Retirement Fund; or
- taking another taxable pension option.

The tax and investment consequences of the remaining funds are separate from the lump-sum calculation.

This is why the largest available lump sum is not automatically the best financial choice for every person. Liquidity, income needs, investment risk, estate planning and the tax position of the remaining pension can all matter.

## Pension tax relief going in versus tax coming out

Pension contributions can receive Income Tax relief during the saving years, subject to Revenue's limits.

At retirement, lump sums and pension income have their own rules.

Keeping those two stages separate helps avoid a common misconception: tax relief on contributions does not mean every euro withdrawn from a pension later is tax free.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is 25% of a pension always tax free in Ireland?

No. A personal pension can generally provide a 25% retirement lump sum, but the amount that can be tax free is subject to the €200,000 lifetime limit across retirement lump sums.

### What is the tax-free pension lump-sum limit?

The current lifetime tax-free retirement-lump-sum limit is €200,000.

### Is the €200,000 limit per pension pot?

No. It is a lifetime limit across retirement lump sums from all sources.

### How is the amount between €200,000 and €500,000 taxed?

The cumulative amount from €200,001 to €500,000 is taxed at 20%.

### What tax applies above €500,000?

The portion above the cumulative €500,000 threshold is taxed under PAYE at the individual's marginal rate.

### Do older pension lump sums affect the limit?

Relevant retirement lump sums previously received on or after 7 December 2005 are taken into account under Revenue's rules.

### Can I use 25% for an occupational pension?

Not necessarily. Occupational schemes can calculate the retirement lump sum using salary and service rules. Check the scheme's benefit quotation.

## Before taking a retirement lump sum

Gather the lump-sum history from all pensions, not just the scheme currently being accessed.

Then compare:

- the scheme's actual lump-sum entitlement;
- previous retirement lump sums;
- the remaining lifetime tax-free amount;
- tax on any amount above the thresholds; and
- what happens to the pension funds that remain invested or are converted into retirement income.
