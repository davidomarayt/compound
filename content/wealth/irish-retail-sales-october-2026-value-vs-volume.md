**Irish shops took in 6.2% more money in October than a year earlier, but the amount of goods they sold rose by only 3.9%, according to the [Central Statistics Office's Retail Sales Index](https://www.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-rsi/retailsalesindexoctober2026provisionalandseptember2026final/) published on Friday 27 November. The gap between those two numbers is, roughly, the extra that shoppers paid for the same things.**

Most coverage focused on the small monthly fall in volume (−0.1%). The more useful story for household budgets sits in the CSO's sector table, which reports both what was spent (value) and how much was bought (volume) for each type of shop.

## The headline numbers

From the CSO release, seasonally adjusted:

- **Volume of sales:** down 0.1% on September, up 3.9% on October 2025.
- **Value of sales:** up 0.3% on September, up 6.2% on October 2025.
- **Excluding cars (motor trades):** volume up 3.3% and value up 5.9% on the year.
- **Biggest monthly volume falls:** bars (−3.0%), motor trades (−1.4%), department stores (−1.2%).

The October figures are provisional and cover the four weeks from 4 to 31 October.

## Paying more for the same: sector by sector

"Value" is the money spent in euro. "Volume" strips out price changes, so it measures how much was actually bought. If value rises faster than volume, the difference is mostly higher prices.

We divided the two for each sector. This is our own rough calculation, not an official price index. It does not replace the [Consumer Price Index](https://www.rte.ie/news/business/2026/1111/1598813-cso-inflation-figures/), which put inflation at 4.3% in October.

| Type of shop | Spending vs Oct 2025 | Amount bought vs Oct 2025 | Implied price effect (our calculation) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bars | +0.3% | −6.5% | about +7.3% |
| Fuel | +5.9% | 0.0% | about +5.9% |
| Hardware, paints & glass | +7.0% | +3.8% | about +3.1% |
| Pharmacy, medical & cosmetics | +8.9% | +5.7% | about +3.0% |
| Department stores | +2.5% | −0.1% | about +2.6% |
| Electrical goods | +4.3% | +1.8% | about +2.5% |
| Clothing & footwear | +4.3% | +2.2% | about +2.1% |
| Supermarkets and other non-specialised stores | +4.6% | +3.5% | about +1.1% |
| Motor trades | +5.0% | +5.6% | about −0.6% |
| **All retail** | **+6.2%** | **+3.9%** | **about +2.2%** |

How the calculation works, using fuel: (1 + 5.9%) ÷ (1 + 0.0%) − 1 = 5.9%. For bars: 1.003 ÷ 0.935 − 1 = about 7.3%.

Two stand out:

- **Fuel.** Forecourts sold exactly the same amount as a year ago and took 5.9% more for it. A household that spent €60 a week on petrol or diesel in October 2025 would pay about **€63.54** for the same litres now. That is about **€3.54 a week**, or roughly **€184 a year**.
- **Pubs.** People bought 6.5% less in bars but spent slightly more in total. In other words, fewer drinks at higher prices.

Supermarkets show the smallest gap of the main categories over the past year, about 1.1%. Car sales went the other way: buyers spent less per unit of volume than a year ago.

## The bigger picture since 2021

The CSO indices use 2021 as the base year (2021 = 100). Comparing value with volume over the full period shows how much more the same basket costs now. Again, this is our rough calculation from the CSO's October 2026 index levels:

| Type of shop | Value index | Volume index | Spending needed for the same amount as 2021 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fuel | 134.2 | 104.5 | about 28% more |
| Bars | 222.6 | 176.6 | about 26% more |
| Specialist food, drink & tobacco shops | 101.7 | 80.8 | about 26% more |
| Supermarkets and other non-specialised stores | 123.1 | 99.1 | about 24% more |
| Electrical goods | 108.3 | 94.4 | about 15% more |
| **All retail** | **134.6** | **119.0** | **about 13% more** |

The supermarket line is the one most households will feel. Spending in supermarkets is about 23% higher than the 2021 average, yet the volume of goods bought is slightly below it (99.1). Households are spending a lot more to bring home about the same.

## A note on these figures

The CSO's value and volume indices are seasonally adjusted separately, so dividing one by the other gives an approximate price effect, not an exact one. The volume measure also reflects changes in the mix of goods within each sector. The October figures are provisional and will be revised next month. The CSO has also said all volume indices will be revised in the coming months after a change to how the Consumer Price Index classifies goods.
