Microsoft’s latest earnings-per-share growth depended overwhelmingly on higher profit, with only a small contribution from a lower diluted share count. That matters for investors building a valuation: a large repurchase headline is not a reliable shortcut for forecasting how quickly each remaining share’s claim on earnings will grow.

This analysis uses the quarter ended **30 September 2026—Microsoft’s fiscal 2027 first quarter**. It examines existing filings rather than a new buyback announcement.

## A 1.3-cent share-count effect

Microsoft’s [results announcement](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor/earnings/fy-2027-q1/press-release-webcast) provides the inputs for a simple bridge:

| Reported quarterly measure | September 2025 | September 2026 |
|---|---:|---:|
| GAAP net income | $27.747bn | $36.629bn |
| Weighted-average basic shares | 7,433m | 7,426m |
| Weighted-average diluted shares | 7,466m | 7,446m |
| Diluted EPS | $3.72 | $4.92 |

Hold current profit fixed and divide it by the **previous year’s diluted denominator**: $36,629m ÷ 7,466m gives approximately **$4.906**. Using the current denominator gives approximately **$4.919**. The difference is **$0.0132 per share**, or about 1.3 cents.

That is the mechanical effect of the denominator change, not a causal estimate of what this quarter’s buybacks earned. Repurchase timing, share issuance and the treatment of stock awards all affect the weighted average. Nevertheless, it is a useful discipline: the share-count change contributes only a small fraction of the reported $1.20 EPS increase.

The diluted count declined by 20 million, but the basic count declined by only seven million. The remainder reflects a smaller incremental diluted-share adjustment. Investors should not label all 20 million as shares removed through buybacks. Nor should they attribute all the profit improvement to recurring operations: the release separately identifies investment-related effects in its GAAP and non-GAAP results.

## The repurchase programme and employee withholding differ

[Note 14 of the 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/789019/000119312526436133/msft-20260930.htm) reports **$2.925 billion** spent under the repurchase programme, versus **$3.955 billion** a year earlier. Compound calculates a **26.0% decline**. The note separately excludes **$1.5 billion** of employee tax-withholding repurchases in the latest quarter.

Withholding shares as awards vest serves a different purpose from discretionary purchases in the market. Both can involve cash, but combining them and describing the total as a deliberate reduction in outside investors’ share count obscures the compensation mechanism.

The balance sheet is a useful cross-check: rounded common shares outstanding were **7,427 million at both June and September**. That is a point-in-time measure, unlike the quarterly averages above. It shows no reduction at the published rounding precision across those two dates; it does not prove that buybacks had no value. Without repurchases, issuance might have left more shares outstanding.

## What should a shareholder model?

A disciplined forecast needs two separate assumptions: how much profit the business can earn, and how many diluted shares will participate in that profit. Forecasting buyback dollars is an intermediate step, not the final answer.

For example, assuming a large annual share-count decline simply because Microsoft keeps repurchasing stock could overstate future EPS. Conversely, dismissing purchases because the closing count looks flat would overlook their possible role in offsetting issuance. The investment question is the net result after compensation and other share movements, at the price paid.

Microsoft disclosed **$37.7 billion** remaining under its authorisation at September-end. The programme has no expiry and can be terminated. That is permission to buy, not a timetable or management promise to shrink the denominator by a specified amount.

For the next filing, compare programme spending with basic and diluted shares separately, preserving the same periods and definitions. Strong business growth accompanied by stable shares can still produce attractive per-share progress. A valuation should earn that conclusion through its profit assumptions rather than importing an unsupported buyback boost.
