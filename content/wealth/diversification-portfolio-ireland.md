**Investing in Ireland 101 · Chapter 5 of 9**

[← Investment tax](/wealth/investment-tax-ireland/) · [Course hub](/wealth/how-to-start-investing-in-ireland/) · [Next: Choosing a broker →](/wealth/choose-investment-broker-ireland/)

Diversification is often reduced to “do not put all your eggs in one basket.”

That is correct but incomplete.

A portfolio can contain twenty investments and still be concentrated.

It can own five funds and still be dominated by the same US mega-cap companies.

It can look global while your salary, house, pension and shares are all tied to the Irish economy.

The useful question is not **how many holdings do I own?**

It is:

> **What single event could damage too much of my financial life at once?**

## What diversification is trying to solve

Diversification reduces risks that come from relying too heavily on:

- one company;
- one sector;
- one country;
- one currency;
- one asset class;
- one investment style;
- or one source of income.

It cannot eliminate market risk.

A globally diversified equity portfolio can still fall sharply during a global recession or financial crisis.

The purpose is to stop one avoidable concentration from determining the entire result.

## Company diversification

If 50% of a portfolio is one company, that company can dominate the outcome.

Even excellent businesses face:

- competition;
- regulation;
- product failure;
- accounting problems;
- management mistakes;
- disruption;
- litigation;
- or changing valuations.

Company diversification is the most obvious layer.

But it is only the first.

## Sector diversification

Imagine owning ten companies.

At first glance, that seems diversified.

But if all ten are banks, your portfolio is highly exposed to:

- interest rates;
- credit losses;
- financial regulation;
- housing;
- and the economic cycle.

Ten tickers do not create ten independent risks.

The same problem can happen with technology, pharmaceuticals, energy or property.

## Country diversification

Irish investors often already have substantial Ireland exposure through:

- salary;
- home;
- pension;
- business;
- State finances;
- and future employment opportunities.

That is not automatically bad.

But adding an entirely Irish share portfolio can concentrate the same economic exposure further.

Global diversification can spread corporate exposure across economies that do not all move in exactly the same way.

## Currency exposure

A global portfolio will often contain assets earning revenue in many currencies.

Currency movements can affect euro returns over shorter periods.

But currency exposure is more nuanced than simply reading the currency in which an ETF is quoted.

An ETF trading in euro can still own US, Japanese and Swiss companies.

**Trading currency is not the same as economic currency exposure.**

## Asset-class diversification

Shares are not the only financial asset.

Depending on the goal, a portfolio may include:

- cash;
- government bonds;
- corporate bonds;
- equities;
- property-related assets;
- or other regulated investments.

Different assets have different expected returns, volatility and sensitivity to interest rates and economic conditions.

The correct mix depends on time horizon and risk capacity.

## Why asset allocation matters more than stock count

Suppose Portfolio A holds:

- 100 global companies;
- but 100% in equities.

Portfolio B holds:

- global equities;
- high-quality bonds;
- and cash.

Portfolio A is highly diversified **within equities**.

Portfolio B is diversified across **asset classes** as well.

Neither is automatically better. A 30-year retirement investor and a person five years from needing the money can reasonably choose different mixes.

## False diversification: five funds, same holdings

This is common.

An investor owns:

- S&P 500 fund;
- Nasdaq 100 fund;
- global technology fund;
- US growth fund;
- global equity fund.

The portfolio contains five products but may repeatedly own the same largest US companies.

That can create a much more concentrated portfolio than the number of funds suggests.

Look at the underlying holdings.

## Home bias

Home bias means allocating more to your own country than its share of the global investment opportunity set.

Some home bias can be deliberate.

But it should be recognised rather than accidental.

For an Irish household, the home itself may already be the largest asset. Employment may also depend on the domestic economy or on a small number of multinational sectors operating in Ireland.

Portfolio diversification should be considered alongside the rest of the household balance sheet.

## Diversification and pensions

Do not analyse a taxable brokerage account in isolation.

If you have:

- €100,000 in a pension;
- €20,000 in a taxable investment account;

the pension is the larger investment portfolio.

If the pension is already globally diversified, the taxable account may not need to duplicate it exactly.

Conversely, if the pension is highly concentrated, that should be part of the household-level risk discussion.

## A simple concentration audit

For each holding, estimate its exposure by:

| Layer | Question |
|---|---|
| Company | Is any one company more important than I intended? |
| Sector | Would one sector slump damage most of the portfolio? |
| Country | Am I heavily dependent on one economy? |
| Currency | Do I understand where underlying revenues come from? |
| Asset class | Is everything exposed to the same market risk? |
| Time horizon | Is the risk appropriate for when I need the money? |
| Wrapper | Are tax and access rules creating hidden concentration? |

The purpose is not to produce a perfect mathematical score.

It is to identify the concentrations you did not knowingly choose.

## How much diversification is enough?

There is no universal number.

More holdings can reduce company-specific risk, but after a point adding another similar security may contribute very little.

A broad diversified fund can sometimes provide more underlying diversification than dozens of hand-picked shares.

But remember the Irish tax question from Chapter 4: product structure and diversification are separate decisions.

## Diversification can feel disappointing during bubbles

A concentrated portfolio can outperform spectacularly when its favoured theme is rising.

A diversified portfolio will usually contain things that are underperforming something else.

That is normal.

Diversification works precisely because not every part of the portfolio is making the same bet.

The psychological cost is watching a concentrated winner do better for a period and wondering why you own anything else.

## Rebalancing restores the intended risk

Suppose a portfolio starts:

- 70% equities;
- 30% bonds.

After a strong equity run, it becomes:

- 82% equities;
- 18% bonds.

The portfolio is now taking more equity risk than originally intended.

Rebalancing means bringing the allocation back toward its target.

That can be done by selling, by directing new contributions toward the underweight asset, or by a combination.

Tax and transaction costs matter in taxable accounts, so rebalancing should not be mechanical.

Chapter 9 covers maintenance in more detail.

## The Irish tax trade-off

A tax-efficient-looking structure that creates extreme concentration may be a bad portfolio.

A perfectly diversified structure with poor tax treatment may also be suboptimal.

The job is to balance:

- diversification;
- tax;
- fees;
- liquidity;
- simplicity;
- and behaviour.

There is no single metric that replaces all six.

## A better way to think about “risk”

Instead of asking:

> “How risky is this fund?”

Ask:

> “What role does this holding play in my whole financial life?”

A volatile global equity fund may be reasonable inside a thirty-year pension.

The same fund may be inappropriate for next year's house deposit.

Risk belongs to the **goal**, not just the product.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is one global ETF diversified enough?

It can provide broad company and country diversification if its underlying index is broad, but it may still be 100% equities and may have material exposure to particular countries or sectors. Tax treatment also needs to be checked for an Irish investor.

### Is owning Irish shares a bad idea?

Not inherently. The issue is concentration. Consider how much Ireland exposure you already have through employment, property, pension assets and business interests.

### How often should I rebalance?

There is no universal schedule. Many long-term investors review periodically or when allocations move materially from target, while considering tax and dealing costs.

### Does more funds mean more diversification?

No. Multiple funds can overlap heavily. Look at the underlying holdings and exposures.
