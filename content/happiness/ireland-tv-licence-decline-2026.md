**Ireland's €160 TV licence is losing paying customers. Not just this year, but year after year.** The latest figures suggest 2026 will mark another fall — and they put a question back on the political agenda: is the collection system the problem, or is the funding model itself running out of road?

## What the latest figures actually show

Figures given by Media Minister Patrick O'Donovan in response to a Dáil question, and reported by [The Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/ireland/2026/10/10/income-from-broken-television-licence-fee-down-57m-in-first-nine-months/) on 10 October, show **536,551 paid licences sold from January to September 2026**.

That's **35,748 fewer licences** than in the comparable nine months of 2025, a decline of roughly **6.2%**. The associated sales revenue is €85.86 million, approximately €5.7 million below the previous year.

Extending that nine-month run rate across a full year produces a forecast of **715,401 licences and €114.46 million**. The full-year number is **a projection, not a confirmed 2026 total**.

An €8.5 million shortfall is the revenue equivalent of around **53,125 licences** at €160 each. It is not a separate count of households that stopped paying: year-on-year changes in actual licence sales must be read from the sales figures.

## Eight years of decline, in one chart

[chart:1]

There were **1,025,534 paid licences sold in 2019**. The 2026 forecast is **310,133 lower** — roughly three in every ten compared with that baseline.

The steepest visible shift came after 2022. Official returns show **947,924 licences in 2022**, followed by **824,278 in 2023**, the year of the RTÉ payments controversy. Sales fell again to **792,243 in 2024** and **768,657 in 2025**.

The RTÉ controversy damaged public trust, but the declining trend predates it. Changing household viewing habits and reduced ownership of conventional television sets may also play a part. The sales series alone **cannot tell us how much is due to non-payment, how much to changing technology, or how much to people who no longer need a licence**.

## What €37 million less looks like

[chart:2]

At the unchanged **€160 fee**, paid licence sales generated about **€151.66 million in 2022**. The 2026 projection is **€114.46 million**: a decline of approximately **€37.2 million, or 24.5%**, in four years.

That is **not** the same as the total income RTÉ receives from licence-related public funding. RTÉ's 2025 annual report records **€183.5 million** in licence fee income, which includes the State-funded free-licence scheme. Comparing that larger figure directly with the €114.46 million forecast for *paid sales* would mix different measures.

## Does streaming make the €160 licence obsolete?

**Not legally.** In Ireland, a smart TV still generally needs a licence even if its owner watches only Netflix or YouTube. Watching streaming services on a computer or phone does not normally require one, provided the device cannot receive television signals through the relevant equipment.

These distinctions are explained by [Citizens Information](https://api.uat.citizensinformation.ie/en/consumer/phone-internet-tv-and-postal-services/tv-licences/) and [An Post's TV licence service](https://www.tvlicence.ie/). The law has **not** been abolished, and a fall in sales is not permission to stop paying when a licence is required.

But the distinction exposes the policy problem. Two households can watch much the same online programmes yet face different obligations depending on whether they own a television set.

## The Government has a bigger RTÉ funding problem

The licence debate comes immediately after a separate Budget 2027 dispute.

The Government's 2024 funding agreement envisaged **€260 million** in total public funding for RTÉ in 2027, including licence receipts and Exchequer support. This week's announced allocation is **€240 million**, €20 million below that earlier plan. RTÉ and the Government are discussing the shortfall, according to [RTÉ's reporting on 9 October](https://www.rte.ie/news/ireland/2026/1009/1594724-chambers-odonovan/).

These are **separate numbers**: the €20 million Budget allocation difference is not the €8.5 million year-on-year drop in projected paid licence revenue.

RTÉ's public-service remit — Irish news, sport, culture, accessible broadcasting and Irish-language output — does not disappear when fewer licences are sold. Equally, a case for public-service media is not an argument for unlimited funding without transparency or accountability.

**The core question is no longer whether public-service broadcasting is worth funding. It is whether a payment system tied to owning a TV set is the right way to do it.**

The licence remains law in 2026. Yet another year of falling paid sales makes reform harder to postpone — and simply asking a shrinking group of people to keep paying €160 is not a long-term funding strategy.
