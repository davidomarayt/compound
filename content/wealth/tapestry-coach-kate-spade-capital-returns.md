**Tapestry’s results increasingly depend on one brand. Coach is growing, widening its margin and funding a larger capital-return plan, while Kate Spade is shrinking and losing money. The investment question is whether Coach can carry both the turnaround and the buyback without reducing financial flexibility.**

For the quarter ended 26 September 2026, Tapestry reported **$1.89 billion of sales, up 11%**. Excluding the divested Stuart Weitzman business from the comparison, pro-forma growth was 12%.

Coach sales rose 16.1% to **$1.66 billion** and operating profit increased 21% to $590 million. Kate Spade sales fell 10.4% to $233 million and the brand recorded a $20 million operating loss.

For the maintained financial record, see the [Tapestry stock profile](/stocks/tpr/). This analysis asks whether Tapestry’s strongest asset can fund brand repair and approximately $2 billion of planned shareholder returns in fiscal 2027.

## Coach is the economic centre

Coach produced about 88% of Tapestry’s quarterly sales, calculated from the reported segment figures. Its operating margin increased to **35.5% from 34.1%**.

That combination—double-digit growth and a higher margin—is powerful. It suggests that the brand is not buying revenue solely through discounting. Tapestry attributes the group’s gross-margin improvement to pricing, brand mix, operational improvements, tariffs and currency.

The concentration is also a risk. Tapestry is no longer a balanced portfolio if one brand supplies nearly nine-tenths of sales and more than all segment operating profit. A slowdown at Coach would affect not only consolidated earnings but also the cash available to repair Kate Spade and repurchase shares.

Investors should watch Coach customer growth, full-price sales and gross margin together. Revenue growth is less valuable if it eventually requires heavier promotion or pulls demand forward.

## Kate Spade is consuming the cushion

Kate Spade’s operating margin fell to **negative 8.7% from positive 2.1%**. On a non-GAAP basis, the brand still lost $6 million, so the problem was not limited to restructuring charges.

The filing attributes the sales decline mainly to direct-to-consumer weakness. Management can simplify product, reduce promotions and improve marketing, but turnarounds often create a conflict: protecting brand positioning can mean accepting lower revenue before demand recovers.

Coach gives Tapestry time to make those changes. It does not make the cost disappear. Corporate expenses and the loss-making brand consume part of Coach’s profit, while management must decide how much to reinvest before returning cash.

The clearest improvement would be Kate Spade narrowing its loss without relying on a short-lived inventory clearance. Stable sales with better full-price demand would be more convincing than a one-quarter revenue bounce.

## Margin expansion has several sources

Tapestry’s GAAP gross margin rose to 78.6% from 76.3%. Non-GAAP gross margin increased 2.2 percentage points to 78.7%.

The company’s bridge included approximately **1.3 percentage points of operational improvement**, a 0.7-point net benefit from tariffs and duties, and a 0.2-point foreign-exchange benefit. Operational improvement is the most useful component because it can reflect pricing, sourcing and mix that management can influence. Tariffs and currency can reverse.

Non-GAAP operating margin rose 2.3 percentage points to 23.2%, even as marketing investment increased. That supports the view that current Coach growth carries operating leverage. The durability test is whether margin remains higher when foreign-exchange and tariff comparisons become less helpful.

## The return plan leans on a full year of cash

Tapestry returned approximately **$700 million** to shareholders in the first quarter, including $600 million of repurchases. It now expects about $2.0 billion of fiscal-year capital returns, with $1.7 billion allocated to buybacks.

First-quarter adjusted free cash flow was only $56 million, down from $103 million. It would be misleading to conclude that this one quarter funds the repurchases: Tapestry’s cash flow is seasonal, and the company expects full-year adjusted free cash flow approaching $1.7 billion.

The balance sheet supplied part of the flexibility. Cash and short-term investments were $782 million, while total borrowings were $2.56 billion. Management reported gross debt equal to 1.1 times adjusted EBITDA.

That leverage is not extreme for a profitable business, but the proposed capital returns roughly match the full-year cash-flow target plus the dividend. If Coach slows or Kate Spade needs more investment, repurchases may compete with resilience. Buybacks also create value only if the price paid is below the value of the future cash flows retired.

## What would confirm the thesis?

Coach needs to sustain growth without giving back its gross-margin gains. Kate Spade should narrow its loss and show evidence that direct demand—not discounting—is improving.

At group level, full-year adjusted free cash flow should approach management’s $1.7 billion target after approximately $300 million of capital expenditure and cloud-computing costs. The diluted share count should decline meaningfully after buybacks, while leverage remains controlled.

The counter-case is that Coach’s momentum softens as Kate Spade continues to consume profit. In that scenario, a fixed commitment to repurchases could leave less room for marketing, inventory discipline or debt reduction.

**Tapestry’s quarter demonstrates how valuable Coach has become. It also exposes how dependent the group is on that one engine.** The $2 billion return plan can work if Coach keeps compounding and Kate Spade stops absorbing the gains; otherwise, capital allocation becomes the pressure point.
