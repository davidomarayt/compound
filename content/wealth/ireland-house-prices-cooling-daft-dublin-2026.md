<p><em>News analysis · 28 September 2026. Asking prices, estate-agent valuations and completed transaction prices measure different parts of the housing market and should not be treated as interchangeable.</em></p>

For the first time in a while, several Irish housing indicators are pointing in the same direction:

**the market is cooling.**

That does not mean house prices are collapsing. It does not mean homes are suddenly affordable. And it does not mean Ireland has solved its housing shortage.

But the latest evidence suggests buyers in some parts of the country are facing **less intense competition than they were a year ago**.

Daft's latest Q3 report shows national asking-price inflation slowing to **3%**, from **5.8%** a year earlier. The number of second-hand homes for sale has risen **12.5% year-on-year**, and the gap between asking and final selling prices has narrowed sharply.

Separately, the Irish Independent reports that property values in **Dublin and parts of the commuter belt are flatlining**, as affordability and cost-of-living pressures weigh on demand.

Put together, the picture is not a housing crash.

It is a market that appears to be becoming **less frantic, more price-sensitive and increasingly split by region**.

For anyone thinking about buying, the more useful question is not "are house prices falling?"

It is:

> **Does this change what I can realistically afford, borrow and bid?**

## What does the latest Daft report show?

Daft's Q3 2026 sales report, reported by RTÉ this morning, puts the average asking price nationally at roughly:

**€445,000**

That is still extremely high.

But the **rate of increase** has slowed materially.

Annual asking-price growth was:

- **3%** in the year to September 2026;
- compared with **5.8%** a year earlier.

The number of second-hand homes available for sale on Daft at the start of September was around:

**13,800**

That was **12.5% higher than a year earlier**.

However, Daft says that is still only about **half the pre-pandemic norm of more than 26,000 homes**.

So supply is improving from a very low base.

It has not returned to normal.

## Dublin is cooling faster than much of the country

The national average hides large regional differences.

Daft's latest report shows annual asking-price growth in Dublin of around:

**2.7%**

while several major cities outside Dublin continue to record stronger increases.

The report also points to softer transaction activity in the capital. Professor Ronan Lyons said the clearest sign of weaker demand was that sales of both new and second-hand homes had fallen in Dublin.

That distinction matters.

A housing market can cool because supply surges.

But it can also cool because buyers simply hit their affordability ceiling.

The latest Dublin data suggests the second factor may be increasingly important.

## The commuter belt is showing the same pressure

The Irish Independent separately reports that property values in Dublin and the commuter belt are **flatlining amid cost-of-living pressures**.

That fits a broader affordability story already visible in other datasets.

The Society of Chartered Surveyors Ireland reported in July that affordability challenges were intensifying for first-time buyers, including couples earning a combined **€113,000**.

Its worked example found that a couple on that income could face an affordability gap of up to **€25,000 in parts of the Dublin commuter belt**.

That is an important change in the housing story.

For years, the dominant problem was that buyers kept chasing a limited number of homes higher.

Now, in some expensive areas, the issue may simply be that buyers cannot stretch much further.

## Asking price is not selling price

This is important when reading housing headlines.

An asking price is what a seller advertises.

A selling price is what a buyer actually pays.

The two can diverge significantly.

Daft says the typical gap between initial listing price and final transaction price narrowed to:

**2.6% nationally**

from:

**6.8% a year earlier**.

That is one of the clearest signs that bidding pressure has eased.

Take a property listed at:

**€400,000**

A 6.8% premium would imply a final sale price around:

**€427,200**

A 2.6% premium implies:

**€410,400**

That is a difference of:

**€16,800**

between the two bidding environments.

This does not mean every house now sells 2.6% over asking.

But it shows why a less frantic market can matter even when headline prices have not fallen.

## Official CSO prices are still rising

There is another reason not to overstate the cooling.

The Central Statistics Office's latest Residential Property Price Index showed national residential prices were still **5.5% higher year-on-year in July 2026**.

Within that:

- Dublin prices were up **4.4%**;
- prices outside Dublin were up **6.4%**.

Those figures are not inconsistent with the Daft report.

They measure different things and different periods.

The CSO index is based on completed transactions filed with Revenue. Daft's latest report includes asking prices and its own matched transaction analysis through September.

The direction of travel is therefore more useful than expecting every dataset to show the same percentage.

## Why buyers are becoming more price-sensitive

Several pressures are colliding.

### House prices are already very high

Daft says national asking prices are now around **44% above pre-Covid levels**.

Even if inflation slows, the absolute price level remains difficult.

A market can become "cooler" without becoming "cheap".

A €450,000 property with a 10% deposit requires a mortgage of:

**€405,000**

### Cost-of-living pressure reduces real affordability

Mortgage approval is only one part of the picture.

Households still need to pay for:

- childcare;
- energy;
- groceries;
- transport;
- insurance;
- maintenance;
- and other monthly costs.

That means a buyer can technically qualify for a mortgage while deciding the repayment is simply too uncomfortable.

### Deposits remain a major hurdle

A €450,000 home with a 10% deposit requires:

**€45,000**

before stamp duty, legal fees, survey, valuation, moving costs or furnishing.

## There is still plenty of mortgage demand

Cooling prices do not mean buyers have disappeared.

Banking and Payments Federation Ireland reported that August was the **strongest month for mortgage approvals in 15 years**.

More than **5,000 mortgages** were approved, worth almost **€1.7 billion**.

First-time buyers accounted for almost **60% of approvals**.

So the current picture is not:

**nobody wants to buy houses.**

It is closer to:

**buyers still want homes, but affordability is limiting how far they can chase prices.**

## What does this mean for a first-time buyer?

Potentially, slightly better negotiating conditions.

Not necessarily cheaper housing.

A cooling market may mean:

- fewer extreme bidding wars;
- more time to evaluate a property;
- more chance of a seller accepting below an ambitious asking price;
- and more second-hand choice than a year ago.

But the biggest constraints remain:

- deposit;
- income;

## Is this the start of a house-price fall?

The evidence does not support making that conclusion yet.

There are clear signs of cooling:

- slower asking-price growth;
- more stock;
- smaller bidding premiums;
- and softer demand in Dublin.

But there are also powerful counterweights:

- mortgage approvals remain strong;
- total stock remains far below pre-pandemic levels;
- prices outside Dublin are still rising faster;
- and official CSO transaction prices remain higher year-on-year.

The most defensible interpretation is:

**Ireland's housing market is slowing, but it is not uniformly falling.**

## The bigger story may be an affordability ceiling

For years, the Irish housing story was mostly about supply.

Too many buyers were chasing too few homes.

That problem remains.

But the latest data suggests another constraint is becoming increasingly important:

**buyers may simply be reaching the maximum they can afford.**

That can cool a market even while supply remains historically tight.

If that continues, the next phase may look different from the last:

- slower price growth;
- longer selling times;
- greater regional divergence;
- less dramatic bidding;
- and much more sensitivity to interest rates and household costs.

For buyers, that is better than an accelerating market.

But it is not the same thing as affordability being fixed.

The average asking price remains close to **€445,000**.

That is the number still defining the challenge.
