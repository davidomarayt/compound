**Some 57% of principal-dwelling-home mortgage borrowers at Irish retail banks are exposed to near-term interest-rate changes, according to the Central Bank's latest Financial Stability Review. In its more severe scenario, one in five of that exposed group would pay over €200 more a month.**

The [Financial Stability Review 2026:II](https://www.centralbank.ie/docs/default-source/publications/financial-stability-review/financial-stability/financial-stability-review-2026-ii.pdf), published on 19 November, covers borrowers on standard variable rates, tracker mortgages or fixed rates expiring within the next year.

The figures are **stress scenarios, not forecasts**. The Central Bank has not announced that mortgage rates will rise by one or two percentage points. The analysis asks what could happen if prevailing market rates were shocked by that amount and the change passed through in full.

## What the Central Bank model found

| Increase in monthly instalment | 1 percentage-point shock | 2 percentage-point shock |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| €50 or less | 52% | 30% |
| €51–€100 | 26% | 21% |
| €101–€200 | 14% | 28% |
| More than €200 | Just under 7% | 20% |

The percentages apply to the **57% exposed group**, not to every Irish mortgage borrower. Rounding in the Central Bank chart means the displayed bands do not always total exactly 100%.

The report also says fixed-rate borrowers can have very different outcomes. Some people who fixed in recent years could see repayments fall when they refix, while borrowers who took out a fixed rate before 2022 may face a larger increase.

## A worked €250,000 example

For a simple repayment mortgage with €250,000 outstanding and 20 years remaining:

| Illustrative rate | Monthly repayment | Change from 3.5% |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 3.5% | €1,449.90 | — |
| 4.5% | €1,581.62 | +€131.72 |
| 5.5% | €1,719.72 | +€269.82 |

The calculation uses the standard amortising-loan formula and assumes the rate remains constant for the full 20 years, with no fees, overpayments or term change. It is an illustration, not the Central Bank's portfolio model.

The example shows why loan balance and remaining term matter. A percentage-point change is not a fixed euro amount. Use the [mortgage switch and rate-change calculator](/mortgage-switch-calculator/) to enter your own balance, rate, term, switching costs and cashback.

## How the stress scenario was constructed

The Central Bank assumed full pass-through of the modelled rate shock. For fixed-rate mortgages expiring within a year, it applied a historical probability that the borrower would refix.

That matters for interpretation:

- the scenario does not say every exposed borrower moves immediately to a new rate;
- it does not predict which mortgage products lenders will offer;
- it does not account for every household action, such as overpaying, extending a term or switching; and
- it describes retail-bank mortgage portfolios rather than every home loan in the State.

The review's broader assessment is not that Irish households are already in a general mortgage crisis. It says mortgage arrears have continued to decline and that household debt-service capacity has remained healthy overall. The stress exercise is designed to identify vulnerability before it becomes distress.

## What a fixed-rate borrower should check now

If your fixed rate ends in the next 12 months, gather the following before comparing offers:

1. current outstanding balance;
2. exact fixed-rate end date;
3. remaining mortgage term;
4. lender's revert rate and available refix options;
5. break fee, if any, for leaving early;
6. legal, valuation and administration costs of switching; and
7. any cashback condition that could distort the headline saving.

Ask your lender for the options in writing. A lower monthly payment achieved only by extending the term can increase total interest, so compare both the instalment and lifetime cost.

## Build the repayment change into the household budget

A €200 monthly increase is €2,400 a year. Before a rate expires, test at least three cases: the current payment, a one-point increase and a two-point increase. Put the difference beside childcare, commuting, energy, insurance and other fixed costs.

If the higher case is uncomfortable, the earlier options are usually wider: building a cash buffer, reducing other debt, seeking switching quotes or asking the existing lender about alternatives. Do not cancel insurance or miss a mortgage payment to create short-term room without first getting appropriate advice.

If affordability is already a concern, contact the lender promptly; the stress scenario is most useful as an early-warning exercise, not as a prediction to wait for.
