**Zoom is no longer mainly a pandemic-era video-meeting story. Its enterprise business is growing, large customers are spending more and contracted backlog is expanding. The harder question is whether management can compound that progress while returning an increasing amount of cash.**

For the quarter ended 31 October 2026, Zoom reported **$1.291 billion of revenue, up 4.9%**. Enterprise revenue grew **8.6% to $805.4 million**, while online revenue slipped **0.6% to $485.3 million**. Enterprise therefore supplied about **62.4% of quarterly revenue**, a Compound calculation, compared with 60% on the rounded mix shown a year earlier.

That shift is constructive because enterprise contracts can bundle meetings, phone, contact-centre and AI products across larger organisations. It also makes sales execution and product breadth more important. The investment question is whether Zoom’s enterprise momentum is strong enough to offset a mature self-service business and fund both innovation and shareholder distributions.

Compound does not currently maintain a dedicated Zoom stock profile, so this analysis does not invent one.

## Enterprise quality is improving, not just its share

The number of customers contributing more than **$100,000 of trailing-12-month revenue rose 8.5% to 4,732**. Those customers generated 34% of revenue, up from 32%. Enterprise net-dollar expansion reached **100%**, compared with 98% a year earlier. A 100% rate means the existing enterprise cohort collectively replaced churn and contraction with expansion; it does not mean every customer renewed or upgraded.

Remaining performance obligations, or contracted revenue not yet recognised, increased **13% to $4.534 billion**. The portion expected beyond the next 12 months grew 23%, faster than the 6% increase in current obligations. That supports revenue visibility, but it is not the same as cash received or a guaranteed profit margin. Contract timing, cancellations permitted by terms and implementation costs still matter.

The online business remains the counterweight. Monthly churn was **2.8%**, up from 2.7%, and online revenue declined slightly. Zoom can manage this segment for efficiency, but a shrinking self-service funnel could reduce the pool from which small customers become larger accounts. The strongest version of the thesis needs enterprise gains without a worsening online decline.

## High margins create room—but the cash trend deserves context

GAAP operating margin was **24.6%**, while Zoom’s non-GAAP margin was **41.6%**. The adjusted measure excludes share-based compensation, acquisition-related charges and other items, so it should not replace the reported result. The gap is a reminder that employee equity is an economic cost even when it does not use cash immediately.

Quarterly operating cash flow was **$587.9 million** and company-defined free cash flow was **$558.1 million**, a margin of about **43.2%**. That is excellent cash conversion. Yet free cash flow fell from $614.3 million a year earlier. For the first nine months, it declined to **$1.531 billion from $1.586 billion**, and the margin eased to 40.2% from 43.8%.

That does not invalidate the enterprise transition. Working-capital timing can move quarterly cash flow, and Zoom is still highly cash-generative. It does mean capital returns should be assessed against a cash base that is strong but not presently accelerating.

## Buybacks and the new dividend raise the capital-allocation bar

Zoom repurchased **$376 million of shares in the quarter**. Since the first quarter of fiscal 2025, it says it has bought 48.1 million shares for $3.8 billion, with roughly $900 million left under the authorisation. The company also initiated a **$0.25 quarterly dividend**.

Using management’s projected diluted share count of about 298 million, a full year at that rate would cost roughly **$298 million**. That is a Compound estimate, not company guidance; the actual cost will depend on eligible shares and future board declarations.

Zoom can afford both today. It reported **$7.3 billion of cash, cash equivalents and marketable securities**, excluding restricted cash, and guides to $1.78–$1.82 billion of fiscal-year free cash flow. But affordability is not the only test. A buyback creates value only when shares are retired at an attractive price and when it does more than offset equity compensation. A dividend creates a recurring expectation that competes with acquisitions, product development and flexibility during a downturn.

## What would confirm the thesis?

The case would strengthen if enterprise net-dollar expansion moves above 100%, large-customer growth remains durable and backlog converts into revenue without sacrificing GAAP margin. Stable or improving free-cash-flow margin after the dividend begins would show the business can fund returns and investment together.

It would weaken if online churn rises, enterprise growth slows toward the mature segment’s rate, or share repurchases fail to reduce diluted shares meaningfully. Investors should also watch whether new AI and contact-centre products generate incremental revenue rather than simply defend existing bundles.

**Zoom has credible evidence of an enterprise business replacing a mature online engine. Its cash pile makes the new distribution policy manageable, but the strategic test is stricter: enterprise growth and per-share cash flow need to improve after the cheques go out.** This analysis makes no price target.
