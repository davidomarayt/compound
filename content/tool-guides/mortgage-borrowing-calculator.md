## What this calculator is for

This tool combines the standard Central Bank loan-to-income ceiling with the deposit you have available. It answers a narrow but useful question: **what purchase price is suggested by those two constraints before lender affordability is considered?**

## How to read the result

Treat the result as an indicative ceiling, not approval. The calculator shows the income-based mortgage amount, the price your deposit could support, and the lower combined purchase-price estimate.

## Worked example

A first-time-buyer household earning €80,000 gross has a standard LTI ceiling of €320,000 at 4× income. With a €40,000 deposit, a €360,000 purchase is indicated if the income limit is the binding constraint. A lender can still approve less.

## Irish context

Under the current Central Bank mortgage measures, first-time buyers are generally subject to a 4× gross-income LTI limit and second/subsequent buyers to 3.5×. Principal-home buyers are generally limited to 90% LTV. Lenders can use limited allowances above the standard limits, but those are not entitlements.

## What can change the answer

Borrower age, employment stability, dependants, childcare, other debt, credit history, stress testing and lender policy can all reduce the amount actually offered. A higher deposit can help the LTV position but does not remove the income and affordability tests.

## Common mistakes to avoid

Do not treat gross income as spendable income. Mortgage repayments come from take-home pay. Do not assume an exception will be granted. And do not use all savings as a deposit without reserving money for stamp duty, legal costs, surveys and an emergency buffer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a bank lend less than this?
Yes. This is a regulatory/deposit estimate, not underwriting.

### Does it include Help to Buy?
No. Model scheme support separately.

### Does it include the First Home Scheme?
No. Shared equity changes the funding structure and has separate eligibility rules.

### Why might my lender quote differ?
Because lenders apply their own affordability and credit standards on top of the macroprudential rules.

## Why the maximum is not necessarily the target

A regulatory maximum answers "what might fit within the macroprudential rules?" It does not answer "what mortgage would leave the household comfortable?"

A household borrowing at the maximum can still have a strong budget if income is high and fixed costs are low. Another household at a lower multiple can feel stretched because of childcare, commuting, maintenance payments or irregular income.

A useful next step is to take the calculator's indicative mortgage and run it through three repayment scenarios: the expected rate, the expected rate plus 0.5 percentage points, and plus 1 percentage point. Then compare those payments with actual net income.

It is also worth distinguishing **approval capacity** from **purchase budget**. If the bank could lend €320,000 and you have €80,000 in cash, that does not automatically mean you should bid €400,000. Some of the cash may be needed for Stamp Duty, legal costs, furnishing and an emergency reserve.

Treat the maximum as an outer boundary. Your own target can rationally be lower.

## Method and limitations

The tool applies the standard LTI multiple for the selected buyer type and combines it with the deposit entered. It does not model lender exceptions, full affordability tests, scheme eligibility or purchase costs.
