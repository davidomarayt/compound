# Pension funds and fees in Ireland

A pension wrapper can be excellent and the investment inside it can still be poor.

The reverse is also true: a good diversified fund can be undermined by an expensive pension contract.

There are therefore two separate questions:

1. **What is the pension invested in?**
2. **What does the entire arrangement cost?**

## The fund is not the pension

A DC pension or PRSA can usually invest in one or more funds.

Common categories include:

- global equity funds;
- Irish or European equity funds;
- government and corporate bond funds;
- cash funds;
- property funds;
- multi-asset funds;
- target-date funds;
- and lifestyle strategies.

The word “pension” tells you almost nothing about the investment risk.

## Risk should match the retirement horizon

A person 30 years from retirement can usually tolerate a different level of short-term market volatility from someone planning to draw benefits next year.

The important risks include:

**Market risk** — the fund falls in value.

**Inflation risk** — the fund grows too slowly to preserve future purchasing power.

**Concentration risk** — too much depends on one company, sector or country.

**Sequence risk** — a large market fall close to retirement damages the amount available when withdrawals begin.

**Behaviour risk** — the member switches funds after a fall and locks in losses.

## Default investment strategies

Many pensions provide a default fund or strategy.

A default is not automatically basic or bad.

For a member who does not want to make repeated investment decisions, a well-designed diversified default can be useful.

The questions to ask are:

- what assets does it hold?
- what percentage is in equities?
- how does that change with age?
- what does it cost?
- does it assume you will buy an annuity, move to an ARF or draw benefits another way?

That final question matters for lifestyle strategies.

## Lifestyle strategies

A lifestyle strategy automatically changes the asset mix as retirement approaches.

Historically, many strategies moved heavily toward bonds and cash because members were expected to buy annuities at retirement.

But not everyone now follows that route.

Someone planning to transfer substantial funds to an ARF may continue to need long-term growth after retirement.

So ask:

> **What retirement outcome is this lifestyle strategy targeting?**

A de-risking strategy should fit the retirement plan, not simply the member’s age.

## Diversification inside the pension

A pension can own several funds and still be concentrated.

For example:

- US equity fund;
- technology fund;
- global growth fund;
- S&P 500 fund.

These can overlap heavily.

The number of fund names is less important than the underlying holdings.

For most retirement savers, the core questions are:

- company diversification;
- sector diversification;
- country diversification;
- bond/equity mix;
- currency exposure;
- and how risk changes over time.

## Pension fees

Pension charges can appear in several places.

<div class="pension-checklist">
<div><strong>Contribution charge</strong><p>A percentage taken before money is invested.</p></div>
<div><strong>Annual management charge</strong><p>A recurring percentage of the fund value.</p></div>
<div><strong>Fund fee</strong><p>Cost embedded within the underlying investment fund.</p></div>
<div><strong>Policy fee</strong><p>Some contracts include fixed or additional policy charges.</p></div>
<div><strong>Adviser charge</strong><p>Initial or ongoing remuneration for advice or service.</p></div>
<div><strong>Switching / transfer costs</strong><p>Check whether moving funds or providers creates charges.</p></div>
</div>

The total cost is what matters.

## Standard PRSA charge caps

The Pensions Authority states that a Standard PRSA can charge no more than:

<div class="pension-number-strip">
<div><span>Contribution charge</span><strong>5% max</strong></div>
<div><span>Annual fund charge</span><strong>1% max</strong></div>
</div>

These are maximums.

A provider can charge less.

A non-standard PRSA does not have the same statutory maximum charge limits.

That does not automatically make a non-standard PRSA expensive. It means you must read the actual charges.

## Why a 1% annual fee matters

Suppose €500 a month is invested for 30 years and earns a hypothetical 7% gross annual return before fees.

A recurring 1% annual charge can reduce the ending value by a six-figure amount compared with a 0% fee illustration.

That is not because 1% is large in one year.

It is because the fee is applied repeatedly to a growing balance, and the money removed can no longer compound.

Use the [Investment Fee Calculator](/investment-fee-calculator/) to compare fee levels over your own horizon.

## Contribution charges deserve special attention

Imagine contributing €500 a month into a pension with a 5% contribution charge.

€25 is removed before each €500 reaches the fund.

Over one year:

- gross contributions: €6,000;
- contribution charges: €300;
- amount invested before other fees: €5,700.

The annual management charge then applies to the fund as well.

This is why looking only at one headline annual management percentage can be misleading.

## Employer pension fees

Workplace schemes can benefit from institutional pricing.

But do not assume that automatically means low cost.

Ask for:

- member-borne annual management charge;
- underlying fund fees;
- administration charges;
- adviser or consultancy fees allocated to members;
- switching costs;
- and any reduction in allocation rate.

Your annual benefit statement or scheme documentation should help.

## Active versus passive pension funds

A passive fund generally tracks an index.

An active fund pays a manager to select investments.

The useful comparison is:

- diversification;
- investment mandate;
- risk;
- fee;
- consistency with the pension goal;
- and evidence that a higher fee is providing value.

Do not choose active or passive based on branding alone.

## The fund name can hide the risk

Labels such as:

- “balanced”;
- “growth”;
- “adventurous”;
- “cautious”

are not standardised risk measurements.

Read the asset allocation.

A “balanced” fund with 70% equities can behave very differently from another provider’s “balanced” fund with 45% equities.

## What to review annually

Once a year, record:

- current pension value;
- fund names;
- equity percentage;
- bond percentage;
- cash percentage;
- largest geographic exposures;
- annual management charge;
- underlying fund fee;
- contribution charge;
- adviser fee;
- projected retirement value.

If you cannot identify the total cost, ask the provider or adviser.

## When switching funds may be a mistake

Do not switch simply because one fund performed better over the last twelve months.

That is performance chasing.

A switch should have a structural reason:

- risk level is wrong;
- diversification is poor;
- fees are too high;
- retirement date changed;
- retirement option changed;
- or the existing fund no longer matches the plan.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a good pension management fee in Ireland?

There is no universal number because pension structures differ. Compare the total cost in euro and percentage terms, not just one quoted charge.

### Is a Standard PRSA always cheaper?

No. It has statutory charge caps, but actual fees vary. A provider can charge less than the cap, and non-standard products can have different pricing.

### Should I choose the pension fund with the highest historical return?

No. Historical return is only one piece of information. Risk, fees, diversification and suitability for your retirement horizon matter.

### Should pension risk automatically fall with age?

Often, but the correct glide path depends on what you plan to do with the money at retirement. An ARF strategy can differ from an annuity-purchase strategy.
