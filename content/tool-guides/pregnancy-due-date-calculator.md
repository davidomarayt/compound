## How pregnancy dating works

Pregnancy is conventionally dated from the **first day of the last menstrual period (LMP)** rather than from the date of conception. The standard LMP calculation assumes a pregnancy length of **280 days, or 40 weeks**.

That convention also assumes a regular 28-day menstrual cycle with ovulation around day 14. Real cycles and ovulation dates vary, and people may not recall the LMP exactly. That is why a calendar calculation is an estimate rather than a clinical measurement.

## Ultrasound dating is more accurate early in pregnancy

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, together with the American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine and the Society for Maternal–Fetal Medicine, states that **first-trimester ultrasound up to 13 weeks and 6 days is the most accurate method to establish or confirm gestational age**. Crown–rump-length dating in this period is typically accurate to about **±5–7 days**.

HSE says all pregnant women in Ireland are offered a dating scan and that this should be done **before 15 weeks**. The standard LMP method assumes a 28-day cycle with ovulation around day 14, so later or earlier ovulation, irregular cycles or uncertain menstrual dates can make the scan and LMP estimates differ.

If your maternity team has already assigned a due date from the clinical record or dating scan, enter that in the optional field. Compound then uses that date for the timeline rather than trying to override it with an LMP calculation. Once an EDD has been established, later scans should not be used casually to keep changing the date because later fetal size increasingly reflects growth as well as gestational age.

The HSE's own pregnancy calculator similarly describes its result as an estimate based on a 40-week pregnancy. ACOG notes that only about **1 in 20 women give birth on the exact estimated due date**, so the EDD should be understood as a clinical timeline anchor rather than a prediction of the exact birth day.

## What the conception date means

The conception date displayed here is **not a claim about the exact day fertilisation occurred**. It is simply the date 266 days before the due date — equivalent to approximately two weeks after LMP in the conventional 28-day model.

Ovulation varies, sperm can survive for several days, and pregnancy dating is deliberately based on gestational age rather than an attempt to identify a precise conception date.

## Timeline milestones

The tool shows calendar points that are useful for orientation:

- **12 weeks** — a simple gestational milestone, not a guaranteed appointment date.
- **18 to 22 weeks** — the HSE says the fetal anatomy/anomaly scan is usually offered in this window.
- **37 weeks** — the beginning of the early-term period in common obstetric terminology.
- **40 weeks** — the estimated due date.
- **42 weeks** — a calendar milestone two weeks after the due date.

Your actual appointments and management should follow your maternity team's plan, not a website timeline.

## Important limitations

This calculator cannot assess pregnancy viability, fetal growth, scan findings, bleeding, pain, reduced movements or any other clinical concern. A due date is also not a prediction of the exact day a baby will be born.

If the dates here differ from the dates given by your maternity team, use the dates in your clinical record and ask your midwife, GP or obstetric team if you are unsure.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the estimated conception date exact?

No. It is an approximate point derived from the due date. Ovulation and implantation vary, and intercourse that leads to conception can occur several days before ovulation.

### Why can my scan change the due date?

Early ultrasound estimates gestational age from fetal measurements and can be more reliable than menstrual dating when cycle length or LMP timing is uncertain.

### Does a due date mean the baby will arrive that day?

No. It is an estimated reference date. Normal birth timing spans a range around it.

### Should I replace the date my maternity team gave me?

No. If your clinician has assigned an estimated due date, use that clinical date for care planning.
