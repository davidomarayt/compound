**Two eligible adults could together contribute up to €24,000 a year to Ireland's proposed Personal Investment Accounts — but not to a joint PIA.** The Finance (No. 2) Bill 2026 describes an *Individual Investment Account* (IIA), the legal draft term for what is commonly called a PIA. Each account would belong to one person, with that person's own **€12,000 annual contribution limit** and **€50,000 threshold** for the proposed 1% annual account-value tax.

That makes couples' planning different from simply opening one shared investment account. The biggest questions are whose money funds each PIA, who actually owns the investments, whether the partners are married, and how the two accounts fit alongside pensions.

**Legislative status — checked 21 November 2026:** The account is **not yet available**. The rules described below come from the [Finance (No. 2) Bill 2026 as amended in Select Committee](https://data.oireachtas.ie/ie/oireachtas/bill/2026/98/eng/ver_a/b98a26d.pdf), not enacted law. **1 July 2027 is the Government's target launch**, conditional on legislation, commencement and provider readiness. Final rules and individual provider practices may differ.

## Can a married couple open a joint PIA in Ireland?

**No, not under the current proposal.** Section 747L of the amended Bill requires an account to be opened and held in the **sole name of a qualifying individual**. Section 747O also requires the holder's declaration to acknowledge that joint ownership is not permitted. This applies whether a couple is married, in a civil partnership or unmarried.

Instead, two qualifying partners could open **one account each**. Each person must independently meet the eligibility conditions: Irish tax residence, age **18 or over** and a Personal Public Service Number (PPSN). Only one IIA is permitted per individual at a time under the draft.

| Proposed account rule | One qualifying adult | Two qualifying partners with separate accounts |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Accounts permitted | One, in sole name | One each, not joint |
| Annual contributions | Up to €12,000 | Up to €24,000 combined, if each contributes €12,000 |
| Annual value threshold before proposed 1% account tax | €50,000 per account | Two separate €50,000 thresholds |
| Ownership and withdrawal decisions | Individual holder | Each holder controls their own account |
| Available to open now? | No | No |

**Important:** Two accounts do not create a jointly owned €100,000 tax-free investment pot. They are **two separate portfolios**, and the tax calculation is made for each one. The proposed annual charge uses the **average of daily closing values**, including cash, rather than the value on 31 December.

## How much could two PIAs save in annual account tax?

The proposed annual charge is **1% of the amount by which each account's average daily value exceeds €50,000**. It is not 1% on the entire balance, nor an income-tax charge on household earnings.

Consider three different ways an **€80,000 combined portfolio** might be distributed across **legitimately accumulated, independently owned accounts**. For clarity, assume each amount remains constant throughout a full calendar year, so average daily value equals the shown balance. There are no returns, withdrawals, provider fees, additional contributions or foreign taxes in these examples.

| Hypothetical accumulated holdings | Proposed tax on Account A | Proposed tax on Account B | Combined annual account tax |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| €80,000 in A; no PIA for B | €300 | — | **€300** |
| €60,000 in A; €20,000 in B | €100 | €0 | **€100** |
| €40,000 in A; €40,000 in B | €0 | €0 | **€0** |

For example, on an €80,000 average daily value in one account, **(€80,000 − €50,000) × 1% = €300**. With two accounts averaging €40,000 each, neither exceeds its own threshold.

At **€120,000 combined**, the same illustration produces **€700** for a single account averaging €120,000 versus **€200 combined** for two accounts averaging €60,000 each. That is a **€500 difference in this narrow annual-tax illustration**, not a guaranteed investment saving.

These comparisons **do not mean you can transfer €40,000 instantly from an existing PIA into your partner's PIA**, or deposit €80,000 in one year. The proposal imposes a contribution limit and individual beneficial ownership; the balances above would have to arise over time within the rules. Moving existing investments, making a gift or selling assets may create separate legal, tax and provider issues. They also ignore provider fees, investment choices, market movements and any final-law changes.

See [how daily-value PIA tax works](/pia/daily-valuation/) and use the [PIA calculator](/pia-calculator/) for a longer-term projection using your own assumptions.

## What if only one spouse earns the household income?

A non-earning spouse would not, merely for that reason, be barred from opening an account under the published draft. Eligibility is about **the individual holder**, not the presence of a salary or qualifying pension earnings.

For **married couples and civil partners**, Revenue says gifts and inheritances between spouses or civil partners are generally exempt from Capital Acquisitions Tax (CAT). That means a higher-earning partner could ordinarily **gift money to the other partner**, who could then use their own money to make contributions to their separately owned PIA, subject to the account rules.

However, **the recipient must genuinely own their account and its investments**. A gift is not a workaround allowing one person to beneficially own two PIAs. The Bill specifies individual beneficial ownership; providers may also restrict third-party funding or require payments to come from a bank account in the holder's name. Their precise deposit and verification rules must be checked at launch.

### Example: one income, two possible PIA contributors

Imagine one spouse has **€2,000 a month available for long-term investing** after household needs and pension decisions.

- **Single-account route:** The earning spouse contributes €1,000 a month to their own proposed PIA, reaching the draft €12,000 annual limit. The other €1,000 could go towards pensions, cash savings or ordinary investments, depending on the household's goals.
- **Two-account route:** The earning spouse contributes €1,000 a month to their own PIA and gifts €1,000 a month to the other spouse, who contributes it to their own eligible PIA. Over a full 12-month period, that is **€12,000 in each account**, assuming the accounts are open, the gift is genuine and provider funding rules allow the arrangement.

The second route is **a possible household structure, not a claim that all providers will support it**. PIA contributions also do **not** receive pension-style income-tax relief. Compare pension tax relief and employer contributions before committing all spare cash to PIAs.

## What if the couple is unmarried or living together?

**The PIA account rules are individual either way.** Two eligible cohabiting adults would still be able to have their own accounts under the proposal, but the **tax treatment of gifts between partners is different**.

Unlike spouses or civil partners, unmarried partners do not have a blanket CAT exemption just because they live together. Revenue's current [small gift exemption](https://www.revenue.ie/en/gains-gifts-and-inheritance/cat-exemptions/small-gift-exemption/index.aspx) is **€3,000 from any one giver to one recipient per calendar year**. Further gifts may fall under the relevant cumulative CAT threshold and aggregation rules. The current **Group C threshold is €22,000 from 7 October 2026**, not an annual allowance, and earlier taxable benefits in that group can use it up.

There are limited special reliefs for certain **qualified cohabitants** where a transfer arises from a qualifying court order. Merely living together does not create the same general gifting exemption as marriage or civil partnership.

**Practical rule:** If one unmarried partner intends to provide substantial money for the other's PIA, check the CAT consequences **before** gifting the money. The recipient's PIA contribution allowance does not automatically exempt the gift.

## Three household examples

### 1. Two earners starting from zero

Both adults are Irish tax resident, over 18 and have PPSNs. One can comfortably set aside €500 monthly, the other €300.

That would be **€6,000 plus €3,600 = €9,600 in first-year contributions** if both accounts were available for a full 12 months. They do not need to contribute the same amount or use identical investments. Each holds a separate portfolio and must stay within their own annual limit.

### 2. One earner supporting a spouse

The household has surplus cash after expenses. One spouse wants to invest while the other has no employment income.

If the recipient meets the proposed eligibility conditions, the couple could examine a **genuine cash gift** followed by contributions into the recipient's **own** account. Because pension tax relief usually relates to the relevant individual's qualifying earnings and circumstances, the household should separately compare any pension options, including those of the earning spouse.

**Next step:** [Compare PIA and pension outcomes](/pia/vs-pension/) and [check Revenue's pension tax-relief limits](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/tax-relief-limits.aspx).

### 3. A couple approaching retirement

Two partners already have retirement pensions and want a separate, accessible investment fund. Over several years, their individual PIAs could accumulate different balances.

Rather than assuming both must own the same funds or amounts, the household should look at **total equity exposure, accessible cash, fees and intended withdrawals**. If one balance passes €50,000, model that person's average daily value and the 1% charge. PIA money would not receive the tax relief available to qualifying pension contributions, and market losses remain possible even close to retirement.

## Should partners use the same PIA provider?

They need not assume they will. The draft focuses on individual accounts, while actual product ranges, charges and operational rules depend on the provider.

A useful household comparison should check **both providers'** all-in charges, not only the account tax: account and custody fees, fund charges, FX conversion, trading costs, minimums, available UCITS ETFs or shares, and whether a provider will support the desired funding arrangements. Choosing two different providers can increase complexity without necessarily improving diversification.

## What happens on separation, divorce or death?

A PIA would be **individually owned**, not an automatic 50/50 household asset. On separation or divorce, legal rights under family law and any court order need to be considered separately from whose name appears on the account. Do not assume a person can simply re-register half an account into their former partner's name.

On **death**, the amended Bill's proposed section **747Y** contains specific estate-administration and eventual account-closure rules. It does **not** describe the deceased person's PIA simply becoming a second PIA for the surviving partner. Contributions to the deceased person's account would cease, and the personal representatives would have duties during administration.

Revenue says **inheritances between spouses or civil partners are generally exempt from CAT**, but that exemption does not itself preserve the PIA tax wrapper or remove the need to follow the account and estate rules. For substantial portfolios, blended families, unmarried couples or a planned separation, legal and tax advice can be important.

## A couple's practical PIA checklist for 2027

1. **Check eligibility separately:** age 18+, Irish tax residence and PPSN for each intended account holder.
2. **Decide who will own each investment:** do not confuse a household goal with joint legal ownership.
3. **Check gift rules before funding a partner's account:** especially where you are not married or civil partners.
4. **Compare pension relief first:** PIA contributions are not pension contributions.
5. **Set a contribution plan:** up to €12,000 per person per year under the proposed rules, with no assumption that unused room transfers.
6. **Review actual provider charges and funding rules:** these may not be published until closer to launch.
7. **Model tax using each account's average daily value:** not a combined year-end balance.
8. **Prepare for ownership changes:** keep records of gifts, contributions and beneficiaries as appropriate.

## Couples and Irish PIAs: frequently asked questions

### Is there a joint Personal Investment Account in Ireland?

**Not under the Bill as amended in Select Committee in November 2026.** The draft requires a sole-name account. A normal joint investment account offered by a broker is **not** the proposed tax-framework PIA. See our separate [guide to Revolut's joint investment accounts](/news/revolut-joint-investment-accounts-ireland-2026/) for that distinction.

### Can husband and wife both have a PIA?

**Potentially yes**, one individually owned account each, if both satisfy the qualifying criteria once the scheme commences. Marriage does not make a single shared PIA or double one person's personal limit.

### Can couples invest €24,000 a year in Irish PIAs?

Under the **proposed** €12,000-per-person annual contribution limit, two qualifying partners could contribute a **combined maximum of €24,000** in a full year. That is a combined household figure across **two accounts**, not one person's allowance. Availability and final rules remain conditional.

### Could the non-working partner receive money to fund a PIA?

A **genuine gift from a spouse or civil partner** is ordinarily CAT-exempt under Revenue's rules, but the recipient must be eligible and must own their account. Whether a provider accepts the particular deposit method remains to be established.

### Are married and unmarried couples taxed the same?

The proposed PIA charge uses **individual account values** in either case. **Gifts between partners** are different: spouses and civil partners generally have CAT exemption, while unmarried partners need to consider the small gift exemption and CAT rules.

### Is the first €100,000 of our household PIA investments tax-free?

**Not as a single household allowance.** Each account has its own proposed €50,000 average-daily-value threshold. If one account averages €80,000 and the other €20,000, the combined proposed account tax is **€300**, even though the two balances total €100,000.

### Can I transfer shares or cash straight from my PIA to my spouse's?

Do **not** assume so. The draft contains rules for changing a person's **own account provider**, which is different from gifting money or securities to another individual. Account ownership, annual contribution limits, investment sales, taxes and provider rules need separate checks.
