**Investing in Ireland 101 · Chapter 8 of 9**

[← Investment fees](/wealth/investment-fees-ireland/) · [Course hub](/wealth/how-to-start-investing-in-ireland/) · [Next: Maintaining the portfolio →](/wealth/maintain-investment-portfolio-ireland/)

“How much should I invest every month?” sounds like a percentage question.

It is really a **cash-flow and goal question**.

There is no universal 10%, 15% or 20% rule that is correct for every Irish household.

A sustainable €250 monthly contribution can be more powerful than an aggressive €800 target that stops after three months.

## Start with the amount you can repeat

The contribution should survive:

- normal bills;
- annual expenses;
- emergency-fund needs;
- debt repayments;
- holidays;
- insurance;
- tax;
- and ordinary life.

If investing makes the rest of the household permanently fragile, the amount is probably too high.

## The three-bucket method

Before deciding the monthly investment number, divide surplus cash into:

### 1. Near-term cash

Money for:

- upcoming bills;
- annual insurance;
- holidays;
- planned purchases;
- tax liabilities.

### 2. Emergency reserve

Money for genuine shocks.

Use the [Emergency Fund Calculator](/emergency-fund-calculator/) to estimate a buffer from your own essential spending.

### 3. Long-term money

Cash that can stay invested through market falls and does not have a near-term job.

This is the pool from which the monthly investment contribution should come.

## What €100, €250 and €500 a month could become

Assume:

- contributions at the end of each month;
- 7% annual return;
- monthly compounding;
- no fees;
- no tax;
- no inflation;
- perfectly smooth returns.

This is a mathematical illustration, not a forecast.

| Monthly amount | 10 years | 20 years | 30 years |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| €100 | €17,308 | €52,093 | €121,997 |
| €250 | €43,271 | €130,232 | €304,993 |
| €500 | €86,542 | €260,463 | €609,985 |

The important observation is not the assumed 7%.

It is how much of the result comes from **time + repeated contributions**.

Use the [Compound Interest Calculator](/compound-interest-calculator/) to replace the assumptions with your own.

## Contributions matter more than return-chasing

Suppose you are investing €100 per month.

Increasing the contribution to €150 is a 50% increase in the amount going into the portfolio.

Finding an investment that reliably produces a 50% higher return is not a realistic planning assumption.

Beginners often spend too much time trying to optimise expected returns and too little time improving the savings rate they actually control.

## Should you invest a percentage of salary?

A percentage can be a useful budgeting shortcut.

But percentages ignore differences in:

- rent or mortgage;
- childcare;
- debt;
- commuting;
- family size;
- pension benefits;
- income stability;
- and near-term goals.

A better process is:

**income → essential spending → planned spending → resilience → long-term goals → investment contribution**

Then convert the resulting contribution into a percentage if that helps you track it.

## Pension contribution or taxable investment?

If some of the monthly amount is for retirement, compare pension contributions first.

Qualifying pension contributions can receive Income Tax relief within Revenue's age-related and earnings limits.

If an employer contributes too, the economics can change significantly.

Use:

- [Pension Tax Relief Calculator](/pension-tax-relief-calculator/)
- [Pension Projection Calculator](/pension-projection-calculator/)
- [Pension vs Investing in Ireland](/wealth/pension-vs-investing-ireland/)

for the retirement portion.

Money needed before retirement may belong elsewhere.

## Starting with €50 is not pointless

The first contribution has two jobs:

1. invest money;
2. build the process.

A small automated contribution teaches you:

- how the platform works;
- how statements look;
- how the investment moves;
- how you react to volatility;
- and how tax records need to be kept.

You can increase the amount as income rises.

## The contribution ladder

Instead of choosing one heroic target, create levels.

**Base contribution:** amount affordable in an ordinary month.

**Stretch contribution:** amount used in lower-spending months.

**Windfall contribution:** rule for bonuses, refunds or irregular income.

Example:

- €250 automatically every month;
- extra €100 when monthly cash flow allows;
- 25% of annual bonus directed to long-term investments.

The exact numbers are personal. The structure reduces the need to make a fresh decision every payday.

## Lump sum or monthly investing?

If you already have a lump sum that is genuinely long-term capital, the question is different from investing out of each salary.

Monthly investing is natural when money arrives monthly.

For an existing lump sum, investing immediately puts more money to work sooner, while phasing it in can reduce the emotional impact of investing just before a market decline.

The choice should reflect:

- risk tolerance;
- time horizon;
- tax;
- dealing costs;
- and whether delaying is actually part of a plan or simply fear.

## What if income is irregular?

For self-employed or variable-income households, a fixed monthly contribution may be too rigid.

Alternative:

- set a low automatic base;
- maintain a larger cash buffer;
- invest a percentage of income above a threshold;
- review quarterly.

Example:

> Invest €100 monthly automatically, plus 30% of quarterly cash surplus above the business and household reserve targets.

This adapts the contribution to actual cash flow.

## Increase contributions when income rises

One of the easiest long-term strategies is to capture part of every pay increase.

If take-home pay rises by €200 a month, you might direct a portion to investing before lifestyle spending expands to absorb all of it.

The exact split is personal.

The principle is to make contribution growth automatic.

## Do not forget fees at small contribution sizes

Fixed trading costs matter more when purchases are small.

If a transaction costs €2:

- €2 on €50 = 4%;
- €2 on €100 = 2%;
- €2 on €500 = 0.4%.

If the platform charges fixed commissions, less frequent purchases can sometimes reduce friction.

Read [Investment Fees in Ireland](/wealth/investment-fees-ireland/) before automating tiny frequent trades.

## A monthly investing template

On payday:

1. bills/standing orders;
2. planned-expense sinking funds;
3. emergency-fund top-up if below target;
4. pension contribution;
5. taxable long-term investment;
6. discretionary spending.

The order can be changed. The value is deciding it once.

## What happens when markets fall?

Do not automatically reduce contributions because the portfolio is down.

A long-term plan should already assume markets sometimes fall sharply.

Review the reason for investing:

- Has the goal changed?
- Has the time horizon shortened?
- Has income changed?
- Has the emergency reserve weakened?
- Has your portfolio become inappropriate?

If none has changed, a market headline alone may not require a contribution change.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is €100 a month enough to start investing?

Yes, if fees are proportionate and the money is genuinely long term. The contribution can grow later.

### What percentage of salary should I invest?

There is no universal percentage. Base it on goals, pension benefits, essential spending, debt, emergency cash and the amount you can sustain.

### Is €500 a month enough for retirement?

That depends on age, existing pension assets, retirement age, employer contributions, State pension assumptions, fees, tax and desired retirement income. Use a pension projection rather than a single rule.

### Should I invest monthly or save up and invest annually?

Consider fixed transaction costs, automation and behaviour. Monthly investing can match monthly income well; fewer larger transactions can reduce fixed dealing costs on some platforms.
