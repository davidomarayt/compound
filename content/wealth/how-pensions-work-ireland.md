# How pensions work in Ireland

A pension is not one product.

It is a set of rules around saving, investing and eventually turning money into retirement income.

In Ireland, your retirement income can come from several places at once:

- the **State Pension**, if you qualify;
- an **occupational pension** through an employer;
- a **PRSA**;
- a **Retirement Annuity Contract (RAC)**;
- **MyFutureFund** auto-enrolment;
- and other non-pension assets.

The first job is to understand which type you actually have.

## The pension system in one picture

<div class="pension-key-grid">
<div><span>STATE</span><strong>State Pension</strong><p>Public retirement income based on the relevant social insurance or means-tested rules.</p></div>
<div><span>WORKPLACE</span><strong>Occupational pension</strong><p>An employer-sponsored scheme. It may be defined benefit or defined contribution.</p></div>
<div><span>PERSONAL</span><strong>PRSA or RAC</strong><p>A personal pension arrangement used to build your own retirement fund.</p></div>
<div><span>AUTO-ENROLMENT</span><strong>MyFutureFund</strong><p>Ireland’s automatic retirement savings system for eligible employees without a qualifying payroll pension.</p></div>
</div>

## Occupational pensions

An occupational pension is established by an employer.

There are two broad types.

### Defined contribution

A **defined contribution (DC)** scheme builds an individual fund.

Your retirement outcome depends on:

- contributions from you;
- contributions from your employer;
- investment returns;
- fees;
- and what happens to the money at retirement.

There is no promise that a particular annual pension will be paid.

The fund value is the starting point for retirement benefits.

### Defined benefit

A **defined benefit (DB)** scheme works differently.

The scheme promises a retirement benefit using a formula, usually linked to service and earnings.

That shifts much of the investment and longevity risk away from the individual and onto the scheme.

DB schemes are therefore not directly comparable with a DC account showing a visible pot value.

If you have a DB pension, understand:

- the pension formula;
- your pensionable service;
- pensionable salary;
- normal retirement age;
- survivor benefits;
- inflation or revaluation rules;
- and what happens if you leave before retirement.

## PRSAs

A **Personal Retirement Savings Account** is an individual pension contract with an authorised provider.

The Pensions Authority describes PRSAs as flexible and portable. They can generally move with you from job to job.

A PRSA can be funded by you and, subject to the rules, by an employer.

There are two categories:

<div class="pension-compare-grid">
<div><strong>Standard PRSA</strong><p>Charges are capped by law. Investment options are restricted to pooled funds.</p><p>Maximum charges: 5% of contributions and 1% a year of fund value.</p></div>
<div><strong>Non-standard PRSA</strong><p>Charges are not subject to those same caps and investment choice can be broader.</p><p>The extra flexibility makes fee comparison especially important.</p></div>
</div>

A charge cap is a maximum, not a good price.

A Standard PRSA can still cost less than the cap, and a non-standard PRSA can sometimes be competitive. Compare the actual contract.

## Retirement Annuity Contracts

A **Retirement Annuity Contract**, often called a personal pension, is another Revenue-approved pension product.

RACs are particularly associated with:

- self-employed people; and
- people with earnings from non-pensionable employment.

Qualifying personal contributions can receive Income Tax relief subject to Revenue limits.

A PRSA is generally more portable and flexible across employment changes, which is one reason PRSAs are now prominent in Irish retirement saving.

## MyFutureFund

MyFutureFund began on **1 January 2026**.

It automatically enrols qualifying employees who are not already actively contributing to a qualifying pension arrangement through payroll.

The core 2026 eligibility rules include employees who are generally:

- aged 23 to 59;
- earning more than €20,000 a year; and
- not already contributing to a qualifying payroll pension for that employment.

The contribution structure differs from ordinary pension tax relief.

In 2026–2028, the scheduled contributions are:

<div class="pension-number-strip">
<div><span>Employee</span><strong>1.5%</strong></div>
<div><span>Employer</span><strong>1.5%</strong></div>
<div><span>State</span><strong>0.5%</strong></div>
<div><span>Total</span><strong>3.5%</strong></div>
</div>

The rates rise in later phases.

Read [MyFutureFund vs a Private Pension in Ireland](/wealth/myfuturefund-vs-private-pension-ireland/) for the full comparison.

## The State Pension is not the same as your private pension

A private pension builds additional retirement income.

The State Pension is a separate public benefit.

For retirement planning, the important question is not whether one replaces the other.

It is:

> **What income will all of my retirement sources produce together?**

A household may eventually receive income from:

- one or two State Pensions;
- occupational pensions;
- PRSAs;
- MyFutureFund;
- ARFs;
- rental or investment income;
- and cash savings.

Planning should look at the whole household.

## What does “tax approved” mean?

Irish pension arrangements can receive favourable tax treatment because they are designed specifically for retirement.

For qualifying personal pension contributions, Income Tax relief may apply within Revenue’s limits.

Inside pension arrangements, investment returns can generally accumulate without the annual personal taxation that would apply to many investments held outside a pension.

Retirement benefits are then subject to their own rules.

That is why a pension is best understood as a **tax and access wrapper around investments**, not as one particular investment.

## What is actually inside a pension?

A pension account may invest in:

- global equities;
- bonds;
- property funds;
- cash;
- multi-asset funds;
- target-date or lifestyle strategies;
- or other permitted assets.

The pension wrapper and the underlying investments are separate decisions.

A “pension” can therefore be conservative, aggressive or somewhere in between.

Chapter 5 covers pension funds and fees in detail.

## When can pensions normally be accessed?

The answer depends on the arrangement.

Revenue states that occupational pension schemes specify a normal retirement age between **60 and 70**, although some schemes can permit earlier retirement in defined circumstances.

PRSAs can normally be accessed from **age 60**.

MyFutureFund uses its own access rules linked to State Pension age.

Ill-health and other special provisions can apply.

Chapter 7 covers access and retirement options.

## The first pension questions to ask

If you already have a pension, find the answers to these:

1. What type of pension is it?
2. Is it DB or DC?
3. What does your employer contribute?
4. What do you contribute?
5. What are the total charges?
6. What is the fund invested in?
7. What is the normal retirement age?
8. What happens if you leave the employer?
9. What death benefits exist?
10. What are the projected benefits?

Do not judge a pension by the account balance alone.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a PRSA and a workplace pension?

A PRSA is an individual pension contract. A workplace occupational pension is established by an employer and governed by its scheme rules.

### Is a pension the same thing as an investment fund?

No. The pension is the wrapper. The money inside it is invested in one or more underlying funds or assets.

### Can I have more than one pension?

Yes. People commonly accumulate several arrangements across a career. Tax relief and retirement rules can look at pensions together in some circumstances.

### Is MyFutureFund a PRSA?

No. MyFutureFund is Ireland’s separate auto-enrolment retirement savings system.
