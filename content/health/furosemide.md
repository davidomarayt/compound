## At a glance

Furosemide is a loop diuretic used to reduce excess fluid in certain conditions. It changes how the kidney handles salt, which increases urine output. [HSE guide](https://www2.hse.ie/medicines/furosemide/)

## The Science Behind the Drug

### Salt transport in the kidney and monitoring fluid balance

In the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, the NKCC2 transporter reabsorbs sodium, potassium and chloride. Furosemide blocks that transporter, increasing sodium and water excretion. The change can also affect potassium and other electrolytes, which explains part of the need for monitoring. [DailyMed source](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=58d2a38d-75f8-dcb9-e063-6294a90a10d7)

## What the evidence measures

Urine output after a dose is an immediate effect. The benefit of long-term treatment is assessed in context through symptoms, weight trends, examination and blood tests. A change in weight can reflect fluid without revealing the cause by itself.

## A fluid review pairs observations

| What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| At home | weight and symptoms as agreed with your team |
| At the clinic | blood pressure, kidney function and electrolytes |


## Can more frequent urination tell me whether fluid control is adequate?

It tells you a short-term effect occurred. The overall response also depends on breathlessness, swelling, weight trends, blood pressure and what the clinical team finds.

## When to ask for help or a medication review

Ask how to respond to a stomach illness, dizziness or markedly reduced fluid intake. Discuss anti-inflammatory pain medicines and avoid changing the prescription on the basis of a single weight reading. [HSE patient guidance](https://www2.hse.ie/medicines/furosemide/)
