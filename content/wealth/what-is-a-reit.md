Buying an investment property usually means a large deposit, one location and responsibility for tenants, maintenance and financing. A **real estate investment trust, or REIT**, offers another route: buy shares in a business that owns, operates or finances income-producing property.

You could gain exposure to warehouses, apartments, shopping centres, hospitals, casinos or data centres without buying an entire building yourself. The attraction is a combination of potential dividend income and growth in the value of your investment.

**REITs can be good investments when their properties generate durable cash flows, their debt is manageable and the share price is sensible. A high dividend yield alone does not establish any of those things.**

This guide focuses on listed REIT shares, using US companies as examples. REIT regimes differ between countries. The examples are a starting point for research, not a ranked list of shares to buy.

## How does a REIT work?

An equity REIT raises money from shareholders and lenders, buys or develops properties, and earns rental income. After operating expenses, interest and other costs, it can distribute income to shareholders and invest in its portfolio.

As a shareholder, you own part of the company. You do not hold a direct legal slice of each building, decide individual lease terms or collect rent from tenants yourself.

The REIT structure comes with specific tax and operating requirements. For US REITs, the distribution requirement is generally at least **90% of REIT taxable income**, excluding net capital gains and subject to statutory adjustments. That is **not 90% of rent, revenue, cash flow or the value of your investment**. It also does not promise a particular dividend yield.

The rule helps explain the sector's income appeal. It also means retained earnings alone may not fund every expansion: borrowing, asset sales and new shares can matter. The [IRS rules](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1120rei) describe the US requirements; do not apply the same percentage automatically to other countries.

## Why REITs can be attractive investments

### 1. Access to property without buying a whole building

Listed shares allow smaller investments and partial sales. That makes it easier to build exposure gradually than to buy a rental house with a mortgage.

A REIT may also own properties an individual could never realistically buy alone, such as a distribution campus or a portfolio of specialised facilities. Professional management handles leasing and operations, although its costs still come out of the business.

### 2. Income that can be spent or reinvested

Dividends can provide a cash income stream. Alternatively, reinvesting them buys more shares and can increase future income if the dividend is maintained. Reinvestment does not remove taxes or protect against falling prices.

Some companies pay monthly and others quarterly. Payment frequency is less important than sustainability: twelve small payments are not inherently better than four larger ones with the same annual total.

### 3. Growth alongside the dividend

A REIT can grow through higher rents, better occupancy, redevelopment and acquisitions. The useful question is whether that growth improves the outcome **per share** after financing costs.

If a company doubles its property portfolio but also doubles its share count, existing shareholders have not automatically doubled their claim on earnings. Look for growth in relevant per-share measures, not simply a larger corporate footprint.

### 4. Different sources of property demand

Logistics space, residential accommodation and data centres serve different customers. A property allocation can therefore expose a portfolio to economic drivers beyond the businesses already held.

However, owning four data-centre REITs is still a concentrated sector bet. Broad stock funds may already hold REITs, and an Irish homeowner may already have substantial household wealth tied to property. Consider the whole portfolio before adding more. Our [diversification guide](/wealth/diversification-portfolio-ireland/) explains this trade-off.

### 5. Potential rental growth during inflation

Some leases have contractual increases or inflation-linked clauses. Others reset when tenants renew. Those mechanisms can support revenue as prices rise.

The protection is incomplete: increases may be capped, leases may be fixed for years and tenants must be able to pay. Maintenance, construction and financing costs can rise too. Treat inflation resilience as a property-and-contract question, rather than a guarantee attached to the REIT label.

## Dividend yield is only one part of your return

Consider an illustrative investment of **€10,000** paying **€500 in dividends** over a year. Ignore tax, fees and exchange rates for this example.

| End-of-year share value | Dividends received | Combined value | Total return |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| €10,600 | €500 | €11,100 | +11% |
| €10,000 | €500 | €10,500 | +5% |
| €8,500 | €500 | €9,000 | −10% |

The same 5% starting income yield accompanies very different outcomes. If the dividend is cut, the income figure changes as well.

A rising quoted yield can reflect a falling share price. A €3 annual dividend on a €60 share is 5%; on a €30 share it is 10%, assuming the payment survives. The higher yield may be warning you about deteriorating prospects.

## Equity REITs, mortgage REITs and REIT funds

| Structure | Main exposure | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equity REIT | Property ownership and operations | Tenants, rents, occupancy, property spending and debt |
| Mortgage REIT | Property loans or mortgage-backed securities | Credit losses, leverage, funding costs, interest-rate risk and hedging |
| REIT fund or ETF | A basket of property securities | Holdings, concentration, fees, legal structure and tax treatment |

A mortgage REIT can have a very different risk profile from a warehouse landlord. Similarly, a listed share's liquidity should not be assumed for a private or non-traded REIT, where exits may be restricted. The [SEC's REIT bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins-65) explains those distinctions.

For fund structures, read [shares, ETFs and funds in Ireland](/wealth/shares-etfs-funds-ireland/). Buying an ETF that holds REITs is not the same legal investment as owning the underlying company shares directly.

## How to read a REIT's earnings

Start with the financial statements, then examine the company's supplemental property and cash-flow measures. Net income remains important, but a conventional price-to-earnings ratio does not tell the whole story.

### FFO: funds from operations

Accounting depreciation reduces reported profit for property assets. **FFO** adjusts net income for real-estate depreciation and amortisation and specified items such as gains or losses on property sales, with further adjustments under Nareit's definition.

FFO is a supplemental operating-performance measure. It is not the same as cash in the bank or cash freely available for dividends. Buildings still require real spending, even when an accounting depreciation charge is added back.

### AFFO: adjusted funds from operations

**AFFO** often adjusts FFO for recurring capital spending and items such as straight-line rent accounting. It can help assess dividend capacity, but there is no universal definition: companies calculate it differently.

Read the reconciliation to the accounts, check what management excludes and use a consistent basis across periods. Do not compare two companies' AFFO multiples as though the definitions are necessarily identical.

### A simple dividend-coverage example

Suppose a hypothetical REIT reports annual AFFO of **€4 per share** and pays **€3 per share** in dividends. Its dividend-to-AFFO payout ratio is **75%**.

If AFFO falls to €3.20 while the dividend remains €3, the ratio becomes **93.75%**. There is much less headroom. Neither ratio alone proves that a dividend is safe: debt repayments, liquidity, capital spending and the quality of the AFFO adjustments still matter.

At a €60 share price, €4 of annual AFFO implies **15 times AFFO**. That multiple only becomes useful when considered alongside growth, property quality, leverage and risk. Cheap-looking shares can remain poor investments if their earning power is shrinking.

## The main risks to understand

**Debt and refinancing.** A property can remain occupied while higher interest costs reduce the cash left for shareholders. Check fixed versus floating borrowing, maturity dates, available liquidity and interest coverage. Long-dated fixed debt can delay the effect of higher rates; it does not eliminate refinancing risk forever.

**Valuation and bond yields.** Investors may demand a higher return from property shares when safer income alternatives become more attractive. A business can perform reasonably while its share-price multiple falls. Falling rates are not an automatic buy signal either, particularly if they accompany weaker tenant demand.

**Tenants and property spending.** Vacant space can mean lost rent plus refurbishment and leasing costs. A long lease is only as valuable as the tenant's ability to honour it and the economics of replacing that tenant.

**Dilution and management decisions.** Issuing shares can finance sensible acquisitions, but an increase in total revenue does not prove that a deal benefits each existing share. Watch per-share outcomes and incentives.

**Currency.** For an Irish investor, a dollar dividend and US share price translate into a changing euro value. An unchanged dollar investment can lose value in euro terms if the dollar weakens.

These risks make listed REIT shares unsuitable substitutes for cash that must be available at a known value on a near-term date. The ability to sell quickly does not guarantee an acceptable sale price.

## Irish tax: compare the return you keep

For an Irish-resident individual investing personally, dividends can create Irish tax liabilities even when foreign tax has already been withheld. Revenue's general guidance explains taxation of foreign dividends and possible double-taxation relief. Income Tax, USC and PRSI can be relevant, depending on your circumstances.

Check the exact REIT, distribution type, domicile and account. A withholding deduction is not necessarily your final tax bill, and a headline dividend yield is not a net return. Reinvesting a taxable dividend does not by itself avoid tax.

Disposals of direct company shares generally fall within capital-gains rules, but a REIT fund or ETF can have different treatment. Do not assume that a fund avoids deemed-disposal rules because its holdings are property companies. Tax residence, domicile and wrappers can also change the position.

## A checklist before investing

1. **Understand the assets:** identify the properties, locations and tenants that drive returns.
2. **Read the earnings trend:** compare relevant per-share figures across comparable periods.
3. **Check dividend coverage:** inspect the cash-flow reconciliation and spending requirements.
4. **Map the debt:** identify what must be refinanced and at what potential cost.
5. **Assess the price:** consider valuation alongside realistic growth, rather than yield alone.
6. **Check portfolio fit:** account for existing property and sector exposure.
7. **Calculate costs and tax:** include dealing fees, currency conversion and your likely net income.

A compelling REIT investment combines a sound property business with a price that leaves room for an acceptable return. Start with the earnings and balance sheet, then decide whether the dividend is part of a sustainable investment case.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are REIT dividends guaranteed?

No. Distribution rules do not guarantee a fixed payment to shareholders. Profitability, funding needs and financial stress can affect dividends.

### Are REITs better than buying a rental property?

They offer smaller entry amounts, professional management and easier partial sales. Direct ownership offers more control but greater operational responsibility and concentration. Compare costs, financing, tax and time commitment rather than assuming one always wins.

### Is a REIT the same as a bond?

No. A REIT share is equity with a variable dividend and no promise to return your purchase price at maturity. Property ownership does not make that equity capital-protected.

### What matters more: yield or growth?

Both contribute to total return, alongside changes in valuation. A modest yield with sustainable per-share growth may outperform a high yield that is later cut, but neither outcome is assured.
