If a planning application near you could affect your home, road, neighbourhood or local environment, you do not have to wait until the council makes its decision.

The planning process gives the public a formal opportunity to make a **submission or observation**.

People often call this an “objection”, but a submission can also support an application or suggest changes.

<div class="cs-intro">
<p class="cs-kicker">Quick answer</p>
<p>Anyone can generally make a written submission or observation on a normal planning application within <strong>five weeks from the date the planning authority receives the application</strong>.</p>
<p>The current fee is generally <strong>€20</strong>.</p>
<p>If you may want to make a third-party appeal later, participating validly at the local-authority stage is especially important.</p>
</div>

## You do not need to live next door

Local Government Ireland states that anyone can make a written submission or observation on a planning application within the statutory period.

You might participate because you are:

- an immediate neighbour;
- a local resident;
- a community organisation;
- an environmental group;
- a business;
- a landowner;
- or simply someone with a relevant planning interest.

The planning authority considers valid planning submissions as part of the application assessment.

## The five-week deadline

The ordinary deadline is **five weeks beginning on the date the planning authority receives the planning application**.

This is not five weeks from:

- when you noticed the site sign;
- when a neighbour told you;
- or when you first viewed the file.

Check the official application receipt date.

Galway City Council's current guidance states that late submissions cannot be considered and the submission and fee are returned.

So timing matters.

## The current €20 fee

The standard fee for a planning-authority submission or observation is **€20**.

Check the relevant authority's payment method before submitting.

Do not assume an unpaid email counts as a valid planning submission.

## “Objection” and “submission” are not exactly the same idea

People commonly say:

> “I'm objecting to the planning.”

The statutory process is broader.

A submission can:

- object;
- support;
- raise concerns;
- suggest conditions;
- request design changes;
- or make neutral planning observations.

That can be useful when you do not oppose the development in principle but want a specific issue addressed.

For example:

> “I support an extension in principle, but the proposed first-floor window would directly overlook our rear bedroom.”

That is different from opposing the entire application.

## What should a submission contain?

Current local-authority guidance generally requires identifying information and enough detail to link the submission to the application.

A useful submission should include:

- your name;
- address;
- correspondence address if different;
- planning application reference;
- site/development address;
- clear planning grounds;
- supporting material where useful;
- and the required fee.

Keep a copy and proof of submission.

If you later appeal, your planning-authority acknowledgement is important.

## Use planning grounds

The strongest submissions deal with matters the planning authority can actually assess.

Examples include:

- overlooking;
- loss of privacy;
- residential amenity;
- traffic safety;
- road access;
- parking;
- scale and massing;
- overdevelopment;
- building height;
- development-plan policy;
- zoning;
- drainage;
- flood risk;
- wastewater;
- ecology;
- protected structures;
- Architectural Conservation Areas;
- landscape;
- noise associated with the proposed planning use;
- and compatibility with surrounding land use.

A planning submission is not the right forum for every private dispute.

## What are weak planning grounds?

Arguments can carry less weight where they are primarily about:

- dislike of the applicant;
- impact on private property value without a planning basis;
- ownership disputes;
- private covenants;
- competition between businesses;
- personal rumours;
- or matters outside the planning authority's jurisdiction.

A private legal issue can still be important — but it may need to be dealt with separately through solicitors or another process.

## Be specific about the impact

Compare these statements.

### Weak

> “The extension is too big and will ruin the area.”

### Stronger

> “The proposed two-storey rear extension extends materially beyond the established rear building line and places a first-floor bedroom window approximately opposite our rear private amenity space. We ask the authority to assess overlooking and the impact on residential amenity against the relevant development-plan standards.”

The second statement gives the planner something concrete to assess.

## Read the development plan

A good submission connects the concern to planning policy.

Search the relevant development plan for policies on:

- extensions;
- residential amenity;
- parking;
- rural housing;
- design;
- protected structures;
- landscape;
- flood zones;
- transport;
- or whatever is actually relevant.

Do not cite dozens of policies just to make the submission longer.

Use the provisions that genuinely apply.

## Look at the drawings before objecting

Site notices and newspaper notices contain only a summary.

The planning file can contain:

- site plan;
- floor plans;
- elevations;
- sections;
- planning report;
- engineering information;
- traffic reports;
- ecological material;
- and other supporting documents.

Review the actual proposal before forming your planning grounds.

Many concerns disappear or become more precise once dimensions and levels are clear.

## Does the council have to agree with objections?

No.

Planning decisions are not votes.

Ten objections do not automatically defeat one application.

The authority assesses:

- the proposal;
- planning policy;
- site conditions;
- technical material;
- and the planning issues raised in valid submissions.

A single strong planning issue can matter more than a large number of repetitive comments.

## Can the applicant see my objection?

Planning applications and associated public-participation material form part of the planning process and are generally available for inspection.

Write on the assumption that your submission is part of a public planning file.

Avoid including unnecessary personal, medical or confidential information.

Keep the submission focused on planning matters.

## What if the applicant changes the plans?

The planning authority may request further information.

Sometimes that produces revised drawings.

Where further information is considered significant, additional public-notice requirements can arise.

Watch the official planning file rather than assuming the original drawings remain the final proposal.

## Will I be told the council's decision?

Local Government Ireland states that after a decision is made, the planning authority must inform a person who made a valid submission or observation.

Keep that correspondence.

If you are considering an appeal, the deadline starts from the planning authority's decision date, not from when you happen to read the notification.

## Why the original submission matters for appeal rights

A normal third-party appellant generally needs to have made a valid submission or observation during the planning-authority stage.

That means if you think you might want to appeal a grant, the first five weeks are crucial.

Missing the original submission window can leave only limited leave-to-appeal routes.

Read [Planning Appeals Ireland](/wealth/planning-appeal-ireland/).

## Third-party appeal

If the council grants permission and you remain dissatisfied, a qualifying third party can generally appeal to An Coimisiún Pleanála within **four weeks beginning on the council's decision date**.

The current ordinary third-party appeal fee is generally €220.

You will normally need your acknowledgement from the original council submission.

## Observation after somebody else appeals

This is a different stage.

If a valid appeal has already been made, a person who is not already a participant may be able to make an **observation on the appeal**.

An Coimisiún Pleanála's current fee is **€50**.

For a standard observation following an appeal, the normal time limit is four weeks from the date of the appeal or last appeal.

An appeal observation is not the same as the original €20 local-authority submission.

## Can I object anonymously?

A valid planning submission requires your identifying details.

Do not rely on anonymous online comments, social-media posts or petitions as substitutes for the statutory process.

If you want your views formally considered, use the planning-authority procedure.

## Should neighbours talk before objecting?

Sometimes.

A direct conversation can resolve practical matters such as:

- window position;
- boundary treatment;
- construction access;
- hedge retention;
- or minor design changes.

But talking to the applicant does not stop the statutory clock.

If the five-week deadline is approaching and the planning issue matters, do not miss your formal submission deadline while waiting for an informal agreement.

## What if the problem is construction rather than the planning design?

Planning permission does not regulate every future annoyance.

Concerns about:

- working hours;
- access;
- dust;
- noise;
- road obstruction;
- damage;
- building-control compliance;

may be addressed through conditions, other legislation or separate enforcement processes.

Distinguish the **proposed planning development** from how construction might later be managed.

## Can you withdraw an objection?

You can communicate with the planning authority if circumstances change, but the original submission forms part of the planning record.

If the applicant amends the design and resolves your concern, you can state that clearly where the process permits.

Do not make threats or seek payment to withdraw planning opposition.

Keep all engagement legitimate and planning-focused.

## Common mistakes

### Waiting until after five weeks

Late local-authority submissions are not considered.

### Writing only “I object”

Give actual planning grounds.

### Focusing on property value

Explain the underlying planning impact instead.

### Missing the application file

Read the drawings and reports.

### Assuming lots of objections equals refusal

Planning is not a referendum.

### Forgetting the acknowledgement

You may need it for a third-party appeal.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long do I have to object to planning permission?

Normally five weeks from the date the planning authority receives the application.

### How much does a planning objection cost?

The standard local-authority submission/observation fee is currently €20.

### Can anyone make a planning objection?

Generally, anyone can make a written submission or observation within the statutory period and requirements.

### Does an objection stop planning permission?

No. The authority considers the planning grounds but makes its own decision.

### Can I appeal if I did not object first?

Normal third-party appeal rights generally depend on participating in the original application. Limited leave-to-appeal provisions exist but are not a general substitute.

### Is my planning submission public?

Treat it as part of the public planning process and avoid unnecessary private information.

## The bottom line

A good planning submission is:

**on time + valid + specific + based on planning grounds.**

Do not try to win by volume.

Help the planner understand:

1. what the impact is;
2. where it appears on the drawings;
3. which planning principle or policy is relevant;
4. what outcome would resolve the issue.

That is far more useful than a generic objection.
