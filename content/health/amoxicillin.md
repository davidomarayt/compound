**Amoxicillin is a prescription antibiotic in the penicillin family.** It treats some bacterial infections. A prescriber decides if it suits the likely infection, based on factors including where the infection is and any history of allergy. Amoxicillin does not treat viral colds or flu. [HSE antibiotics guide](https://www2.hse.ie/medicines/antibiotics/) · [HPRA patient leaflet](https://assets.hpra.ie/products/Human/15319/bafe216b-4eb4-4d1c-88a1-9a724fe5072d.pdf)

## The Science Behind the Drug

Many bacteria use a mesh called **peptidoglycan** to give their cell wall strength. Enzymes called **penicillin-binding proteins** help join the strands of that mesh as the bacterium grows. Amoxicillin binds to some of these enzymes and blocks their work. The weakened wall can break, killing susceptible bacteria. [HPRA prescribing information, section 5.1](https://assets.hpra.ie/products/Human/16705/Licence_PA1418-013-001_25012024163956.pdf)

This target explains why amoxicillin can work on certain bacteria. Viruses have no peptidoglycan cell wall. Antibiotic resistance can also prevent amoxicillin from working against some bacterial infections. [HPRA prescribing information, section 5.1](https://assets.hpra.ie/products/Human/16705/Licence_PA1418-013-001_25012024163956.pdf) · [HSE antibiotics guide](https://www2.hse.ie/medicines/antibiotics/)

## What should you tell your prescriber about penicillin allergy?

Tell your prescriber about any suspected reaction to a penicillin or another antibiotic before you take amoxicillin. If you can, describe the medicine, what happened and how quickly the symptoms began. The Irish leaflet says to avoid amoxicillin if you are allergic to it or to penicillin, and to check with a pharmacist or doctor if you have a history of an allergic reaction to another antibiotic before taking it. [HPRA patient leaflet](https://assets.hpra.ie/products/Human/15319/bafe216b-4eb4-4d1c-88a1-9a724fe5072d.pdf)

**Call 112 or 999 for signs of a severe allergic reaction**, such as sudden throat or tongue swelling, breathing difficulty or fainting. Use an adrenaline auto-injector immediately if one is available. [HSE anaphylaxis guidance](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/anaphylaxis/)

## How long should you take it?

Follow the dose, timing and course length on your prescription. These depend on the infection and factors such as kidney function. Ask a pharmacist or prescriber if directions are unclear, the symptoms get worse or you are concerned about side effects. Use antibiotics prescribed for you for the current infection. [HSE antibiotics guide](https://www2.hse.ie/medicines/antibiotics/) · [HPRA patient leaflet](https://assets.hpra.ie/products/Human/15319/bafe216b-4eb4-4d1c-88a1-9a724fe5072d.pdf)

## What side effects can it cause?

Diarrhoea, nausea and a skin rash are listed as common side effects. Ask your pharmacist or prescriber if a new rash develops. Seek urgent medical advice for severe diarrhoea, especially if it contains blood or mucus, or comes with fever and stomach pain. [HPRA patient leaflet](https://assets.hpra.ie/products/Human/15319/bafe216b-4eb4-4d1c-88a1-9a724fe5072d.pdf)
