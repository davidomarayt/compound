A bowl of porridge is not just “carbs”. Depending on what you put in it, it can also provide protein, fibre, fat and several vitamins and minerals. A food can belong to more than one nutritional category, and a balanced diet depends on that overlap.

**Macronutrients are the nutrients we need in larger amounts. Micronutrients are vitamins and minerals, needed in smaller quantities but still essential.** Understanding both helps you judge a meal more usefully than counting calories or looking for the highest-protein label. The HSE recommends variety across food groups to supply that mix. [HSE: how to eat well](https://www2.hse.ie/living-well/healthy-eating/how-to-eat-well/)

This guide covers the main nutrients, the full set of 13 vitamins, key minerals and practical Irish food choices. It is a reference for generally healthy adults, not a personalised diet or a feeding guide for babies and children. Pregnancy, illness and restricted diets can change what you need.

<div class="nutrient-takeaway" markdown="1">

### The useful starting point

Build meals around a protein source, a starchy or wholegrain food, and vegetables or fruit; include appropriate sources of calcium and some unsaturated fat across the day. Then check the situations that food alone may not cover, particularly Irish vitamin D guidance and folic acid before pregnancy. You do not need to track every nutrient to eat well.

</div>

## Macronutrients, micronutrients and calories

| Nutrient group | What belongs here? | What the name tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Energy-providing macronutrients | Protein, digestible carbohydrate and fat | Needed in relatively large amounts; usually measured in grams |
| Micronutrients | Vitamins and minerals | Needed in smaller amounts; often measured in milligrams or micrograms |
| Other essentials to remember | Water and dietary fibre | Important even when a macro-tracking app gives them less attention |

In nutrition, “calories” usually means **kilocalories (kcal)**. The standard energy factors used on EU food labels are **4 kcal per gram of protein**, **4 kcal per gram of carbohydrate other than polyols**, and **9 kcal per gram of fat**. Fibre and polyols use different factors. Alcohol supplies energy too, at **7 kcal per gram**, but is not an essential nutrient. [EU food-information regulation, Annex XIV](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2011/1169/annexes)

A worked example: 20 g protein, 40 g carbohydrate and 10 g fat contribute approximately **330 kcal**: (20 × 4) + (40 × 4) + (10 × 9). This is an arithmetic illustration, not a recommended meal. An actual label can differ because of fibre, other components and rounding.

A micronutrient supplement does not replace the energy and protein in food. Equally, meeting a calorie target does not prove that your diet provides enough vitamins, minerals or fibre.

## Protein: more than a gym nutrient

Protein supplies amino acids used to build and maintain body proteins. Its role extends beyond muscle to the many proteins that allow the body to function. Useful food sources include fish, eggs, dairy foods, lean meat, beans, lentils and soya foods. The HSE includes both animal and plant foods in its protein-containing food group. [HSE food groups](https://www2.hse.ie/living-well/healthy-eating/how-to-eat-well/)

EFSA’s **population reference intake for healthy adults is 0.83 g per kilogram of body weight per day**, applying to mixed diets with animal and plant protein. It is a reference for nutritional adequacy, not a universal sports-performance target or an upper limit. [EFSA: protein reference intakes](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/120209)

| Illustrative adult body weight | Calculation at 0.83 g/kg | Rounded daily amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 kg | 60 × 0.83 | 50 g |
| 75 kg | 75 × 0.83 | 62 g |
| 90 kg | 90 × 0.83 | 75 g |

These are worked examples of that reference, not individual prescriptions. Pregnancy, recovery from illness, substantial training or a medical condition may require a different assessment. People with kidney disease should use their clinical team’s advice rather than increasing protein from an online target.

For someone playing GAA, rugby or doing endurance training, a nutrition plan should consider the training itself, total energy and recovery as well as protein. Sport Ireland Institute provides resources specifically for active people and athletes. [Sport Ireland nutrition resources](https://www.sportireland.ie/institute/performance-service/nutrition/nutrition-resources)

A practical meal-planning question is: **where is the protein in this meal?** It might be eggs on toast, lentils in soup, yoghurt with breakfast, or fish at dinner. Protein powder can be a convenient food product, but a “high protein” claim does not tell you whether the rest of your diet is balanced.

## Carbohydrates and fibre: look at the food, not just the grams

Carbohydrates include sugars and starches. Their food sources range from oats, potatoes and beans to soft drinks and sweets; those foods are not nutritionally interchangeable. WHO recommends that carbohydrate intake come mainly from wholegrains, vegetables, fruit and pulses. [WHO: healthy diet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet)

EFSA gives a reference range of **45–60% of dietary energy from carbohydrate** for adults. This is a population reference range, not a requirement to make every meal fit a percentage. [EFSA reference tables, Table 3](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/assets/DRV_Summary_tables_jan_17.pdf)

For Irish shopping and cooking, think porridge oats, wholemeal bread, potatoes, wholegrain pasta, brown rice, peas, beans and fruit. The practical improvement is often to change the type or combination of food, rather than remove an entire food group.

### Fibre deserves its own attention

Dietary fibre is not digested and absorbed in the same way as ordinary starch and sugars. EFSA’s adult adequate intake is **25 g daily**, based on normal bowel function. It is a useful benchmark, not a ceiling or an individually measured requirement. [EFSA reference tables](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/assets/DRV_Summary_tables_jan_17.pdf)

Include fibre-containing foods repeatedly: oats at breakfast, wholemeal bread at lunch, beans in a stew and vegetables with dinner. If a digestive condition makes certain foods difficult, a dietitian can help adapt the pattern instead of leaving you to exclude more and more foods.

### “Of which sugars” is not the same as added sugar

The sugars line on a label includes sugars naturally present in foods such as milk and fruit as well as added sugars. WHO’s **free sugars** category includes added sugars plus sugars in honey, syrups, fruit juices and juice concentrates; it does not include sugars within intact fruit or naturally present in milk. WHO recommends keeping free sugars below **10% of total energy**, with further benefits below **5%**. [WHO guidance](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet)

So plain yoghurt and a sugary drink can both list sugars, but the label needs to be read alongside the ingredients and the food itself.

## Fat: the type matters

Dietary fat supplies energy and helps the body absorb the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K. Unsaturated fats include those in rapeseed and olive oils, nuts, seeds and fish. WHO recommends replacing saturated and trans fats with unsaturated fats rather than treating all fat as equivalent. [WHO: dietary fats](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet)

EFSA’s adult reference range for total fat is **20–35% of dietary energy**. It is a broad planning range, not a reason to avoid every food containing fat. [EFSA reference tables](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/assets/DRV_Summary_tables_jan_17.pdf)

In practice, try rapeseed oil for cooking, nuts with a snack or oily fish in the weekly rotation. Butter and cream need not define every meal simply because they are familiar Irish ingredients. Portion size still matters because fat is energy-dense.

Omega-3 is a family of fats, not a vitamin. Oily fish provides EPA and DHA; plant sources such as walnuts and flaxseed provide ALA. These are different forms, so a “contains omega-3” claim does not by itself identify which one you are getting. [NIH: omega-3 fatty acids](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/)

## The 13 vitamins, explained

**Fat-soluble vitamins are A, D, E and K. The B vitamins and vitamin C are water-soluble.** These descriptions concern how the body handles them; neither means unlimited supplementation is safe. The table gives selected functions and food examples, not a complete biochemical description or a dose recommendation.

| Vitamin | Selected role | Everyday food examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Vision and immune function | Eggs, dairy; carrots and leafy greens supply beta-carotene |
| B1 — thiamin | Energy metabolism and nervous-system function | Wholegrains, peas, pork |
| B2 — riboflavin | Energy metabolism; skin and eyes | Milk, eggs, fortified cereals |
| B3 — niacin | Energy metabolism; skin and nervous system | Meat, fish, wheat flour |
| B5 — pantothenic acid | Energy metabolism | Chicken, beef, eggs, mushrooms |
| B6 | Use of protein and carbohydrate; haemoglobin formation | Poultry, bananas, oats |
| B7 — biotin | Fat metabolism | Present in small amounts across many foods |
| B9 — folate | Healthy red blood cells and cell development | Leafy greens, chickpeas, fortified cereals |
| B12 | Red blood cells and nervous-system function | Fish, meat, eggs, dairy; B12-fortified foods |
| C | Collagen-related tissue health and wound healing | Peppers, citrus fruit, berries, potatoes |
| D | Bone and muscle health | Oily fish, egg yolk, some fortified foods |
| E | Protection of cells | Vegetable oils, nuts, seeds |
| K | Normal blood clotting | Broccoli and other green leafy vegetables |

Sources for the table: HSE guides to [vitamin A](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-a/), [B vitamins](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/b-vitamins-and-folic-acid/), [vitamin C](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-c/), [vitamin D](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/), [vitamin E](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-e/) and [vitamin K](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-k/).

Foods containing several B vitamins are not automatically “energy boosters”. Supporting energy metabolism is different from providing calories or producing a noticeable benefit from extra vitamins when your intake is already adequate.

## Minerals: from bones to oxygen transport

Some minerals are needed in larger amounts than others; “trace” describes the amount required, not their importance. These are selected functions and sources, not instructions to take a supplement for each row.

| Mineral | Selected role | Food examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calcium | Bones, teeth and muscle function | Milk, yoghurt, calcium-fortified drinks, calcium-set tofu |
| Iron | Oxygen transport in red blood cells | Meat, beans, lentils, fortified cereals |
| Iodine | Thyroid hormone production | Fish, shellfish; some iodine-fortified drinks |
| Magnesium | Muscle and nerve function; energy metabolism | Wholegrains, nuts, leafy greens |
| Potassium | Fluid balance and muscle function | Potatoes, vegetables, fruit, pulses |
| Sodium and chloride | Fluid balance; chloride also supports stomach acid | Salt and foods containing salt |
| Phosphorus | Bones, teeth and energy release | Dairy, meat, fish, oats |
| Zinc | Enzyme activity and wound healing | Meat, shellfish, dairy, cereals |
| Selenium | Immune and reproductive functions | Fish, eggs, meat |
| Copper | Iron metabolism and blood-cell formation | Nuts, shellfish |
| Manganese | Enzyme function | Wholegrains, nuts |
| Molybdenum | Enzyme function | Pulses, cereals, nuts |

Sources: HSE on [calcium](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/calcium/), [iron](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iron/) and [iodine](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iodine/); NHS on [other vitamins and minerals](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/others/). Food composition varies, including with fortification and preparation.

You may also see fluoride discussed for dental health, and chromium in supplement marketing. Their presence in a product is not proof you need to supplement them. EFSA did not establish a dietary requirement for chromium because the evidence did not establish its essentiality in humans. [EFSA reference-value summary](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2017_09_DRVs_summary_report.pdf)

Choline is another essential nutrient, often discussed alongside vitamins, although it is not one of the 13 vitamins. It contributes to cell membranes and nervous-system functions. Eggs, meat, fish and soya foods can contribute. [NIH: choline](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Choline-HealthProfessional/)

## The Irish priorities: where to pay particular attention

### Vitamin D: follow the Irish seasonal advice

At Ireland’s latitude, sunlight is not strong enough for skin production of vitamin D between October and early March. The HSE recommends the following **supplement** amounts; these are not amounts to subtract your food intake from. [HSE vitamin D guidance](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/)

| Adult situation | HSE advice |
| --- | --- |
| Age 18–64, without the year-round factors below | 15 micrograms daily from Halloween to St Patrick’s Day |
| Age 18–64 with darker skin, reduced sun exposure, or pregnancy | 15 micrograms daily throughout the year |
| Age 65 or older | 15 micrograms daily throughout the year |

For vitamin D, **15 micrograms = 600 IU**. If a clinician has prescribed a different dose, follow that advice. Children have different guidance. Our [dedicated Irish vitamin D guide](/health/vitamin-d-ireland-winter-who-should-take-it/) explores the topic in more depth.

### Folic acid: before pregnancy matters

The HSE recommends **400 micrograms of folic acid daily for women who can become pregnant**, even when not actively planning a pregnancy. Its pregnancy-planning page advises starting at least three months before conception and continuing throughout pregnancy. Some people need a prescribed higher dose; discuss that with a GP. [HSE: folic acid when planning a pregnancy](https://www2.hse.ie/pregnancy-birth/trying-for-a-baby/food-supplements/folic-acid/)

Folate-rich foods remain useful, but they do not replace this specific supplement recommendation. Avoid choosing a dose solely from the largest number on a prenatal label.

### Iron: low energy is a reason to investigate, not self-diagnose

The HSE highlights heavy menstrual bleeding as a risk for iron-deficiency anaemia. Its adult intake guidance is **8.7 mg daily for men**, **14.8 mg for women aged 19–50**, and **8.7 mg for women over 50**. These are dietary reference amounts, not treatment doses; personal circumstances may differ. [HSE: iron](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iron/)

Persistent fatigue has many possible causes. Ask your GP about appropriate assessment rather than taking high-dose iron indefinitely. Iron supplements can cause digestive side effects, and overdose is particularly dangerous for children. Keep them out of children’s reach. [HSE: iron safety](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iron/)

### B12, calcium and iodine when changing your diet

A vegan diet needs a reliable B12 source from fortified foods or supplementation. Plant foods should not be assumed to supply it naturally in dependable amounts. Vegan diets also need planning for calcium, iron, iodine, vitamin D and other nutrients. [NHS: the vegan diet](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/how-to-eat-a-balanced-diet/the-vegan-diet/)

If you swap cow’s milk for a plant drink, read the label for **calcium, B12, vitamin D and iodine**, as well as protein. Fortification varies: one added nutrient does not guarantee the others. Soya, oat and almond drinks are not nutritionally identical. The HSE specifically notes that only some plant drinks are iodine-fortified. [HSE: iodine](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iodine/)

For example, a hypothetical drink with 120 mg calcium per 100 ml supplies **300 mg in 250 ml**. That tells you about calcium only. It says nothing about its iodine or protein unless you check those entries too.

## How much do you need? Read the type of reference first

Different authorities and labels can use different reference systems. That is why this guide names the source beside numerical recommendations rather than combining them into a supposedly universal target.

| Term | What it means for the reader |
| --- | --- |
| Population reference intake (PRI) | A level intended to cover almost everyone in a specified healthy population group |
| Adequate intake (AI) | A reference estimated when evidence is insufficient to establish a PRI |
| Reference intake range | A range for a macronutrient, often expressed as a percentage of energy |
| Tolerable upper intake level (UL) | A safety boundary for habitual intake, not a desirable target |
| Label reference intake or nutrient reference value | A standardised comparison on packaging, not your personal prescription |

EFSA explains how dietary reference values differ by nutrient and population group. A day's intake below a PRI does not on its own diagnose a deficiency, and a percentage on packaging cannot diagnose your nutritional status. [EFSA: dietary reference values](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/dietary-reference-values)

**1 milligram (mg) = 1,000 micrograms (µg or mcg).** Confusing the units makes a thousand-fold difference. Check the nutrient amount per recommended daily serving, especially when combining products.

## Water and electrolytes belong in the picture

Water is essential even though it supplies no calories. EFSA’s adult adequate intakes are approximately **2.0 litres for women and 2.5 litres for men daily from all sources**, including food as well as drinks. These are reference amounts under moderate conditions; needs change with activity, heat and circumstances. They are not instructions to drink that much water on top of everything else. [EFSA reference tables](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/assets/DRV_Summary_tables_jan_17.pdf)

“Electrolytes” includes minerals such as sodium, potassium and chloride. Their presence on a sachet does not mean every desk worker needs a daily sports drink. A long training session, illness and an ordinary workday are different situations; use advice appropriate to the situation. If you have been given a fluid or electrolyte restriction, follow your clinical team’s plan.

## Put the nutrients into ordinary Irish meals

Healthy Ireland recommends **5–7 servings of vegetables, salad and fruit daily**, choosing wholegrain foods where possible, and using a variety of foods. Fresh, frozen and tinned options can all make meal preparation more practical. Batch cooking is particularly useful when time or shift work makes regular meals difficult. [Healthy Ireland: Eat Well](https://www.gov.ie/en/healthy-ireland/publications/eat-well/)

The following is an **illustrative meal pattern**, not a calorie-controlled menu or a guarantee that every nutrient target is met. Portions, products and your needs determine that.

| Meal | Example | What to notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Breakfast | Porridge with milk or a suitably fortified soya drink, berries and seeds | Several nutrient groups in one bowl |
| Lunch | Lentil and vegetable soup, wholemeal bread and yoghurt or a fortified alternative | Include a protein-containing food, not only bread and soup stock |
| Dinner | Salmon, potatoes and broccoli; or tofu and chickpeas with potatoes and vegetables | The plant alternative changes the nutrient profile; it is not an exact nutritional copy |
| Snack if wanted | Fruit and a handful of nuts, or yoghurt | Choose around appetite and the rest of the day |

### A shopping list that does several jobs

Instead of buying a product for every vitamin, build a flexible cupboard: oats, wholemeal bread, potatoes, beans or lentils, eggs or tofu, milk or an appropriate fortified alternative, vegetables, fruit, nuts and a cooking oil. Add foods you enjoy and can reliably prepare.

The same tin of beans can go into soup, a baked potato filling or a chilli. Frozen vegetables can serve a quick dinner without requiring you to use up a large fresh purchase. Those are meal-planning examples, not promises about current prices. Compare unit prices and buy quantities you will actually use.

An “Irish”, “natural” or “organic” claim does not replace the nutrition information. Local food can fit a good diet, but nationality alone cannot tell you its fibre, saturated fat or micronutrient content.

## Read food labels without being distracted by the headline

On most prepacked foods, the EU nutrition declaration includes energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt. Values are expressed per **100 g or 100 ml**, which helps compare products on the same basis; per-portion information can also appear. [European Commission: nutrition labelling](https://food.ec.europa.eu/food-safety/labelling-and-nutrition/food-information-consumers-legislation/nutrition-labelling_en)

First compare like with like per 100 g. Then calculate what your actual portion supplies. For example, a hypothetical cereal containing **8 g fibre per 100 g gives 3.2 g in a 40 g serving**, before adding anything else. A prominent number per 100 g can look more impressive than the amount in the bowl.

Check which vitamins or minerals are listed when relying on fortification. “High protein” does not tell you the fibre content, and a vitamin claim does not tell you how much salt is present. A label is a set of separate facts, not a single health score.

## Supplements: useful for a reason, not automatically better

There are clear reasons for some supplements: public-health recommendations, a diet that does not reliably provide a nutrient, or a diagnosed deficiency or medical need. There is also a difference between **preventing a shortfall** and **treating a deficiency**. The latter may need a different dose and follow-up.

Avoid stacking a multivitamin, an “energy” product and several individual nutrients without checking the combined amounts. Some water-soluble vitamins can still cause harm in excess; high supplemental B6, for example, can damage nerves. [HSE: B vitamins](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/b-vitamins-and-folic-acid/)

During pregnancy, avoid supplements containing vitamin A as retinol unless specifically advised, and avoid liver and liver products. [HSE: vitamin A](https://www2.hse.ie/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-a/)

Competitive athletes also need to consider contamination with prohibited substances. Sport Ireland warns that supplements can pose anti-doping risks; a marketing claim does not remove that risk. [Sport Ireland supplements policy](https://www.sportireland.ie/sites/default/files/2019-10/sport-ireland-anti-doping-supplements-policy.pdf)

Before buying, be able to answer: **what gap am I addressing, what amount am I taking, and who should review it?** A pharmacist can check interactions; a GP or registered dietitian can help where symptoms or a restricted diet need assessment.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I meet my macros and still miss micronutrients?

Yes. Matching protein, carbohydrate and fat totals does not determine which foods supplied them. Two menus with similar macros can contain different amounts of fibre, vitamins and minerals. That is why the food pattern matters alongside the numbers.

### Do I need to count macros?

No. This guide provides a food-based starting point. Tracking can be useful for a specific purpose, but it is not a prerequisite for nourishing meals. If monitoring food makes you anxious or restrictive, seek support rather than making the tracking more detailed.

### Can a multivitamin replace fruit and vegetables?

It cannot replace the whole food pattern: it does not reproduce the fibre, water and food variety provided by those foods. It may supply selected nutrients, but that is a narrower function.

### Does a normal BMI prove that my nutrition is good?

No. BMI uses height and weight; it does not assess what you eat or measure nutrient status. Our [Irish BMI calculator and limitations guide](/bmi-calculator/#bmi-limits) explains why it is only one part of a health assessment.

### Do I need blood tests for every vitamin?

There is no universal panel prescribed by this article. Discuss persistent symptoms, unintentional weight change, a highly restricted diet or a known absorption problem with your GP. The useful question is which assessment could change your care, rather than how many nutrients a test package lists.

### What should I change first?

Review a few typical meals. Find one repeatable improvement: include a protein source at a meal that lacks one, add vegetables, choose a wholegrain option or check the fortification of a dairy replacement. Separately, check the Irish supplement guidance relevant to you. A practical routine is more useful than a perfect-looking list you cannot maintain.
