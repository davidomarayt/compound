**Investing in Ireland 101 · Chapter 4 of 9**

[← Shares, ETFs and funds](/wealth/shares-etfs-funds-ireland/) · [Course hub](/wealth/how-to-start-investing-in-ireland/) · [Next: Diversification →](/wealth/diversification-portfolio-ireland/)

Ireland does not have one investment-tax rate.

A direct share, an Irish bank deposit, a pension and an investment fund can all produce “investment returns” while being taxed under different systems.

That is why tax belongs in the investment decision **before** you buy.

This guide covers the main regimes an Irish-resident individual is likely to encounter. It is educational, not a substitute for advice on a specific security or tax return.

## The Irish investment tax map

| Investment type | Typical tax route for an Irish-resident individual | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Direct shares | CGT on most gains; dividend income taxed separately | CGT generally 33%; €1,270 annual individual exemption |
| Irish company dividends | Income Tax + USC + PRSI on gross dividend | 25% DWT generally withheld first |
| Many Irish/equivalent investment funds | Exit-tax regime | 38% individual rate from 1 Jan 2026; deemed disposal may apply |
| Deposit interest | DIRT | 33% for Irish-resident individuals |
| PRSA investments | Pension regime | Investment income and gains not taxed as they arise inside PRSA |

The word **typical** matters. Product structure, domicile, residency and personal circumstances can change the treatment.

## Capital Gains Tax on direct shares

Revenue states that the standard CGT rate is **33% for most gains**.

Individuals also have an annual personal exemption of **€1,270**.

The exemption applies to net chargeable gains after allowable losses. It cannot be transferred to a spouse or civil partner.

### Simple example

You buy shares for €10,000 and later sell them for €16,000.

Ignoring transaction costs and previous losses:

**Gain:** €6,000

**Less annual exemption:** €1,270

**Chargeable amount:** €4,730

**CGT at 33%:** €1,560.90

That is a simplified example. Allowable acquisition/disposal costs and losses can change the answer.

Use the [Capital Gains Tax Calculator](/capital-gains-tax-calculator/) for the arithmetic, then check Revenue for filing and payment obligations.

## The €1,270 exemption is not a €1,270 tax credit

This is an important distinction.

It exempts the first €1,270 of qualifying annual net gains for an individual.

It does **not** reduce your tax bill by €1,270.

At a 33% rate, fully using a €1,270 exemption is worth up to €419.10 of CGT compared with having no exemption.

## Dividends are not taxed like capital gains

Revenue states that dividend income is added to your other income and can be liable to:

- Income Tax;
- Universal Social Charge; and
- PRSI.

For dividends paid by Irish-resident companies to an Irish resident, **Dividend Withholding Tax is generally deducted at 25%** before payment.

That withholding is not necessarily the final liability. It is a credit against the tax due on the gross dividend.

### Example

An Irish company declares a gross dividend of €1,000.

DWT at 25% = €250.

Cash received = €750.

The investor still declares the gross dividend and calculates the final tax liability based on their circumstances, receiving credit for the €250 already withheld.

## Foreign dividends add another layer

Foreign dividends can involve:

- foreign withholding tax;
- Irish Income Tax;
- USC;
- PRSI;
- currency conversion;
- and double-taxation treaty relief where available.

Do not assume the amount that lands in the brokerage account is the taxable amount.

Revenue's current dividend guidance distinguishes Irish, UK and other foreign dividends.

## ETF and fund tax: why Ireland is different

For many Irish-domiciled investment funds and equivalent offshore funds, Ireland uses a special investment-fund tax regime rather than ordinary CGT.

Revenue confirmed that Finance Act 2025 reduced the individual rate from **41% to 38% from 1 January 2026** for Irish-domiciled investment funds and equivalent offshore investment funds in relevant EU, EEA and treaty OECD countries.

That rate change matters, but the larger structural issue remains **deemed disposal**.

## What is eight-year deemed disposal?

Where the relevant fund regime applies, the law treats a chargeable event as happening at the end of an eight-year period even if you have not sold the investment.

In plain English:

> You can owe tax on a gain that still exists only on paper.

After the first eight-year event, subsequent eight-year periods can also create chargeable events.

Revenue's ETF guidance confirms the interaction of the eight-year deemed-disposal rule with ETFs where that regime applies.

## Deemed-disposal example

Suppose an investment within the relevant regime is worth:

- €20,000 when acquired;
- €30,000 at the eight-year deemed-disposal date.

Illustrative gain: €10,000.

At a 38% rate, the simple tax arithmetic would be €3,800 before considering the detailed rules and any relevant credits.

The investment does not need to have been physically sold for the event to matter.

This is why cash-flow planning and record keeping are important for long-term Irish fund investors.

## Not every security is taxed the same because it trades on an exchange

A common mistake is to think:

> “It has a ticker, so CGT applies.”

That is not reliable.

An ordinary company share can trade on an exchange.

An ETF can trade on the same exchange.

An investment trust can also trade on an exchange.

Their legal structures differ.

The tax analysis starts with **what the security legally is**, not with the fact that you clicked “buy” in the same brokerage app.

## DIRT on deposit interest

Revenue states that DIRT is deducted at **33%** from deposit interest paid to Irish-resident individuals.

Deposits therefore sit in a different tax bucket from direct shares and many funds.

Foreign deposit interest can have separate reporting rules.

## Losses: another reason the tax regime matters

Under ordinary CGT rules, allowable capital losses can generally be relevant when calculating net chargeable gains.

The investment-fund exit-tax regime works differently.

This means “38% versus 33%” is not the entire comparison. The timing of tax, loss treatment and administrative burden can all affect the after-tax result.

## Tax should be modelled after fees, not instead of them

An investment can be tax-efficient but expensive.

Another can be cheap but taxed in a way that produces a lower after-tax result for your circumstances.

The meaningful comparison is:

**gross return → product costs → platform costs → tax → net return to you**

Use the [Investment Fee Calculator](/investment-fee-calculator/) alongside the tax rules rather than treating either in isolation.

## Keep records from the first purchase

For every investment, keep:

- exact product/security name;
- ISIN or ticker where relevant;
- legal structure and domicile;
- acquisition date;
- acquisition cost;
- dealing and foreign-exchange costs;
- income distributions;
- tax withheld;
- disposals;
- and any deemed-disposal dates.

A spreadsheet created in year one is far easier than reconstructing eight years of transactions later.

## A simple tax-identification workflow

Before buying, answer:

### Step 1: What exactly is the product?

Company share? ETF? UCITS fund? Investment trust? Deposit? Pension fund?

### Step 2: Where is it domiciled?

Do not infer this from the currency or stock exchange.

### Step 3: Which Irish tax regime applies?

CGT? Income tax? Fund exit tax? DIRT? Pension rules?

### Step 4: When is tax triggered?

On sale? On payment of income? Every eight years? On withdrawal from the pension?

### Step 5: Who handles the tax?

Provider withholding? Broker reporting? Self-assessment?

### Step 6: What records do you need?

Decide this before there are hundreds of transactions.

## The danger of copying US or UK investing advice

US content may assume:

- Roth IRA;
- 401(k);
- US capital-gains rules;
- and US ETF taxation.

UK content may assume:

- ISA;
- SIPP;
- UK dividend allowances;
- and UK fund rules.

Those frameworks do not automatically apply to an Irish resident.

Investment principles can travel across borders.

Tax rules do not.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the Capital Gains Tax rate in Ireland?

Revenue states that CGT is 33% for most gains. Individuals have a €1,270 annual personal exemption.

### What is the ETF tax rate in Ireland in 2026?

Revenue confirmed a 38% individual rate from 1 January 2026 for Irish-domiciled investment funds and equivalent offshore investment funds in the relevant countries. Exact treatment depends on the product.

### What is deemed disposal?

Where the relevant fund regime applies, a taxable event is treated as occurring at the end of an eight-year period even if the investment has not been sold.

### Are dividends taxed at 25%?

For Irish-resident company dividends, 25% DWT is generally withheld. That is a withholding tax, not necessarily the final liability. Dividend income is added to other income and can be liable to Income Tax, USC and PRSI.

### Is DIRT still 33%?

Revenue states that DIRT is 33% on deposit interest paid to Irish-resident individuals.
