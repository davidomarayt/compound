Published figures, not invented estimates.

The library reproduces the euro values behind the official Where Your Money Goes charts. The source snapshot was last updated in October 2026 and captured for this release on 13 November 2026. Our pages focus on 2026, with historical series ending in 2026. They do not silently substitute the source’s 2027 budget projections.

02 / WHAT THE TOTAL MEANS

Gross expenditure is a defined view.

The national total combines the source’s gross voted and non-voted expenditure. Voted figures include departments and agencies, the Social Insurance Fund and the National Training Fund. Centrally held funding not allocated to departments is outside the voted dataset’s stated scope. This is not a claim that the chart includes every public-sector transaction or every local-authority payment.

Current spending generally covers day-to-day costs; capital spending concerns longer-term assets. At national level, the official current/capital chart covers the voted part only. Compound adds the published non-voted total as a separate third category so the displayed national classification reconciles. We do not guess how non-voted spending splits between current and capital.

A sector contains programmes, and a programme can contain component lines. Adding a parent to its children counts the same money twice. Our CSV marks each level so readers can select a consistent level before calculating totals.

03 / MISSING DETAIL STAYS VISIBLE

Reconcile, don’t fabricate.

The eight national sectors sum to the published 2026 national total. At lower levels, some Social Protection programme totals exceed the sum of their visible component lines. We retain the parent total, show the source lines and state the unitemised balance explicitly. That balance is an arithmetic coverage gap in this source, not evidence of lost money, fraud or a separate programme.

The non-voted sector’s three published components exceed its parent total by €1,000. We preserve both figures and label this small source rounding difference. We do not silently adjust a department’s allocation to make a chart add up.

Some source labels repeat. Numbered rows preserve distinct entries. Where a source uses an unclear accounting code, we keep that label instead of giving it a speculative policy meaning. A single-line drill-down is not dressed up as extra granularity.

Education has one top-level programme, so its 16 components open directly on the education page. This avoids a duplicate intermediary page. Other programmes with meaningful multi-line detail receive their own canonical pages.

04 / YOUR PERSONAL COMPARISON

An illustration, not a trace.

The optional personal view uses this formula:

Your entered annual contribution × programme allocation ÷ national spending total

The same national denominator is used at every depth. A component inside a small programme therefore does not suddenly receive the whole household contribution. Missing detail is not redistributed across the known components.

This illustration describes the spending mix. It does not follow your actual tax payment, earmark PRSI across departments, measure benefits your household receives, or determine whether you are a “net contributor”. Government expenditure is funded through more than employee payroll deductions, and public benefits cannot all be assigned a defensible household cash value.

The receipt calculator and this spending library are different tools. Here, enter a contribution explicitly; salary, household circumstances and relief selections are not passed in page links. These controls do not send your entries to Compound or to analytics. Normal site advertising and consent controls continue to apply.

05 / CHANGES NEED CONTEXT

Money over time is not a performance score.

Historical charts use nominal euro values, without an inflation or population adjustment. Changes can reflect non-core allocations, transfers of functions, new departmental structures or changing classification. They are not necessarily like-for-like changes in service funding.

We reproduce the history available on each source sector page. Most begin in 2016; the housing series supplied by this snapshot begins in 2020. We do not fill absent earlier years with zeros. Programme comparisons use the source’s 2024, 2025 and 2026 rows. A zero under a programme label can mean the classification was absent, not that its underlying services cost nothing.

A budget allocation is not a contract value, purchase order, payment record, completed project cost or measure of service quality. None of those is inferred from these charts.

06 / TRACEABILITY

Sources, attribution and corrections.

Every sector and programme page links to its primary source in a dedicated references section. The numerical snapshot is versioned in Compound’s repository, with capture date and SHA-256 hashes for the source chart captures. The display template is generated from that snapshot; automated tests check they agree.

Chart design, descriptions, navigation and calculations are Compound’s own. The underlying voted expenditure dataset is published by the Department of Public Expenditure, Infrastructure, Public Service Reform and Digitalisation and listed under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0. The non-voted figures are separately attributed to the official expenditure charts and their Department of Finance data basis; the voted dataset licence is not presented as a blanket licence for unrelated material.

Found a wrong value or unclear label? Contact Compound with the page, year and source. A future data revision should update the dated snapshot, reconciliation checks and affected pages together, rather than mixing source vintages.
