**Gartner is producing more profit per share while its core demand indicator grows slowly. The tension is whether buybacks are amplifying a healthy subscription business or compensating for one that needs to reaccelerate.**

The research company’s [Q3 release](https://investor.gartner.com/news-releases/news-release-details/gartner-reports-third-quarter-2026-financial-results) reported diluted earnings per share of **$3.35**, up 613%, and adjusted EPS of **$3.59**, up 30%. Those figures look powerful. They also need context: the prior-year quarter included a $150 million goodwill impairment, and Gartner has retired a large number of shares.

Meanwhile, contract value—the annualised value of subscription contracts—reached **$5.3 billion**, up only **2.5%** year on year on a foreign-currency-neutral basis.

For the maintained financial record, see [Gartner’s stock profile](/stocks/it/). This analysis separates operating progress from the arithmetic of a shrinking share count.

## Contract value is growing, but not quickly

Gartner’s [10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/749251/000074925126000307/it-20260930.htm) says Global Technology Sales contract value grew 2% and Global Business Sales grew 4%. In both divisions, new clients were the primary driver.

Retention was relatively steady. GTS client retention improved to **85% from 84%**, while wallet retention remained 98%. GBS client retention held at 87%, but wallet retention fell to **99% from 102%**.

Wallet retention above 100% means spending growth among retained clients more than offsets contractions. A move below 100% suggests the retained base is no longer expanding in aggregate. It does not mean the entire business is shrinking, because new clients also contribute.

Insights revenue—the core research subscription segment—grew 2% to **$1.292 billion**. Its gross contribution margin improved to **78% from 77%**, helped by higher revenue and an $8 million reduction in personnel expense, which Gartner attributes primarily to lower average headcount.

That is good cost discipline. It is not the same as stronger demand. A subscription business can expand margins for a time by controlling headcount, but enduring growth eventually needs contract value to accelerate.

## The share count is doing significant work

Gartner repurchased **886,818 shares for $138.2 million** in Q3. Over the first nine months it spent **$1.220 billion** on 7.83 million shares.

The effect on per-share figures is substantial. Q3 diluted weighted-average shares fell to **63.9 million from 75.0 million**, a decline of about 14.8%. If net income were unchanged, that reduction alone would increase earnings per share by roughly 17%, because the same profit would be divided among fewer shares.

Actual adjusted EPS grew 30%, so buybacks were not the whole story. Adjusted EBITDA excluding the divested Digital Markets operation rose 7.5% to $364 million. But the share count provided a large additional lift.

GAAP net income rose to **$214.4 million from $35.4 million**, yet last year’s goodwill impairment explains much of that dramatic comparison. Investors should avoid treating the 613% GAAP EPS increase as the underlying growth rate.

## Cash supports the buybacks, with debt in the picture

For the first nine months, operating cash flow was **$1.084 billion**, up from $996 million. Gartner spent $59.6 million on property and equipment and received $99.8 million from selling Digital Markets.

It also used $1.2 billion of cash for repurchases—more than the period’s operating cash generation. The balance sheet ended September with **$1.633 billion of cash** and **$2.981 billion of long-term debt**. Gartner says repurchases may be funded with cash on hand and borrowings.

This does not make buybacks inherently bad. Retiring shares can create value when the price paid is below the long-term value of the cash flows acquired. But when repurchases outrun internally generated cash, the valuation paid and the balance-sheet trade-off become more important.

Q3 free cash flow, Gartner’s non-GAAP measure after capital expenditure, was **$275 million**, up 2.2%. That modest growth sits much closer to contract-value growth than adjusted EPS does.

## What would strengthen the case?

The strongest evidence would be contract value moving back towards mid-single-digit growth while retention remains high. That would show new sales and expansion inside existing accounts supporting the subscription engine.

It would also help to see free cash flow grow fast enough to fund buybacks without leaning on cash reserves or debt. If Gartner can combine faster contract value, durable margins and disciplined repurchases, per-share growth becomes more repeatable.

The counter-case is that demand stays soft, margin gains depend on lower headcount and buybacks continue to carry too much of the per-share result. A smaller denominator can improve EPS, but it cannot create customer demand.

**Gartner’s operating model remains highly cash generative. The next investment test is whether contract value catches up with the per-share story before financial engineering has to do even more work.**
