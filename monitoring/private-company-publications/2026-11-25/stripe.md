# Stripe and Deel's Stablecoin Card: Can Keeping Contractor Pay in One App Create More Payment Revenue?

Payment providers do not necessarily benefit most from the instant a worker receives money. There is also a commercial opportunity in what happens next: where the money is held, how it is converted and which infrastructure processes its eventual spending.

On 24 November, [Stripe](https://stripe.com/newsroom/news/stripe-powers-new-deel-card-that-lets-contractors-spend-their-earnings-in-stablecoins-globally) and [Deel](https://www.deel.com/blog/introducing-new-deel-card/) announced a new prepaid Visa card that allows eligible contractors to spend funds held in Deel's digital US-dollar balance, DLUSD. Stripe says the card is powered by its infrastructure in 26 markets across Latin America, the Caribbean and Africa.

The commercial thesis is that retaining pay within a single platform could turn a payroll relationship into recurring card spending. Whether that creates incremental profitable payment volume is not established by the launch.

## What each company contributes

Deel is a payroll and contractor-management platform. Contractors can receive earnings and may be able to convert balances into DLUSD, a stablecoin issued using Bridge infrastructure. Privy provides the wallet technology, and Stripe Issuing supports card spending.

According to Stripe, the prepaid debit card is issued by Lead Bank under Visa licence, with Bridge Ventures acting as programme manager and Deel as the platform provider. These roles matter: the customer-facing app, card issuer and underlying payment infrastructure are not the same legal entity.

The card can reduce the need for a contractor to move funds to a separate application before using them. But eligibility, the terms applying to each card and the fees involved need to be checked. The announcement does not establish an Irish launch.

Stripe is privately held, and no standalone partnership revenue, audited card-specific earnings, contract value or commercial fee split was disclosed. It would be misleading to equate the amount workers hold in a digital wallet with Stripe's revenue.

## The $45 million figure is not a revenue number

Deel says contractors have held more than $45 million in its stablecoin wallet since June. This is a company-reported measure with a stated starting point. It is not independently audited in the product release and does not establish a current outstanding balance, turnover or the amount that will be spent through the new card.

For an investor evaluating the business, card usage is an additional step. Some contractors may leave their balances in the wallet; others may withdraw or spend them. Some transactions could displace volume already processed by another Stripe product rather than generate entirely new activity.

The useful future disclosure would distinguish the number of active cards, repeat monthly card usage, incremental spend, support costs and net economics after rewards and card-network arrangements.

## Why cashback can be expensive

The companies advertise cashback of up to 1.5%, subject to conditions. That is not a universal guaranteed rate on every transaction, and rewards may change.

Consider an entirely hypothetical group of 1,000 active contractors, each spending $300 a month through a card. Monthly card volume would be $300,000. If all of it earned the headline maximum of 1.5%, rewards would cost $4,500.

Suppose, for illustration only, that the card programme earned an effective 0.8% of volume before rewards and other costs. That would be $2,400. If the same party paid all $4,500 of rewards, it would face a $2,100 shortfall even before fraud, operations or support costs.

The actual allocation of fees and promotional expenses among Stripe, Deel, the issuer and other partners is not disclosed. The example is not a forecast and does not claim Stripe receives 0.8%. It simply shows why card rewards need to be assessed alongside the programme's total economics.

A temporary reward may still be rational as an acquisition expense if it encourages enough subsequent profitable activity. But reward-funded volume is not necessarily durable after the incentive changes.

## A contractor's decision is different from Stripe's

Workers should compare the total cost of receiving, retaining and spending their earnings. A digital balance denominated in US dollars may be useful when expenses are also dollar-denominated. Where rent and groceries are paid in another currency, exchange rates, conversion charges and local acceptance become important.

A meaningful comparison uses the amount of local purchasing power received after all fees, rather than a headline cashback number alone. A card that is convenient for everyday purchases may be less useful for obligations requiring a bank transfer.

The product announcement also explicitly states that DLUSD is not a bank account, is not legal tender and does not have FDIC deposit insurance. Rewards are promotional rather than interest or investment returns. Card and travel benefits have conditions. Contractors need to understand custody, redemption, available support and what happens if access to the app is disrupted.

## The investment question is incremental usage

The launch differs from [Stripe's HSBC Tap to Pay distribution partnership](/wealth/stripe-hsbc-tap-to-pay-bank-distribution-small-business/), which expands how merchants can accept payments. Deel concerns the spending side: keeping contractor earnings within the payroll platform and enabling card transactions from them.

The common strategy is embedding infrastructure in an established customer relationship instead of relying on consumers or merchants to find a separate provider. If that makes payments more convenient and supports repeat use, it could expand Stripe's addressable activity. But the launch alone cannot establish profitability.

Follow the [Stripe company research profile](/companies/stripe/) for verified company developments. The key evidence to watch is card activation, actual recurring spending, costs after incentives and expansion into additional markets on confirmed terms.
