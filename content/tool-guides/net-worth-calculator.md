## What net worth actually measures

**Net worth is a balance-sheet number:** the current value of what you own minus the current amount you owe.

The Central Statistics Office uses essentially the same framework when measuring Irish household wealth. In the Household Finance and Consumption Survey, **gross wealth** is the value of real and financial assets, while **net wealth** is gross wealth less debt.

That sounds simple, but the quality of the result depends on the quality of the values you enter. A bank balance is precise. The value of a home, private business, vehicle or collection of valuables is an estimate. This calculator is designed to make those assumptions visible rather than create false precision.

## What to count as an asset

For a personal or household net-worth snapshot, common assets include:

- cash in current, savings and credit-union accounts;
- shares, funds and other investments at current market value;
- pension and retirement funds at their current fund value;
- your main home and other property at a realistic current market value;
- vehicles, business interests and other material assets where a reasonable current value can be estimated.

The CSO's Irish household wealth survey similarly includes main residences, other property, land, businesses, vehicles and valuables as real assets, alongside deposits, shares, funds, voluntary pensions and other financial assets.

The important rule is **do not count the same value twice**. If a business owns a property and your estimate of the business already includes that property, do not add the property again personally. Likewise, do not include a pension fund inside “investments” and then again under “pensions”.

## Use current value, not what you originally paid

Net worth is intended to be a snapshot of the balance sheet **today**.

A home bought for €250,000 that could reasonably sell for €350,000 is a €350,000 asset for this purpose. A car bought for €40,000 that might now sell for €22,000 is closer to a €22,000 asset.

The opposite is true for debt. Enter the **current outstanding balance**, not the original amount borrowed and not the total of all future repayments including future interest.

## Why pensions are included — and also shown separately

Pension assets can be a major part of long-term household wealth, but they are not the same as cash in a savings account. Access is restricted and the eventual amount available can be affected by investment performance, pension rules and taxation.

Compound therefore includes pension pots in the main net-worth figure but also shows **net worth excluding pensions**. That second number is not “liquid net worth” — your home and other illiquid assets may still be included — but it helps separate retirement assets from the rest of the balance sheet.

## Property equity is not the same as property value

If a home is worth €400,000 and the mortgage balance is €250,000, the household has approximately **€150,000 of home equity** before selling costs and any other adjustments.

Compound's property-equity result combines the property values you enter and subtracts the related mortgage balances. Property equity can be negative if secured debt exceeds the estimated property value.

## Debt-to-assets: useful, but not a score

The debt-to-assets ratio divides total liabilities by total assets. A household with €100,000 of debt and €400,000 of assets has a 25% ratio.

Lower leverage generally means more of the asset base is financed by the household's own equity, but there is no universal “good” percentage for an individual household. Age, income stability, mortgage stage, interest rates, liquidity and the type of assets and debts all matter.

The Central Bank also tracks household debt relative to assets at an economy-wide level. Those national figures are useful context, but they should not be treated as a personal target.

## Irish context: useful perspective, not a leaderboard

The CSO's 2023 Household Finance and Consumption Survey reported **median household net wealth of €256,900**. The main residence represented 46% of gross household wealth, and 66% of households had some form of debt.

Those statistics describe households across Ireland, not an appropriate target for any one person. Household size, age, home ownership, pension coverage, region and life stage all have a large effect on the number. A 28-year-old renter and a mortgage-free retired couple should not expect similar balance sheets.

More recent Central Bank aggregate data show the same broad lesson: housing and pension/financial assets make up a large share of Irish household wealth, while household liabilities are dominated by long-term loans.

## Net worth is not the same as financial health

A high net worth can coexist with poor cash flow. Someone may own a valuable home but have little accessible cash and a stretched monthly budget. Conversely, someone early in their career may have a low or negative net worth while having a strong income, manageable debt and a high savings rate.

For that reason, use this number alongside other questions:

- Do you have enough accessible cash for emergencies?
- Is expensive debt falling over time?
- Are pension and long-term investment contributions on track?
- Is your monthly cash flow sustainable?
- Is too much of your wealth concentrated in one asset?

Net worth is most useful as a **trend you track against your own previous position**, not as a single score.

## A simple way to use this calculator

Pick a date — for example the end of each quarter or year — and use reasonably consistent valuation methods each time.

Keep bank and investment balances exact where possible. For property, vehicles and private businesses, avoid changing the valuation method merely to make the result look better. Over time, the change in your balance sheet can then be separated into things such as saving, debt repayment and changes in asset values.

## Important limitations

This is an educational balance-sheet calculator, not a formal valuation, insolvency assessment or financial-planning recommendation.

It does not deduct hypothetical Capital Gains Tax, transaction costs, pension tax, estate costs or selling costs from the asset values you enter. Those amounts may matter if an asset is actually sold or transferred. Private-company interests, property and valuables may also sell for materially more or less than an estimate.

The calculator runs entirely in your browser. Compound does not receive the values you enter.

## Worked example

Consider a household with:

- a home worth €400,000;
- €20,000 in cash;
- €30,000 in investments;
- €80,000 in pension funds;
- a €250,000 mortgage;
- and a €10,000 car/personal loan.

Total assets are €530,000 and total liabilities are €260,000, producing an estimated **net worth of €270,000**.

Within that number, home equity is €150,000. The pension is part of long-term wealth but is not normally available like a bank balance. That is why Compound also separates property equity, financial assets and net worth excluding pension pots rather than treating every euro of wealth as equally liquid.

## Method and limitations

The calculation is current asset values minus current liabilities. Property equity is property value minus related secured borrowing. The debt-to-assets ratio divides total liabilities by total assets.

No hypothetical selling tax, transaction cost or future market movement is deducted. Private businesses, property, vehicles and valuables can be difficult to value accurately. Use consistent valuation methods if you track the figure over time.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I include my pension?

Yes for a broad net-worth measure, but pensions are less liquid than cash or ordinary investments. That is why Compound also shows net worth excluding pension pots.

### Should I include my car?

Only if you want a fuller balance sheet and can use a realistic resale value. Vehicles usually depreciate, so original purchase price is not appropriate.

### Is home equity the same as cash?

No. Equity can be economically valuable while remaining difficult or expensive to access without selling, refinancing or borrowing.

### Is negative net worth automatically a crisis?

No. A young household with a large mortgage can have low or negative net worth while still having strong income and manageable cash flow. The trend and the structure of assets and liabilities matter.
