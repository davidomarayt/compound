A **€399-a-month PCP** can look cheaper than a **€760-a-month car loan**.

That does not mean the PCP is cheaper.

It may simply mean that a large part of the car's price has been moved to the end of the agreement.

That single distinction explains most of the confusion around car finance in Ireland.

If you are comparing a **personal car loan, Hire Purchase (HP) and Personal Contract Plan (PCP)**, the useful questions are not just:

> What is the monthly payment?

They are:

> **How much cash will I pay in total, when do I legally own the car, what will still be owed at the end, and what happens if I want to exit early?**

The [Car Finance Calculator](/car-finance-calculator/) is built around exactly those questions.

## The short version

The three structures are fundamentally different.

| Finance type | Who owns the car during the agreement? | Typical payment shape | Large final payment? | Mileage / condition restrictions? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Personal car loan | You normally own it from the start | Regular loan repayments | Usually no | Normally no finance-specific restrictions |
| Hire Purchase | Finance company until final payment | Deposit + regular repayments | Sometimes | Depends on agreement |
| PCP | Finance company until final payment | Deposit + lower regular repayments | Usually yes — balloon / GMFV | Commonly yes |

The CCPC's current guidance says that with a personal loan you own the car from the day you buy it. With HP and PCP, the finance company remains the legal owner until the required final payment has been made.

That difference affects your flexibility.

With a personal loan, the car is your asset and the loan is a separate debt.

With HP or PCP, you generally cannot sell the car without the finance company's permission because you are not yet the legal owner.

[CCPC: paying for your car](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/borrowing-money/car-loans-and-car-finance/paying-for-your-car).

## What is a personal car loan?

A personal car loan is the simplest of the three structures.

You borrow a fixed amount from a bank, credit union or other lender, use the money to buy the car and then repay the loan over an agreed period.

The important feature is ownership:

**you normally own the car from the start.**

That means the car can usually be sold by you if your circumstances change, although the loan itself still has to be repaid according to its terms.

A personal loan can therefore separate two decisions:

1. whether you want to keep the car; and
2. how you repay the debt.

That is different from HP and PCP, where the vehicle itself remains legally tied to the finance agreement until ownership passes to you.

## What is Hire Purchase?

Hire Purchase is a credit agreement where the finance company buys the car and you effectively hire it while making repayments.

You normally pay:

- a deposit;
- monthly instalments;
- interest and any applicable fees; and
- sometimes a small completion or option-to-purchase fee.

You become the legal owner only when the agreement has been fully completed.

The CCPC recommends comparing the **total HP price** rather than just looking at the monthly repayment.

That is important because two HP quotes with similar monthly payments can have different:

- deposits;
- terms;
- interest rates;
- documentation fees;
- completion fees; and
- total amounts payable.

[CCPC: Hire Purchase](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/borrowing-money/car-loans-and-car-finance/hire-purchase).

## What is PCP?

A Personal Contract Plan is closely related to Hire Purchase but deliberately leaves a large amount unpaid until the end.

The structure usually has three parts.

### 1. Deposit

CCPC says a PCP deposit is commonly around **10% to 30% of the car's value**, paid in cash or through a trade-in.

### 2. Monthly repayments

The monthly payments can look relatively low because you are not paying off the entire financed amount during the agreement.

### 3. Final balloon payment

A large final amount is left outstanding.

This is often described as the **balloon payment** or **Guaranteed Minimum Future Value (GMFV)**.

If you want to own the car at the end, you must pay that amount.

That is why a PCP can have the lowest monthly payment while still producing a similar — or even higher — total cash cost if you ultimately keep the car.

[CCPC: Personal Contract Plan](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/borrowing-money/car-loans-and-car-finance/personal-contract-plan).

## A worked €40,000 example

Take an illustrative €40,000 car.

Assume:

| Assumption | Personal loan | Hire Purchase | PCP |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Car price | €40,000 | €40,000 | €40,000 |
| Deposit / trade-in | €8,000 | €8,000 | €8,000 |
| Amount financed | €32,000 | €32,000 | €32,000 |
| Comparison term | 4 years | 4 years | 4 years |
| Illustrative annual rate | 6.5% | 6.9% | 4.9% |
| PCP balloon / GMFV | — | — | €18,000 |

These rates are **illustrative assumptions**, not current lender quotations.

Using standard amortising-payment maths for the personal loan and HP, and a balloon-payment calculation for PCP, the approximate result is:

| Result | Personal loan | Hire Purchase | PCP — keep car |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Monthly repayment | €759 | €765 | €395 |
| Final balloon | €0 | €0 | €18,000 |
| Approx. total cash paid | €44,426 | €44,710 | €44,973 |

That table is the core lesson.

The PCP monthly repayment is roughly **€364 lower than the personal loan**.

Yet if the car is kept and the €18,000 balloon is paid, the overall cash paid is slightly higher under these particular assumptions.

The lower monthly payment did not come from making €18,000 disappear.

It came from postponing it.

## PCP can still be cheaper — but you have to compare the same outcome

There is no rule that PCP must cost more than HP or a loan.

A subsidised manufacturer finance rate, a strong deposit contribution or a genuinely low APR can change the arithmetic.

The comparison has to use the same outcome.

If one quote is:

- a three-year PCP where you hand the car back;

and another is:

- a five-year personal loan where you own the car outright;

then the final positions are different.

One leaves you with no car.

The other leaves you owning an asset.

Comparing only the monthly payments would hide that.

## What happens at the end of a PCP?

The CCPC describes three common choices.

### Pay the balloon and keep the car

You pay the final balloon amount and ownership transfers to you.

Before choosing a PCP, this raises an important question:

**Where will the balloon money come from?**

Possible sources include savings, another loan, refinancing or the sale of another asset.

If you need another loan to fund the balloon, the PCP has not really ended your borrowing — it has moved part of it into a second financing decision.

### Return the car

If the agreement's conditions are met, you can generally hand the car back instead of paying the balloon.

But returning does not necessarily mean every additional cost is zero.

The CCPC notes that contracts can include:

- annual mileage limits;
- excess-mileage charges;
- rules on wear and tear;
- servicing requirements; and
- other vehicle-condition obligations.

If those terms have not been met, charges can arise.

### Trade the car into another PCP

If the car's market value is above the final payment, the difference may provide equity towards the next deposit.

For example:

- market value at end: €21,000;
- balloon / GMFV: €18,000;
- potential gross equity: €3,000.

That €3,000 is not guaranteed.

It depends on the actual second-hand value, the vehicle's condition and the terms of the agreement.

If the car is worth €18,000, there may be no equity.

If it is worth less than €18,000, paying the balloon to own the car may mean paying more than its current market value.

CCPC also notes that if you simply hand the car back, any equity is not returned to you.

## The balloon changes how you should read the interest rate

A PCP can advertise a low rate and still leave a large total amount unpaid.

That does not make the rate irrelevant.

It means the rate and the balloon must be considered together.

Suppose two PCPs have identical:

- car price;
- deposit;
- term;
- interest rate.

But one has a €14,000 balloon and the other has an €18,000 balloon.

The second can produce a lower monthly repayment because more principal has been pushed into the final payment.

A lower monthly number can therefore be created without reducing the total amount that ultimately has to be funded if you keep the car.

## APR matters — but so does total amount payable

When comparing credit, APR is designed to help express the annualised cost of borrowing.

But for a real car-finance comparison you should also look at the lender's **total amount payable** and the actual cash-flow structure.

That means checking:

- cash price;
- deposit;
- amount financed;
- APR / interest rate;
- term;
- monthly repayment;
- fees;
- final balloon;
- total amount payable;
- mileage conditions;
- end-of-contract charges; and
- ownership position.

## A realistic three-year PCP can look very different again

Now keep the same:

- €40,000 car;
- €8,000 deposit;
- €32,000 financed amount;
- 4.9% illustrative PCP annual rate;
- €18,000 balloon.

But shorten the PCP to **three years**, which the CCPC says is a common PCP duration.

Ignoring fees, the estimated monthly repayment rises to roughly:

**€492 a month**

because the portion being repaid before the balloon now has to be covered over fewer months.

This is another reason not to compare quotes with different terms.

A €450 monthly PCP over three years and a €450 monthly loan over five years are not equivalent offers.

## Mileage is not a small-print detail

PCP return economics depend heavily on mileage.

Assume a contract allows:

**15,000 km a year**

but you actually drive:

**18,000 km a year**

Over a three-year agreement, that is:

**9,000 excess kilometres**

If the contract charged an illustrative:

**€0.10 per excess kilometre**

the excess-mileage charge would be:

**€900**

That figure is only an example. Your own contract determines the actual allowance and charge.

The point is that a PCP quote should be tested against your real driving pattern before the monthly payment is judged.

For someone driving 25,000 km a year, a quote built around a much lower mileage allowance may not be an apples-to-apples comparison.

## Condition requirements matter too

If you return a PCP car, the agreement can set standards around:

- body damage;
- wheel damage;
- tyres;
- servicing;
- modifications; and
- general wear and tear.

The exact standard is contractual.

A driver planning to return the car should understand those requirements at the beginning of the agreement, not only in the final month.

## Ownership changes your flexibility

This is one of the biggest practical differences between the three structures.

### Personal loan

You normally own the car.

If you want to sell it, you can generally do so and separately deal with the remaining loan balance.

### Hire Purchase / PCP

The finance company owns the car until the agreement is completed.

You cannot simply sell someone else's asset.

The CCPC says that if you are struggling with HP or PCP payments, the finance company may allow the car to be sold to repay the debt, but you must obtain its permission first.

That restriction can matter if:

- your income changes;
- you move abroad;
- your family needs a different type of car;
- your annual mileage changes sharply; or
- you simply want to exit the vehicle early.

## The half rule: a major Irish consumer protection

Hire Purchase and PCP have an important legal feature known as the **half rule**.

Under the Consumer Credit Act 1995, the CCPC explains that you can end an HP or PCP agreement by returning the car, subject to the half-rule conditions.

If you have already paid at least half of the total hire-purchase cost, you can return the car and end the agreement, although you may still owe amounts for damage or repairs.

If you have paid less than half, you can still return it but may have to pay enough to bring the amount paid up to the halfway point.

Arrears can also remain payable.

[CCPC: problems making car repayments](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/managing-debt/problems-making-car-repayments).

This is not the same as **voluntary surrender**.

CCPC warns that with voluntary surrender you can remain liable for the outstanding debt after the car is sold.

The wording and process matter, so anyone considering an early exit should read the agreement and the CCPC guidance carefully before signing documents.

## The one-third rule also matters for repossession

CCPC states that, for HP and PCP, repossession rights change once more than one-third of the relevant purchase price has been paid.

Below the threshold, repossession can occur without legal action in circumstances covered by the rules.

Above it, the lender must take legal action.

This is another example of why HP and PCP should not be treated as ordinary unsecured personal loans with a different marketing name.

They are legally and practically tied to the vehicle.

## Car finance can affect your credit record

The Central Bank says that credit of **€500 or more** is reported to the Central Credit Register under the applicable reporting rules.

Hire Purchase and PCP providers are now within the Central Bank's consumer-protection framework, and regulated firms must carry out creditworthiness and affordability assessments before granting covered credit.

[Central Bank: short-term credit](https://www.centralbank.ie/consumer-hub/short-term-credit).

So a low monthly PCP repayment should not be treated as evidence that the overall commitment is small.

The finance still represents debt and can affect future borrowing decisions.

## Dealer contribution: separate the discount from the finance

A dealer or manufacturer may offer:

- a deposit contribution;
- a lower promotional finance rate;
- a trade-in bonus;
- or a combination.

Those can make a finance package genuinely more attractive.

But separate the components.

For example:

**Car cash price:** €40,000  
**Finance-only deposit contribution:** €2,000  
**Customer deposit:** €8,000

The €2,000 contribution is economically valuable.

But you still need to compare:

1. the best cash price you could negotiate;
2. the total amount payable under the finance offer;
3. whether the promotional rate applies only to a specific term or model; and
4. whether there are fees or conditions attached.

Do not compare the finance rate in isolation from the purchase price.

## Trade-in value can disguise the real deposit

Suppose your current car is worth €12,000 and you use the full value as the PCP deposit.

The new monthly payment may become very low.

But the €12,000 trade-in is still money.

It is part of your economic cost even if no cash leaves your bank account that day.

When using the calculator, enter the value of the trade-in being consumed as the deposit.

Otherwise the comparison understates what you are putting into the deal.

## Put the car decision in the wider household budget

A car is often treated as a one-off purchase, but most households replace vehicles repeatedly over decades. A €10,000 difference in car choice can therefore recur several times.

For that longer view, see [How much does your lifestyle actually cost over a lifetime?](/wealth/lifetime-cost-of-lifestyle-ireland/), which models repeated major purchases separately from day-to-day transport running costs.

## A useful comparison is "cost to own", not only "cost per month"

For someone who expects to keep the car long-term, calculate:

**deposit + all repayments + all fees + balloon**

That gives a much cleaner **cost-to-own** comparison.

For PCP, also compare the balloon with the expected market value at the end.

If the car is expected to be worth:

**€21,000**

and the balloon is:

**€18,000**

there may be roughly:

**€3,000 of potential equity**

before transaction costs or any condition adjustments.

If the car is expected to be worth:

**€16,000**

and the balloon is:

**€18,000**

then paying €18,000 to own a €16,000 vehicle is economically different from handing it back under the contract, assuming the return conditions are satisfied.

This is why the calculator's Advanced mode asks for an estimated end value.

It is an assumption, not a prediction.

## A useful comparison for someone who changes cars frequently is different

If you normally replace the car every three years, the question may be less about ultimate ownership and more about the total cost of having access to the vehicle for that period.

For a PCP return scenario, consider:

**deposit + monthly repayments + fees + likely return charges**

Then compare that with the economic cost of owning a car over the same period:

**purchase / finance cost + interest + fees - resale value**

Those are closer to the same outcome.

The headline monthly payment alone is not.

## Common mistakes when comparing car finance

### Looking only at the monthly repayment

PCP is specifically structured to defer a large amount.

Monthly payment is therefore an incomplete metric.

### Ignoring the deposit

A €10,000 trade-in is still €10,000 of value committed to the deal.

### Forgetting the balloon

If your plan is to keep the car, the balloon belongs in the total cost.

### Comparing different terms

Three years at €500 a month is not directly comparable with five years at €500 a month.

### Assuming GMFV is guaranteed profit

GMFV is the contractual final amount, not a promise that the car will be worth more than that in the open market.

### Ignoring mileage

A low monthly PCP can become less attractive if your real mileage is materially above the contracted allowance.

### Rolling into a new PCP without checking the equity

The fact that a dealer can move you into another agreement does not tell you whether your current car has generated meaningful equity.

### Financing the balloon without counting the second loan

If you borrow again to pay the final PCP amount, include the cost of that new borrowing in the long-run ownership calculation.

## Which figures matter most?

For a personal loan:

- amount borrowed;
- APR / annual rate;
- term;
- monthly payment;
- fees;
- total amount payable;
- early-repayment terms.

For HP:

- cash price;
- deposit;
- total HP price;
- rate;
- fees;
- term;
- ownership transfer;
- half-rule position.

For PCP:

- everything above, plus;
- final balloon / GMFV;
- mileage allowance;
- excess-mileage rate;
- return-condition rules;
- servicing rules;
- estimated end value;
- likely equity;
- plan for the balloon.

## The practical way to compare three offers

Start with the **same car and the same negotiated cash price**.

Then record:

1. how much value you are putting in upfront;
2. every monthly payment;
3. every mandatory fee;
4. any final payment;
5. whether you own the car at the end;
6. what the car may be worth at that point;
7. what restrictions apply if you return it; and
8. what happens if you need to exit early.

Then run the exact figures through the [Car Finance Calculator](/car-finance-calculator/).

That turns a question that often looks like:

> €395 versus €765 a month — why would anyone choose the higher payment?

into the more useful comparison:

> **What am I paying in total, and what do I own when the agreement is finished?**

That is the comparison that exposes the real economics of PCP, Hire Purchase and a car loan.
