It often happens gradually.

The alarm feels harder.

The drive home is suddenly darker.

Going for a walk after work requires more effort than it did a month ago.

By 8pm, you feel as if the day has lasted much longer than it actually has.

And because it happens at roughly the same time every year, it is tempting to conclude that your body is simply “bad at winter”.

There is a real biological reason the darker part of the year can feel different.

But there is also a lot of overstatement around it.

Less daylight can affect the timing signals that help regulate your body clock. Seasonal changes can also affect routines, activity, socialising and sleep. For some people, autumn and winter bring a genuine depressive illness called **seasonal affective disorder — SAD**.

For many others, the change is milder: lower energy, more reluctance to get up, less time outdoors and a sense that the day closes in earlier.

Those are not necessarily the same thing.

<div class="cs-intro">
<p class="cs-kicker">Quick answer</p>
<p><strong>Shorter days can influence the light signals that help regulate sleep and circadian rhythms, and some people do notice changes in energy or mood as autumn and winter arrive.</strong></p>
<p>But feeling tired in October does not automatically mean you have seasonal affective disorder. Sleep loss, stress, illness, low activity, iron deficiency, medication, work patterns and many other factors can produce the same symptom.</p>
<p>The practical first steps are simple: get daylight early in the day, keep your wake time reasonably consistent, stay active, and do not let the darker evenings quietly remove every outdoor or social activity from your week.</p>

## Why does autumn feel different?

Humans do not run only on clocks on the wall.

Your body contains an internal timing system — usually called the **circadian system** — that helps coordinate sleep and wakefulness across roughly 24 hours.

Light is one of its most important environmental signals.

The light-dark cycle helps the brain distinguish biological day from biological night.

That does not mean every cloudy morning instantly determines your mood.

It means light exposure is one of the cues your body uses to keep timing organised.

A 2024 real-world study involving more than 1,000 people found associations between outdoor daylight exposure and measures of wellbeing and depressive symptoms. Sleep problems and regularity of daily routines appeared to explain some of that relationship.

That is interesting, but it is not proof that simply spending more time outside will prevent depression.

A systematic review of personal light exposure and sleep or mood found the evidence much less certain. The included studies were mostly observational, and the overall quality was low.

That distinction matters.

**The biological mechanism is plausible. The practical advice is reasonable. The evidence is not strong enough to turn daylight into a cure-all.**

## Ireland's clocks go back on 25 October 2026

In Ireland, the clocks go back by one hour on **Sunday, 25 October 2026**.

At 2am, the clocks return to 1am.

The immediate effect is more light in the morning and darkness arriving earlier in the evening.

For someone who finishes work around 5pm or 6pm, that can change daily behaviour very quickly.

A walk that happened naturally in September may suddenly require deliberate planning.

Children's activities move indoors.

People drive rather than walk.

Social plans can feel like more effort.

The clock change does not create winter.

But it can make the seasonal shift feel abrupt.

## Morning light matters because timing matters

The HSE's sleep guidance specifically advises getting outside and getting light exposure early in the day.

That advice is not only about “fresh air”.

Morning light acts as a timing signal for the body clock.

If possible, try to get outside reasonably soon after waking.

It does not need to become a complicated routine.

You could:

- walk for part of your commute;
- have coffee outside;
- walk the dog before work;
- take a short walk after the school run;
- or simply spend a little time outdoors rather than moving from house to car to office under artificial light.

On dark Irish winter mornings, this will not always mean bright sunshine.

The point is exposure to outdoor daylight rather than trying to recreate summer.

## Why can you feel tired even if you are sleeping the same number of hours?

Sleep duration is only one part of sleep.

Timing and regularity matter too.

Imagine two people who both sleep seven and a half hours.

One sleeps roughly 11pm to 6:30am every night.

The other sleeps 11pm to 6:30am on workdays, stays up until 2am on Friday, sleeps until 10am Saturday, stays up late again, then struggles to sleep Sunday night.

The total hours can look similar.

The timing is very different.

The HSE recommends keeping a regular waking time and getting early-day light exposure to help the body clock stay in rhythm.

That does not mean you need military precision.

It means that if autumn tiredness is becoming a problem, repeatedly shifting sleep and wake times by several hours may make the week harder.

For the wider evidence on sleep duration, see [How much sleep do adults need?](/health/how-much-sleep-do-adults-need/).

## Dark evenings can change behaviour before they change biology

This is one of the more overlooked explanations.

When evenings are bright, you may:

- walk after dinner;
- meet someone outside;
- garden;
- go to the park;
- cycle;
- bring children somewhere;
- or simply spend longer away from the sofa.

When it is dark, wet and cold, those behaviours can disappear without a conscious decision.

That creates a second pathway.

It is not only:

**less daylight → body clock**

It can also be:

**darker evening → less activity → fewer plans → less social contact → more time indoors**

Those changes can matter for wellbeing.

The HSE recommends keeping active, particularly outdoors in daylight, as one of the self-help measures for seasonal affective disorder.

Our [evidence-based guide to being happier](/happiness/how-to-be-happier-evidence-ireland/) reaches a similar conclusion from a broader evidence base: relationships, activity and meaningful engagement matter more than chasing a permanently positive mood.

## Is this seasonal affective disorder?

Not necessarily.

The HSE describes seasonal affective disorder as a type of depression triggered by seasonal change.

Symptoms typically start in autumn or winter and improve in spring.

Possible symptoms include:

- persistent low mood;
- loss of pleasure or interest in normal activities;
- irritability;
- feelings of hopelessness, guilt or worthlessness;
- low self-esteem;
- anxiety or stress;
- becoming less sociable;
- and reduced sex drive.

SAD is therefore more than saying:

**“I hate it getting dark at five.”**

The key features are the **depressive symptoms**, their impact on daily life and the recurring seasonal pattern.

Some people have relatively mild symptoms.

For others, it can significantly affect functioning.

## The word “seasonal” can hide other causes

If you become exhausted every autumn, seasonality is worth noticing.

But do not let the calendar close the investigation.

Persistent tiredness can have many causes.

Examples include:

- insufficient sleep;
- insomnia;
- stress;
- depression;
- anxiety;
- shift work;
- sleep apnoea;
- infection;
- anaemia or iron deficiency;
- thyroid problems;
- medication;
- pregnancy;
- alcohol;
- and other physical or mental health conditions.

If the fatigue is new, severe, persistent or difficult to explain, it is reasonable to speak to your GP rather than assuming winter is the cause.

This is particularly important if tiredness is accompanied by symptoms such as unexplained weight change, breathlessness, persistent pain, marked weakness, major sleep disruption or a significant change in mood.

## What actually helps with seasonal low mood?

The HSE lists several practical measures for seasonal affective disorder.

They include:

- getting as much natural sunlight as possible;
- spending time outdoors, including a lunchtime walk;
- keeping work and home environments bright;
- sitting near windows;
- staying active, especially outdoors;
- eating a balanced diet;
- managing stress;
- and talking to friends and family about what is happening.

These are sensible starting points because they are low-risk and address multiple parts of the problem at once.

A lunchtime walk, for example, can give you:

- daylight;
- movement;
- a break from work;
- and potentially social contact.

You do not need to decide which component is responsible for feeling better before doing it.

## A realistic autumn routine

Wellbeing advice often becomes another list of things you are failing to do.

A better approach is to protect a few behaviours that winter tends to remove.

### Morning: get outside

Try to get some outdoor light relatively early.

Five or ten minutes is more realistic than designing an elaborate morning routine you will abandon after three days.

If you already walk the dog or walk to transport, you may not need to add anything.

### Midday: use the daylight that exists

In December, waiting until after work for outdoor time can mean waiting until darkness.

Lunch may be the easiest daylight window.

Even a short walk can be useful.

### Evening: replace the activity that disappeared

If you used to walk at 7pm in summer but will not do it in winter darkness, choose what replaces it.

That might be:

- a gym session;
- a swimming pool;
- an indoor sport;
- a walk before work instead;
- a weekend hike;
- or moving the walk to lunchtime.

The important thing is noticing that the summer habit disappeared rather than wondering in January why your activity level collapsed.

### Night: keep the sleep anchor

The most useful sleep anchor is often the **wake time**.

The HSE advises getting up at your regular time even after a poor night's sleep.

That helps avoid turning one bad night into several days of shifted sleep timing.

## What about vitamin D?

This is where two separate ideas are often mixed together.

Ireland's winter sunlight is not strong enough to allow the skin to make adequate vitamin D in the same way it can during stronger months.

That is a nutritional issue.

It does **not** mean every autumn drop in mood is caused by vitamin D deficiency.

The HSE has specific vitamin D supplementation advice, which we cover separately in [Vitamin D in winter: who in Ireland should take a supplement](/health/vitamin-d-ireland-winter-who-should-take-it/).

Take vitamin D because it is recommended for the relevant health reasons and age group — not because it has been marketed as a guaranteed fix for winter mood.

## Does a SAD lamp work?

Light therapy is a recognised treatment option for seasonal affective disorder.

It usually involves sitting near a specially designed bright light box, commonly in the morning.

The HSE says evidence is mixed overall, while noting that some research has found benefit, particularly when light therapy is used first thing in the morning.

More recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have also found evidence that bright or white light therapy can improve SAD symptoms, although the studies vary in design and quality.

That does **not** mean any bright lamp on the internet is appropriate.

The HSE advises choosing a light box intended for SAD treatment and following the manufacturer's instructions.

Very bright light may not be suitable for everyone, particularly people with certain eye conditions or those taking medicines that increase light sensitivity.

If you are unsure, speak to your GP before using one.

## What light therapy is not

A SAD lamp is not the same thing as:

- a normal desk lamp;
- a phone screen;
- a bright television;
- or keeping every ceiling light on until midnight.

Timing matters.

Bright evening light can itself influence circadian timing.

So the lesson from light therapy should not become:

**“more light at every hour is always better.”**

The practical everyday advice remains simpler:

**seek natural daylight during the day, particularly earlier in the day, and protect a normal night-time environment when you are trying to sleep.**

## Do you need more sleep in winter?

People sometimes report wanting to sleep more in winter.

That does not automatically mean the body suddenly requires an extra two hours every night.

If you are consistently sleepy, start with the basics:

- are you actually getting enough sleep?
- has bedtime moved later?
- are you sleeping in much longer at weekends?
- are you waking repeatedly?
- are you drinking more alcohol?
- has stress increased?
- are you getting less exercise?
- do you snore heavily or stop breathing during sleep?
- is the tiredness present even after a good night?

Most adults generally fall somewhere around the **7–9 hour** range, but individual need varies.

The more useful test is how you function during the day.

If you are getting apparently adequate sleep but remain persistently exhausted, the answer may not be simply “sleep more”.

## Stress can masquerade as autumn fatigue

October is not only darker.

For many people, it also means:

- work is fully back after summer;
- schools and activities are busy;
- commuting increases;
- Christmas spending starts appearing;
- annual targets become more visible;
- and there is less leave on the horizon.

You can therefore have a seasonal pattern without daylight being the only cause.

The environment and the calendar change together.

If your exhaustion comes with constant worry, irritability, difficulty concentrating or an inability to switch off, our [guide to stress symptoms and what actually helps](/happiness/stress-symptoms-what-helps-ireland/) may be more relevant.

## A useful question: what changed since August?

Instead of trying to diagnose yourself immediately, compare your routine.

Ask:

- Am I getting less daylight?
- Am I going outside less?
- Has my exercise dropped?
- Am I seeing friends less often?
- Has my sleep timing changed?
- Am I working longer?
- Has commuting increased?
- Am I drinking more?
- Am I spending much more time on screens at night?
- Has anything else in my health changed?

That turns a vague feeling — **“winter makes me tired”** — into specific things you can investigate.

Sometimes the answer will genuinely be seasonal.

Sometimes autumn merely exposes a sleep or stress problem that was already there.

## When to speak to your GP

Speak to your GP if low mood or fatigue is persistent, worsening or affecting daily life.

You should particularly seek help if you have:

- a persistent low mood;
- lost interest or pleasure in activities;
- significant changes in sleep or appetite;
- difficulty functioning at work or home;
- recurring symptoms every autumn or winter;
- or symptoms that make you wonder whether you may be depressed.

If you have thoughts of suicide or self-harm, seek urgent support rather than treating it as a seasonal lifestyle problem.

SAD is a real form of depression and can be treated.

The HSE lists treatment options including talking therapies, antidepressant medication and light therapy, depending on the person and severity of symptoms.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why am I more tired in autumn?

Shorter daylight, changes in body-clock signals, less outdoor activity, changed routines and poorer sleep can all contribute. But autumn tiredness is not specific enough to diagnose a cause, and persistent fatigue can have many medical or psychological explanations.

### Can darker evenings make you tired?

They can indirectly. Darkness may change circadian cues and also changes behaviour: people often spend less time outdoors, move less and change their routines. The evidence linking real-world light exposure directly to mood and sleep is suggestive but not definitive.

### When do the clocks go back in Ireland in 2026?

Ireland's clocks go back by one hour on Sunday, 25 October 2026.

### What is seasonal affective disorder?

Seasonal affective disorder, or SAD, is a type of depression with a seasonal pattern, usually beginning in autumn or winter and improving in spring.

### Is feeling tired in winter the same as SAD?

No. Tiredness alone is not enough to diagnose SAD. SAD involves depressive symptoms and a recurring seasonal pattern that can significantly affect daily life.

### Does going outside help with winter tiredness?

The HSE recommends getting natural sunlight and staying active, particularly outdoors. Early-day light also helps provide a timing signal to the body clock. It is sensible advice, but it is not a guaranteed treatment for every cause of fatigue.

### Do SAD lamps work?

Light therapy is a recognised SAD treatment and some systematic reviews find benefit, particularly from bright white light. Evidence is not perfect, and light boxes are not suitable for everyone. Follow HSE or medical advice rather than treating any bright lamp as therapy.

### Should I take vitamin D for winter tiredness?

Vitamin D supplementation is recommended in Ireland for specific nutritional and bone-health reasons, but taking vitamin D should not be treated as a guaranteed solution to seasonal tiredness or low mood.

## The bottom line

Autumn can genuinely feel different.

Light is one part of the explanation.

But the biggest seasonal change may be the combination of:

**less daylight + less movement + fewer outdoor plans + altered sleep + more time indoors.**

That is useful because several of those factors are changeable.

You do not need to defeat winter.

A better goal is to stop winter quietly removing all of the things that were helping you in summer.

Keep a little morning light.

Use the daylight in the middle of the day.

Protect movement and social contact.

Keep sleep reasonably regular.

And if low mood or fatigue becomes persistent or starts interfering with life, do not dismiss it as “just the dark evenings”.
