*Updated 29 October 2026 with Amazon's third-quarter results. This expands our existing profit-quality analysis; the original publication date and URL are preserved.*

Amazon's cloud business is accelerating. Its cash generation after investment is moving in the opposite direction. That tension is the most useful way to read tonight's results: **stronger operating performance does not automatically mean more cash available to shareholders.**

The [SEC-furnished release](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000101872426000038/amzn-20260930xex991.htm) reports Q3 sales of **$201.944 billion**, operating income of **$28.076 billion** and diluted earnings of **$1.89 a share**. Revenue rose about 12%, while the operating margin reached 13.9%. Yet net income declined from $21.187 billion to $20.685 billion.

## Why profit can fall while the business improves

The year-earlier comparison included $9.5 billion of pre-tax Anthropic investment gains. That makes headline net-income growth a poor standalone measure of progress in retail and cloud services. It also reinforces the distinction we made in our original analysis: investment revaluations, operating earnings and cash flow answer different questions.

A pre-tax investment gain cannot simply be subtracted from after-tax profit to calculate an accurate adjusted result. Read the reconciliation before treating either number as a clean measure of recurring earnings.

## AWS is doing more of the heavy lifting

AWS generated **$46.0 billion of revenue**, up **39.3%**, and **$17.8 billion of operating income**. Using the rounded segment numbers, its operating margin was approximately 38.7%. It supplied roughly 63% of group operating profit while accounting for about 23% of revenue.

That concentration gives investors a practical test. Cloud demand matters, but so does the profit retained from each additional dollar of cloud revenue. A faster growth rate can coexist with a changing margin or a much larger investment requirement. AWS operating income is not its standalone free cash flow.

Retail comparisons need care too: Amazon moved Prime Day into Q2 in most large markets. Management estimates that excluding this timing shift would add roughly four percentage points to Q3 revenue growth. That is management's counterfactual comparison, not additional reported sales.

## The cash test is getting harder

Trailing-twelve-month operating cash flow was **$168.3 billion**; free cash flow was **negative $42.4 billion**. The latter compares with a $7.6 billion outflow at June, disclosed in the [Q2 release](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-earnings-q2-2026-report). These are rolling twelve-month windows, not individual quarterly cash flows.

The arithmetic implies roughly **$210.7 billion** of net property-and-equipment spending under Amazon's free-cash-flow definition: $168.3 billion minus negative $42.4 billion. That is not interchangeable with management's roughly $220 billion cash-capex expectation for calendar 2026; the periods and definitions differ.

For shareholders, the question is whether new capacity earns enough over its life to justify the cash committed today. Negative free cash flow can accompany attractive investment. It can also conceal weak returns if utilisation or pricing disappoints. Revenue growth alone cannot decide between those outcomes.

## What to watch next

Amazon guides Q4 sales to **$240–247 billion** and operating income to **$28.5–33.5 billion**. Those are forecasts, not reported results.

The next useful evidence is whether cloud expansion sustains margins, retail timing effects unwind, and investment begins converting into stronger cash returns. This quarter supports the operating-growth case. It leaves the capital-return case open.
