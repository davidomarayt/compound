**Investing in Ireland 101 · Chapter 6 of 9**

[← Diversification](/wealth/diversification-portfolio-ireland/) · [Course hub](/wealth/how-to-start-investing-in-ireland/) · [Next: Investment fees →](/wealth/investment-fees-ireland/)

The cheapest trade is not necessarily the cheapest investment account.

A slick app is not necessarily the safest legal structure.

And the platform with the longest list of products is not automatically the right platform for a long-term Irish investor.

The job is to compare the **whole service**.

## Start with the legal entity, not the brand

Investment platforms often advertise under a consumer-facing brand while the regulated service is provided by a particular legal entity.

Before sending money, identify:

- the exact legal entity;
- the country in which it is authorised;
- the regulator;
- the entity that holds client money;
- and the entity that holds securities.

The Central Bank of Ireland maintains registers of regulated firms. Firms operating in Ireland from another EEA state can also fall under cross-border authorisation arrangements.

Do not assume an app-store listing or Irish website automatically means the firm is authorised by the Central Bank itself.

## What regulation does—and does not—mean

The Central Bank says regulated firms providing investment advice must follow requirements around suitability, risk and clear communications.

But regulation does not mean:

- your investments cannot fall;
- a recommended product cannot disappoint;
- every product sold by a regulated firm is itself regulated in the same way;
- or all losses are compensated.

Regulation is an important layer of protection. It is not a guarantee of investment performance.

## Investor compensation is not market-loss insurance

For eligible clients of covered Irish-authorised investment firms, the Investor Compensation Scheme can compensate **90% of the net loss up to a maximum of €20,000** when a firm is unable to return money or investment instruments owed to the client.

The Central Bank is explicit that this does not compensate ordinary market losses or poor investment performance.

If you use a provider authorised elsewhere in the EEA, check which compensation scheme and legal protections apply to that entity.

## Custody: where are the investments actually held?

This is one of the least glamorous and most important questions.

Ask the broker:

- Are client assets segregated from company assets?
- Who is the custodian?
- Are securities registered individually, in nominee form or through an omnibus account?
- What happens to the assets if the broker fails?
- Can holdings be transferred to another broker?
- Are there transfer fees?

The answers matter more than the colour of the trading interface.

## Compare the complete fee stack

A platform advertising “€0 commission” can still charge through other routes.

Potential costs include:

| Cost | What to check |
|---|---|
| Dealing commission | Fixed or percentage per trade |
| FX conversion | Explicit fee plus currency spread |
| Platform fee | Flat or percentage of assets |
| Custody fee | Charge for holding securities |
| Fund fee | Ongoing charge inside an ETF/fund |
| Bid–ask spread | Difference between market buy/sell prices |
| Inactivity fee | Charge for not trading |
| Transfer-out fee | Cost to move assets elsewhere |
| Market-data fee | Optional or required pricing feeds |

Use the [Investment Fee Calculator](/investment-fee-calculator/) to compare annual costs over a long horizon.

## Foreign-exchange fees deserve special attention

Many Irish investors buy securities priced in US dollars or sterling.

If the broker converts euro every time you buy or receive a dividend, a small FX percentage can become a recurring drag.

For example, a 1% FX cost on a €500 monthly investment is €5 each month, or €60 a year, before any fund or platform fee.

Over decades, recurring friction matters.

## Fractional shares: useful, but understand the structure

Fractional shares can make recurring investing easier when one full share costs more than the amount you want to invest.

But check:

- whether you legally own a fraction of the underlying security or a contractual interest;
- whether fractions can be transferred to another broker;
- how dividends are handled;
- and what happens if you close the account.

Convenience is useful. Portability can matter later.

## Recurring investing

Automation can be one of the most valuable platform features.

A broker that supports:

- scheduled deposits;
- scheduled purchases;
- clear confirmations;
- and easy record export

can reduce the amount of monthly decision-making.

But automation does not rescue a poor product. It simply makes your chosen process easier to repeat.

## Tax records are a real feature in Ireland

A broker can be cheap and still create expensive administrative work.

Before choosing a platform, check whether you can export:

- purchase date;
- quantity;
- euro cost;
- original-currency cost;
- FX conversion;
- commission;
- disposal proceeds;
- dividends;
- tax withheld;
- and complete transaction history.

For fund investments, you may also need to track acquisition lots and deemed-disposal dates.

A clean CSV export can be more useful than a flashy performance chart.

## Does the broker calculate Irish tax for you?

Do not assume so.

International brokers often provide transaction statements but leave Irish tax reporting to the investor.

Even when a platform shows “tax documents”, they may be designed for another jurisdiction.

Check what the broker actually supplies for an Irish resident.

## Product availability can create hidden risk

More products are not always better.

Access to:

- leverage;
- CFDs;
- options;
- inverse ETFs;
- highly concentrated thematic products;
- crypto derivatives

can encourage a beginner to take risks that were never part of the original plan.

A good long-term platform does not need to make speculation effortless.

## What about “best broker in Ireland” lists?

They can be useful for discovery.

But the word **best** hides assumptions.

Best for:

- monthly ETF investing?
- direct Irish shares?
- US shares?
- pension investing?
- low FX costs?
- tax reporting?
- large portfolios?
- beginner simplicity?

The useful answer is a comparison against your own requirements.

## Broker red flags

Be cautious when a provider:

- makes regulation difficult to verify;
- promises unusually high or guaranteed returns;
- pressures you to deposit immediately;
- promotes leverage as routine;
- makes withdrawal rules unclear;
- or cannot explain where client assets are held.

The Central Bank regularly warns consumers about unregulated investment products and firms.

## A five-minute due-diligence routine

Before opening an account:

1. Find the exact legal entity.
2. Verify authorisation.
3. Read the custody/client-assets explanation.
4. Download the fee schedule.
5. Find the FX fee.
6. Find the transfer-out process.
7. Check what records can be exported.
8. Confirm which investor-compensation regime applies.
9. Read what happens if the firm stops trading.
10. Only then compare the user interface.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best investment platform in Ireland?

There is no universally best platform. Regulation, custody, fees, FX costs, product range, tax records and transfer options matter differently depending on how you invest.

### Should an Irish investor only use an Irish broker?

Not necessarily. EEA firms may provide services cross-border, but you should understand the legal entity, regulator and compensation regime that applies.

### Does the Investor Compensation Scheme cover stock-market losses?

No. The Central Bank states that the scheme is for eligible situations where an investment firm cannot return client money or instruments, not for ordinary market losses.

### Are zero-commission brokers actually free?

Not necessarily. Check FX, spreads, platform fees, custody costs, fund charges and transfer fees.
