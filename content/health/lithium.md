## At a glance

Lithium is a mood stabiliser used in certain mood disorders. It requires regular blood tests and a plan for what to do if illness, other medicines or fluid intake changes. [HSE guide](https://www2.hse.ie/medicines/lithium/)

## The Science Behind the Drug

### Brain signalling and a narrow therapeutic range

Lithium influences several intracellular signalling systems. Proposed effects include changes in inositol metabolism and kinase signalling, but the full link to mood stabilisation is incompletely understood. Lithium is cleared mainly by the kidneys. Changes in kidney handling of salt and water can change lithium levels and toxicity risk. [HSE source](https://www2.hse.ie/medicines/lithium/)

## What the evidence measures

Treatment reviews combine symptom course and relapse prevention with a blood level taken at the specified time relative to the dose. Kidney function, thyroid function and other monitoring provide different information about long-term safety.

## Keep a record with each blood level

| What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Dose time | record when the last dose was taken |
| Context | note recent illness and changes to prescribed or over-the-counter medicines |


## Why does the time of a blood sample matter?

A blood level changes after a dose. The clinic wants a measurement at a defined time so it can compare the result with previous tests and the range used for that treatment plan.

## When to ask for help or a medication review

New coarse tremor, confusion, marked drowsiness, poor coordination, or vomiting and diarrhoea can signal toxicity. Seek urgent medical advice if these occur. Check anti-inflammatory painkillers, diuretics and blood pressure medicines with the care team. [HSE patient guidance](https://www2.hse.ie/medicines/lithium/)
