An extra €100 can look small beside a six-figure mortgage. It is also €1,200 a year that has to come from somewhere. Before committing it, you deserve a clearer answer than “every little helps”.

Here is one: on an illustrative **€250,000 mortgage with 25 years remaining at 4%**, paying an extra €100 each month could save approximately **€18,619 in interest** and finish repayments **two years and ten months earlier**.

That result assumes the rate never changes, payments stay up, every extra euro reduces the balance, and no fees apply. It is a calculation, not a lender’s offer. Those assumptions are where the useful conversation starts.

## What the extra money actually does

A repayment mortgage gradually pays back the amount borrowed, while also covering interest. At the beginning of our example, the first month’s interest is about €833. The scheduled €1,319.59 payment therefore reduces the balance by roughly €486.

An additional €100 takes that reduction to approximately €586. Next month, interest is calculated on a slightly smaller amount. Continue making the larger payment and more of each later payment can go towards clearing the debt.

There is no special savings account inside the mortgage. You are borrowing less, sooner. The CCPC says extra payments must be applied to the capital balance to deliver the expected benefit; without clear instructions, money can instead remain in a credit or suspense account. [CCPC overpayment guidance](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/buying-a-home/about-mortgages/paying-extra-off-your-mortgage).

Without overpayments, this model produces approximately €145,878 of interest over the remaining term. With the extra €100, that falls to €127,258. The saving is spread across future years; it is not a cash payment you receive today.

The final repayment arrives in month 266 rather than month 300. The comparison counts actual monthly payment dates, including a smaller final payment, rather than pretending that a mortgage is cleared partway through a payment month.

A different balance, rate or remaining term changes the answer. So does stopping the extra payments. Use the calculator to see the scale for your own situation before deciding whether the monthly commitment is comfortable.

## The fixed-rate detail that matters

Do not assume an allowance to overpay also gives you the right to shorten the contractual term without a charge. A lender can accept extra capital but recalculate a lower monthly payment over the existing term. That still saves interest, but it is a different outcome from the calculator above.

AIB provides a useful example. Its published fixed-rate allowance permits up to **€5,000 per calendar year** without an early repayment charge under the allowance, with monthly repayments reduced and the remaining term unchanged. Its guidance separately says requesting a shorter term while on a fixed rate requires breaking out of that rate and may attract a fee. This is AIB’s published arrangement, not a rule for every Irish mortgage. [AIB’s full conditions](https://www.aib.ie/our-products/mortgages/flexible-payment-options).

Before transferring money, ask your lender:

- What overpayment is allowed under my particular agreement, and what charges apply?
- Will it reduce capital immediately, lower my required payment, or change the term?
- Can I keep paying the original amount plus €100, and how should that be arranged?

Get the answer for your loan, particularly if it has separate fixed and variable portions. The calculator cannot interpret a mortgage contract.

## Keep some money outside the house

A lower mortgage balance will not pay a mechanic’s invoice next week. Before committing spare money, consider essential bills, upcoming expenses and an accessible cash buffer. Do not assume an overpayment can later be withdrawn.

Also look at other borrowing. Clearing expensive credit-card or personal-loan debt may save more interest per euro than reducing a cheaper mortgage, subject to any repayment charges. The CCPC recommends comparing debt costs with savings returns and planning around what you can actually afford. [CCPC savings guidance](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/saving-and-investments/savings).

There is no virtue in overpaying €100 and then borrowing it back at a higher rate to get through the month. A smaller sustainable amount—or waiting until your finances are steadier—can be a more sensible choice.

## What if you saved the money instead?

Compare savings interest after tax, while also valuing access to cash. For a simple illustration, a deposit paying 4% gross leaves 2.68% after 33% DIRT alone: €40 of interest on €1,000 becomes €26.80. Revenue lists a 33% DIRT rate; exemptions exist, and PRSI can also apply in some circumstances. [DIRT rate](https://www.revenue.ie/en/additional-incomes/dirt/what-dirt-rate-is-applicable.aspx) · [Revenue’s deposit-interest overview](https://www.revenue.ie/en/additional-incomes/dirt/index.aspx).

This arithmetic is not a comparison of current bank offers. Nor does it make mortgage overpayment automatically superior: cash available for a broken boiler serves a different purpose from money committed to your home.

Pension contributions are another separate decision, involving tax relief, possible employer contributions and restricted access. Our [pension-tax-relief guide](https://compound.ie/wealth/pension-tax-relief-ireland-how-to-claim/) explains that side of the choice. An overpayment calculator cannot rank all these priorities for you.

## Check the rate as well as the repayment

Paying extra is only one route to reducing mortgage costs. Reviewing your existing lender’s options or switching could help without requiring the same extra monthly outlay. Compare the overall cost, including legal or valuation costs and any fixed-rate break charge, rather than choosing on cashback alone. [CCPC switching guidance](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/buying-a-home/about-mortgages/switching-your-mortgage).

For the next step, take the calculator result and your mortgage statement to the lender. Ask what the same plan would mean under your actual agreement. If the fees, payment treatment and household budget all work, you can make the decision with a clearer picture of what that €100 is buying: less interest, and potentially fewer years of repayments.
