The Central Bank sets the outer mortgage limits.

Your lender decides whether it is willing to lend to **you**.

Those are not the same thing.

A first-time buyer might be within the 4× income rule and still be approved for less because the lender's affordability assessment takes account of:

- existing debt;
- childcare;
- dependants;
- age;
- employment;
- income stability;
- spending;
- savings history;
- credit history;
- and the likely mortgage repayment.

## The current income limits

The standard Loan-to-Income limits are:

<div class="home-number-strip">
<div><span>First-time buyer</span><strong>4×</strong><p>Maximum standard LTI multiple of gross income.</p></div>
<div><span>Second/subsequent buyer</span><strong>3.5×</strong><p>Maximum standard LTI multiple of gross income.</p></div>
</div>

A first-time buyer household with €90,000 of qualifying gross income has a standard LTI ceiling of:

**€90,000 × 4 = €360,000.**

A second/subsequent buyer household on the same income has:

**€90,000 × 3.5 = €315,000.**

Use the [Mortgage Borrowing Calculator](/mortgage-borrowing-calculator/) to model the rule.

## The 10% deposit rule still applies

For principal-home purchases, first-time and second/subsequent buyers are generally limited to 90% Loan-to-Value.

That means the property price must satisfy both:

**income rule** and **deposit rule**.

Example:

First-time buyer income: €80,000  
LTI maximum: €320,000  
Savings available for deposit: €60,000

A €380,000 home requires a €38,000 minimum deposit.

The deposit is sufficient.

But the €320,000 LTI maximum means the buyer would still need €60,000 of their own money toward the price.

The tighter constraint wins.

## Lenders can approve less than the macroprudential limit

The Central Bank rules are not a promise.

A lender performs its own affordability assessment.

The CCPC says lenders examine:

- income;
- savings habits;
- spending;
- credit history;
- and ability to make repayments.

If the lender believes the repayment is not affordable, the approved mortgage can be below the 4× or 3.5× maximum.

## Approval in Principle

Before serious bidding, get **Approval in Principle (AIP)**.

AIP is an initial indication of:

- whether the lender is prepared to lend;
- and approximately how much.

It is useful because:

- you know your working budget;
- estate agents may ask whether finance is approved;
- and you can bid with more confidence.

But AIP is not final mortgage approval.

## How long does AIP last?

The CCPC says AIP is typically valid for around **6 to 12 months**, depending on the lender.

If it expires, you may need an extension or updated assessment.

Your circumstances also matter.

A job change, new loan, missed payment or large new financial commitment can affect the lender's final decision.

## The lender checks twice

The CCPC describes two broad stages:

### First check — AIP

The lender assesses you.

### Second check — property and final finances

After you go sale agreed, the lender:

- reviews your finances again;
- checks the property;
- receives the valuation;
- confirms required documents;
- and issues a formal Letter of Offer once satisfied.

That is why "mortgage approved" during house hunting often really means AIP.

## Mortgage allowances / exceptions

The Central Bank permits lenders to have a limited share of new lending above the standard macroprudential limits.

Currently:

- up to 15% of first-time-buyer lending may be above the limits;
- up to 15% of second/subsequent-buyer lending may be above the limits;
- up to 10% of buy-to-let lending may be above the limits.

This is an allowance for lenders.

It is **not a borrower entitlement**.

A lender can have different internal criteria for how exceptions are allocated.

Do not construct a home-buying budget that only works if an exception is granted.

## Income is not always treated the same way

A lender may treat income differently depending on its nature.

Examples:

- basic salary;
- overtime;
- bonus;
- commission;
- self-employed income;
- contract income;
- rental income;
- allowances.

The Central Bank limit uses gross income, but the lender decides what income it accepts for underwriting.

Two lenders can therefore arrive at different approvable incomes for the same household.

## Self-employed applicants

Self-employed buyers usually need stronger evidence of sustainable income over time.

Expect to provide records such as:

- tax returns;
- Revenue documents;
- business accounts;
- bank statements;
- proof of tax compliance;
- and information on business liabilities.

The exact documentation varies by lender and business structure.

The important point is to prepare early.

## Existing loans reduce affordability

Car finance, personal loans, credit-card balances and other commitments matter even if you remain under the LTI ceiling.

A €400 monthly car repayment is €4,800 of annual household cash flow already committed before the mortgage.

That can reduce the lender's affordability assessment.

Clearing expensive debt can therefore improve both:

- household resilience;
- and mortgage underwriting.

## Savings history matters

Lenders want evidence that you can sustain the proposed housing cost.

Regular monthly saving helps demonstrate repayment capacity.

So can:

- existing rent;
- current loan repayments that will end;
- or another proven recurring financial commitment.

The relevant calculation differs by lender.

But a stable, explainable bank account is easier to underwrite than one with repeated overdrafts and unexplained borrowing.

## Stress the repayment yourself

Do not stop at the lender's approval amount.

Use the [Mortgage Calculator](/mortgage-calculator/) to test the mortgage at:

- the expected rate;
- a rate 1 percentage point higher;
- and a rate 2 percentage points higher.

Then ask whether the household still works.

A fixed rate can protect you for a period, but the mortgage may last decades.

## Borrowing maximum versus affordability maximum

These are three different ceilings:

<div class="home-principle-grid">
<div><span>01</span><strong>Central Bank ceiling</strong><p>The macroprudential LTI/LTV rules.</p></div>
<div><span>02</span><strong>Lender ceiling</strong><p>What the lender approves after underwriting.</p></div>
<div><span>03</span><strong>Your ceiling</strong><p>The repayment and purchase price that still leave room for life.</p></div>
</div>

The purchase price should respect the lowest relevant ceiling.

## Bridging loans: a 2026 change

In 2026, the Central Bank amended the mortgage measures so qualifying **principal-home bridging loans** are exempt from the LTI limit.

The exemption is narrowly defined and does not mean ordinary second-home borrowing is exempt.

LTV rules continue to apply.

This is primarily relevant to home movers buying before selling an existing principal residence.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much can a first-time buyer borrow in Ireland?

The standard Central Bank LTI limit is four times gross income. A lender may approve less based on affordability.

### Is AIP a guaranteed mortgage?

No. AIP is preliminary. Full approval comes after further financial checks and property checks.

### Can I borrow more than four times income?

Lenders have a limited allowance to lend above standard limits, but an exception is not guaranteed.

### Does rent count toward repayment capacity?

Lenders can consider demonstrated rent and savings history as evidence of repayment capacity, but underwriting rules vary.
