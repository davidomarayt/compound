**Ireland had a record 2,884,100 people in employment in the third quarter of 2026, up 58,700 in a year, according to the latest official Labour Force Survey.**

The headline is strong: employment rose 2.1%, the unemployment rate fell from 5.3% to 5.0%, and total hours worked increased.

The household picture is more mixed. The [CSO release](https://www.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-lfs/labourforcesurveyquarter32026/keyfindings/) also shows **130,200 part-time workers wanted more hours and more pay**, while long-term unemployment increased to 37,100.

That combination matters. A person can be counted as employed while still being unable to secure the hours or income their household needs.

## Ireland's labour market at a glance

| Q3 2026 measure | Result | Annual change |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| People in the labour force | 3,034,800 | +53,900 / +1.8% |
| People in employment | 2,884,100 | +58,700 / +2.1% |
| Employment rate, age 15–64 | 75.4% | +0.7 percentage points |
| People unemployed | 150,700 | −4,800 / −3.1% |
| Unemployment rate | 5.0% | −0.3 percentage points |
| Hours worked per week | 89.2 million | +3.1% |

The labour force passed three million for the first time in the comparable series. The CSO estimates that demographic change added 31,800 people over the year and higher participation added a further 22,100.

Those figures describe the size and activity of the labour market. They do not establish that living standards or real take-home pay rose by the same percentages.

## One in five workers was part-time

There were 578,200 people working part-time, equal to **20% of everyone in employment**. Of that group, 130,200 were classified as underemployed because they wanted to work more hours for more pay.

That means **22.5% of part-time workers**, or roughly one in 4.4, were underemployed.

The distinction is important. Some people choose part-time work for study, caring, health or personal reasons. Underemployment isolates the group saying the hours available are not enough.

Consider a worker paid €16 an hour who wants 35 hours but receives 22:

> 13 missing hours × €16 = **€208 less gross pay each week**

Across a 52-week year, that illustrative gap is €10,816 before tax. The CSO does not report that every underemployed person faces this rate or hours gap; the example simply shows why being “employed” can still leave a material household-income shortfall.

Workers comparing different schedules can use Compound's [salary-to-hourly-rate calculator](/salary-hourly-rate-calculator/) and [take-home-pay calculator](/take-home-pay-calculator/) to separate headline salary from hours and net income.

## Long-term unemployment moved in the wrong direction

Total unemployment fell to 150,700, but its duration changed:

- short-term unemployment fell by 5,200 to 108,500; and
- long-term unemployment rose by 2,600, or 7.6%, to **37,100**.

Long-term unemployment accounted for 25.5% of cases where duration was stated, up from 23.2% a year earlier. The long-term unemployment rate itself remained 1.2%.

This is not inconsistent with a falling overall unemployment rate. New or short spells of unemployment can decline while a smaller group remains out of work for longer.

For households, duration can be more important than the national rate. Savings run down, eligibility can change and returning to work can involve transport, clothing and childcare costs. ## Youth unemployment improved, but remained high

There were 48,900 unemployed people aged 15 to 24, down 5,800 in a year. The youth unemployment rate fell from 14.1% to **12.6%**.

That is a meaningful improvement, but the rate remained more than twice the 5.0% all-age measure. Education participation and the smaller youth labour force affect this comparison, so it should not be read as 12.6% of every young person being out of work.

## Which sectors gained and lost employment?

The largest reported increase was in **Education**, up 30,200 people or 12.9%. The largest decline was in **Professional, Scientific & Technical Activities**, down 19,500 or 9.5%.

Quarterly survey estimates by sector can move and the CSO introduced the revised NACE Rev. 2.1 classification in 2026. These changes are useful signals, not proof that every occupation within the broad sectors followed the same direction.

The number of employees rose by 69,500 to 2,504,700, while self-employment fell by 5,300 to 361,200. People who worked from home declined by 21,200 to 966,600; those who never worked from home increased by 79,500 to 1,907,800.

For a worker considering a new role, that shift makes commuting cost and time part of the pay comparison, not a separate lifestyle footnote.

## LFS employment is not the Live Register

The Labour Force Survey is Ireland's official measure of employment and unemployment using International Labour Organisation criteria. It is based on a large household survey.

The Live Register is an administrative count of people claiming certain jobseeker payments and can include some people working limited hours. It should not be used as a substitute unemployment total.

Similarly, the 2.1% rise in employment is not a 2.1% pay increase. It counts people, while the 3.1% increase in weekly hours is a total across the economy. Neither tells an individual worker what happened to hourly pay, inflation-adjusted income or household costs.

## What workers and jobseekers can do with the data

1. **Compare paid hours, not only salary.** Ask how many hours are guaranteed and how overtime is allocated.
2. **Price the commute.** More office-based work can change fuel, public transport, parking and childcare costs.
3. **Use sector figures as context, not a promise.** Hiring conditions differ sharply within each broad category.
4. **If part-time involuntarily, document availability.** A clear record of extra shifts requested can support a discussion with an employer or a search for a second role.
5. **If unemployed long term, check current entitlements and activation supports.** Eligibility depends on contribution history, means and circumstances, not the national unemployment rate.

Ireland's record employment level is real and positive. So is the fall in total unemployment. But the new survey also shows why the word “employed” is not a complete measure of household security: hours, pay, permanence and the duration of any unemployment spell still determine what reaches the family budget.
