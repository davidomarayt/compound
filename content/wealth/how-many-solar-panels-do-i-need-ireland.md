**The short answer:** A 4kWp array might suit a smaller household, while 6kWp is worth comparing for a family with meaningful daytime electricity use. An 8kWp array can be sensible for a larger, more electric home — **but bigger does not automatically mean better payback**. The right answer depends more on your half-hourly electricity use, roof and quote than on the number of bedrooms.

Start with the [Compound Solar Hub](/solar/) for the whole buying journey. If you already have a price from an installer, take that number to the [Solar Payback Calculator](/solar-payback-calculator/) instead of using our example prices below.

## 4kW vs 6kW vs 8kW solar: the numbers at a glance

| | 4kWp system | 6kWp system | 8kWp system |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Approximate 440W panel count | 9 panels | 14 panels | 18 panels |
| Illustrative annual production | **3,400 kWh** | **5,100 kWh** | **6,800 kWh** |
| Example installed price **before** grant | €7,500 | €10,500 | €13,500 |
| Standard SEAI solar grant, **if eligible** | €1,800 | €1,800 | €1,800 |
| Example net cost | **€5,700** | **€8,700** | **€11,700** |
| Annual value: modelled 4,200kWh household | €740 | €1,033 | €1,295 |
| Simple payback of that example | **7.7 years** | **8.4 years** | **9.0 years** |

**Important:** The three prices and self-consumption percentages above are **transparent illustration inputs, not observed 2026 average installation prices or quotes**. Each example excludes batteries and assumes identical roof quality. Export income is counted as value, but does not reduce gross grid consumption. Actual results may differ sharply with shading, location, equipment, tariffs, daytime use and cost.

The €1,800 standard SEAI grant is capped at 4kWp, meaning the modelled 6kWp and 8kWp systems receive **no additional standard PV grant** above 4kWp. Special enhanced supports have different criteria; check the [current SEAI grants](/solar/grants/) for your circumstances.

We use **850 kWh produced per kWp installed per year** as a round example, not a national forecast. SEAI illustrates that a well-located 3kW system can produce about 2,600kWh annually. Your roof pitch, orientation, shading and inverter specification may produce a different result.

## First: how much electricity do you use — and when?

The CRU's 4,200 kWh per year figure is a **tariff-comparison benchmark**. It is not a prediction for your house and cannot tell you how much solar electricity you will directly use.

Before requesting a solar quote, find these numbers:

1. **Annual imported kWh:** ideally from 12 months of supplier bills or [smart-meter data](/wealth/average-home-electricity-use-ireland/).
2. **Daytime load:** electricity used while your roof generates power. A home empty from 9am to 6pm behaves differently from a home with daytime occupancy.
3. **Future demand:** an EV, heat pump, electric hot water or home office may make a larger array more valuable, **if its timing aligns with solar**.
4. **Tariff opportunity cost:** a solar kWh displacing 30c daytime electricity is not worth 30c if it would otherwise have been used for an EV charging at a 10c night rate.

Read our [guide to typical Irish household electricity use](/wealth/average-home-electricity-use-ireland/) before choosing a system purely by the number of bedrooms.

## How much could 4kW, 6kW and 8kW save in a typical home?

Consider a household using **4,200kWh annually**. Compare three different arrays on the same roof, without batteries or EV solar charging.

Our published *model assumptions* are:

| Input | Assumption |
| --- | --- |
| Annual generation | 850 kWh per kWp |
| Electricity you avoid importing | €0.30/kWh |
| Clean Export Guarantee payment on exports | €0.15/kWh |
| Self-use share with 4kWp | 45% of generation |
| Self-use share with 6kWp | 35% of generation |
| Self-use share with 8kWp | 27% of generation |
| Battery | None |
| All-in example prices, before grant | €7,500 / €10,500 / €13,500 |

Why do the assumed self-use percentages fall as the system gets larger? Because the same home can only use so much power at the time it is generated. Adding panels increases midday surplus. The shares are explicitly **scenario inputs**, not measured household averages.

[chart:2]

Here is the calculation underneath the graphic:

| | 4kWp | 6kWp | 8kWp |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Solar used directly in home | 1,530kWh | 1,785kWh | 1,836kWh |
| Exported | 1,870kWh | 3,315kWh | 4,964kWh |
| Value from avoided imports (30c) | €459 | €536 | €551 |
| Export revenue (15c) | €281 | €497 | €745 |
| **Total estimated annual value** | **€740** | **€1,033** | **€1,295** |

Notice what the 8kWp system is doing. It produces twice as much electricity as the 4kWp system, but **most of its extra power is being exported** under these assumptions. Whether that is attractive depends on the export tariff and what the larger installation costs.

## The question installers' headline figures can miss: what does the upgrade earn?

If someone quotes **€7,500 for 4kWp**, **€10,500 for 6kWp** and **€13,500 for 8kWp**, ask two separate questions.

**Question one: Is the whole installation worthwhile?** In our example, the answers are 7.7, 8.4 and 9.0 years of simple payback respectively, using annual value from avoided imports plus exports.

**Question two: Are the *extra* panels worthwhile?** Spending an additional €3,000 to move from 4kWp to 6kWp earns an extra **€293 per year** in the 4,200kWh model. That extra investment has a **10.2-year marginal simple payback**. Moving from 6kWp to 8kWp adds about **€263 annually**, with an **11.4-year marginal simple payback**.

[chart:3]

The bigger option is not necessarily bad. Over a long period, it can deliver greater total cash value, even when the incremental return is lower. But comparing **marginal** returns makes it harder to justify an oversized array with unrealistic bill-saving claims.

These periods are **not discounted investment returns** and exclude maintenance, inverter replacement, output degradation, financing, changing export prices and tariff changes. An installer projection should disclose those assumptions too.

## What changes for an EV or heat-pump home?

A larger array may be more useful where there is genuinely more **daylight** demand — for example, a home with daytime EV charging, a daytime-operating heat pump or regularly occupied rooms.

For comparison only, suppose a high-daytime-demand household directly uses **65% / 57% / 50%** of the 4kWp / 6kWp / 8kWp generation. Keep all other assumptions the same.

[chart:4]

Under that separate model, the 8kWp array produces an estimated **€1,530/year** in combined avoided imports and exports, versus €1,295 in the standard example. Its €11,700 net illustrative cost would give a **7.6-year** simple payback.

But this stronger case requires a real load that overlaps generation. An EV which is parked at work all day and charged cheaply overnight may contribute **almost no additional direct solar self-consumption**. A heat pump's heaviest loads can fall in winter, when solar output is much lower.

**Do not size panels by the EV's annual mileage alone.** For a full comparison of daytime charging, night tariffs and storage, use the [Solar + EV + Battery Optimiser](/solar-ev-battery-optimiser/).

## Will you generate enough electricity in winter?

Annual kWh figures hide a substantial mismatch between production and demand.

SEAI says approximately **75% of annual solar production happens from May to September**. Winter heating and evening lighting demand can be greatest during the months when solar contributes least. More panels help generate more winter units, but **an 8kWp system does not mean you are independent of the grid in December**. For monthly output for 4, 6 and 8 kWp, see our [winter solar generation and bills guide](/wealth/solar-panels-winter-ireland/). It keeps the SEAI general rule of thumb separate from a particular PVGIS-derived Dublin model.

This is especially important if someone tells you that a 6kWp array generating 5,100kWh a year will simply cancel a 5,100kWh annual bill. It will not: some generation occurs when you cannot use it, and grid imports remain necessary at other times.

A battery shifts generation from midday into the evening; it **does not store summer solar energy for the winter**. The separate [€600 battery grant](/solar/grants/) helps eligible households with initial cost, but is not a reason to assume that storage automatically pays for itself. See our [battery economics guide](/wealth/solar-panels-vs-battery-ireland/) and [Battery Payback Calculator](/solar/batteries/).

## Roof area, orientation and grid connection: what can stop you going bigger?

**Panel count and space.** Using 440W modules, approximately 9 / 14 / 18 panels make 4 / 6 / 8kWp respectively. They will need suitable roof area, access margins and mounting clearances. Exact space depends on the module dimensions and roof shape, not just total area.

**Direction and shading.** A shaded south-facing array can underperform a clear east/west roof. Ask for annual kWh from a roof-specific model with shading and inverter losses, **not just a kWp headline**. For the trade-offs, see our [east- vs west- vs south-facing solar roof guide](/wealth/east-west-facing-solar-panels-ireland/).

**Inverter capacity and export.** The peak **DC panel** rating (kWp) is not the same as the inverter's AC capacity or permitted export. The standard ESB Networks microgeneration notification route (NC6) generally covers up to 6kVA single-phase or 11kVA three-phase. An 8kWp DC panel array might be paired with a smaller inverter, but the resulting clipping, approvals and export constraints need assessment. The installer must identify the correct ESB Networks application route before works proceed.

**Planning.** Rooftop solar on houses is generally exempt from planning permission, subject to conditions; protected structures and other sensitive settings may need additional checks. Confirm with the local authority where relevant.

## What grants apply in Ireland in 2026?

For the **standard domestic SEAI Solar PV grant** the published structure is €700 per kWp for the first 2kWp, then €200 per extra kWp up to 4kWp, capped at **€1,800**. Eligible houses generally need an MPRN and to have been built and occupied before 2025, with no previous solar grant at that MPRN.

SEAI separately lists a **€600 grant** for qualifying battery systems of at least 5kWh. There are also enhanced support arrangements for some households on qualifying welfare payments, so do not use the standard table without checking your eligibility.

For qualifying domestic supply-and-install projects, Revenue's **0% VAT** treatment may apply; buying panels as a supply-only purchase has different treatment. **Apply for grant approval before works begin.** Check the official [SEAI grant page](https://www.seai.ie/grants/home-energy-grants/individual-grants/solar-electricity-grant), [Revenue guidance](https://www.revenue.ie/en/vat/vat-on-property-and-construction/construction-fixtures-fittings-solar-panels/solar-panels.aspx) and Compound's [solar grants explainer](/solar/grants/).

## How to choose your size without wasting money

You do not need to guess what an installer considers a 'normal' home. Ask three registered companies for quotes using **the same written brief** and compare the answers side by side.

Your written comparison should show: proposed kWp, panel count and module wattage, inverter AC rating, projected annual kWh, estimated self-use and exports, all-in cost before grant, grant eligibility, battery costs separately, roof works, warranties and who completes the ESB Networks paperwork.

Then follow this decision sequence:

1. **Audit usage.** Use 12 months of bills and, preferably, half-hourly smart-meter data.
2. **Compare 4kWp, 6kWp and 8kWp** using roof-specific generation assumptions from the same installer.
3. **Model actual avoided import and export rates.** Avoid pricing every solar unit at the retail import rate.
4. **Calculate the marginal return** from paying for an extra 2kWp, not just the whole-package payback.
5. **Check installers' numbers.** Use the [Solar Quote Comparator](/solar/compare-quotes/) and [installer checklist](/solar/installers/) before committing.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many solar panels does a 3-bedroom house need in Ireland?

There is no fixed count. A three-bedroom house might have modest daytime demand or significant electric heating and EV charging. Nine 440W panels make approximately 4kWp; fourteen make 6kWp. Start with actual annual and daytime electricity use, roof orientation and a roof-specific production estimate.

### Is 6kW enough for a house with an electric car?

Potentially, but check where and when the EV charges. An EV charged mainly overnight or away from home cannot directly use much midday solar, even if its annual energy demand is high. [Compare EV and solar together](/solar-ev-battery-optimiser/).

### Is an 8kW solar system too big in Ireland?

Not inherently. It can suit high electricity demand, suitable roof space and an attractive export arrangement. What matters is the price and economic value of the **extra capacity**, including inverter and network connection constraints.

### Does an 8kW solar array receive a bigger SEAI grant than 4kW?

Not under the standard domestic PV grant, which is capped at €1,800 by 4kWp. Other eligibility-based schemes and separate battery support can change the overall funding available.

### Are savings from export counted in solar payback?

They can be, but they should be shown separately from avoided grid imports. A unit sold to the supplier is not the same as a unit consumed behind the meter, and the tariffs usually differ.

### Can I eliminate my electricity bill by installing enough solar panels?

Usually not. Solar generation and household demand occur at different times and seasons. Standing charges and other bill components can remain even if substantial energy is generated, and batteries cannot carry summer production through winter.
