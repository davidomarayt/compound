Ireland is preparing to introduce a **Personal Investment Account (PIA)** in 2027.

For Irish investors, it could be one of the biggest structural changes to ordinary investing in years.

The proposed account is designed to let an eligible adult hold investments such as **shares, bonds, funds and ETFs inside one tax framework**. The existing deemed-disposal regime will not apply inside the account, providers are expected to handle the tax administration, and there will be no compulsory holding period.

But the three numbers that will determine how attractive the account actually is have **not yet been announced**:

1. the tax-free threshold;
2. the annual tax rate; and
3. the annual contribution limit.

Those are due to be announced in **Budget 2027 on 6 October 2026**.

So this is not a page predicting what the Government might do. It is a living guide to what is confirmed, what remains unknown, and what the new account could change for an Irish investor.

> **Current status — 25 September 2026:** the framework has been announced, but the account is not yet available to open. The key tax figures are expected in Budget 2027. Compound will update this page when the official numbers are published.

## Personal Investment Account Ireland: the short version

| Question | What we know so far |
|---|---|
| When is it expected to launch? | 2027 |
| Who is expected to qualify? | Irish residents aged 18+ with a PPS number |
| How many accounts? | The published outline provides for one account per eligible person |
| What can it hold? | Listed shares, listed bonds, regulated-market instruments and suitable investment funds, including ETFs |
| Can it hold crypto? | No, under the current proposal |
| Can it hold derivatives? | No, under the current proposal |
| Minimum contribution? | None announced |
| Annual contribution cap? | Yes, but the amount is not yet announced |
| Lock-in period? | None |
| Tax-free amount? | Yes, but the threshold is not yet announced |
| Tax above that amount? | A low flat annual rate is proposed; the rate is not yet announced |
| Deemed disposal inside the PIA? | No, under the published framework |
| Who handles the tax? | Providers are expected to calculate, report and pay tax due to Revenue |
| Can you switch provider? | Transfers are intended to be tax-neutral where possible |
| Is your return guaranteed? | No. The value of investments can rise or fall |

That final point matters.

A Personal Investment Account is a **tax and administration wrapper**. It does not turn an investment into a deposit, guarantee a return or remove market risk.

## Why Ireland is creating a Personal Investment Account

Irish households save a lot, but relatively little household wealth is invested directly in capital markets.

The Central Bank of Ireland found that Irish households held **38% of their financial assets in cash and deposits**, compared with an EU average of 30%. It also found that just **2.3%** of Irish household financial assets were held in direct investments such as listed equity and debt securities, compared with an EU average of 7.5%.

More recently, Central Bank banking data showed the stock of Irish household deposits at **€176.4 billion at the end of July 2026**.

The Department of Finance says the PIA is intended to make retail investing simpler and more accessible while supporting long-term financial resilience.

That does not mean cash is bad. Emergency funds and money needed in the short term generally serve a different purpose from long-term investments.

The policy question is whether people who *do* want to invest should face a simpler route than the current mix of CGT, dividend taxation, exit tax, deemed disposal and self-administered reporting.

For the wider foundations, start with [Investing in Ireland 101](/wealth/how-to-start-investing-in-ireland/).

## What investments will be allowed?

The published framework says eligible assets are expected to include:

- listed company shares;
- listed bonds;
- financial instruments traded on a regulated market;
- investment funds suitable for retail investors; and
- exchange-traded funds (ETFs).

Highly complex or higher-risk products including **crypto assets and derivatives** are not expected to qualify.

The final rules may contain more detailed eligibility criteria, so an ETF being available through a broker does not automatically mean it will be eligible for a PIA.

If you are new to the terminology, [Shares, ETFs and Funds in Ireland](/wealth/shares-etfs-funds-ireland/) explains what each structure actually is before tax enters the picture.

## The biggest change: no deemed disposal inside the PIA

For many Irish investors, this is the headline feature.

Under the existing Irish tax regime, many Irish-domiciled funds and equivalent offshore funds can be subject to the investment-fund tax regime rather than ordinary Capital Gains Tax.

Revenue confirmed that the individual rate applying to relevant Irish-domiciled investment funds and equivalent offshore funds in qualifying jurisdictions fell from 41% to **38% from 1 January 2026**.

That regime can also include **eight-year deemed disposal**: tax can become due at an eight-year point even though the investor has not actually sold the investment.

The Department of Finance framework for the Personal Investment Account says the existing investment tax regime, including deemed disposal, **will not apply to investments held inside the PIA**.

Instead, a new annual tax structure will apply to the account.

That is an important distinction: deemed disposal is not currently being abolished for every Irish investor or every ETF. The exemption is part of the proposed PIA framework.

The Government has deferred wider consideration of the existing retail-investment tax regime, including deemed disposal, beyond Budget 2027.

For the current rules outside the PIA, see [Investment Tax in Ireland: CGT, ETFs, Dividends and Deemed Disposal](/wealth/investment-tax-ireland/).

## How will the new PIA tax work?

The outline published so far says the account will have:

- a **tax-free threshold**;
- a **low flat annual tax rate** on the value above that threshold; and
- an **annual maximum contribution limit**.

All three figures remain unknown.

The tax structure is therefore different from simply charging tax when you sell an investment for a profit.

Based on the framework currently described, the basic annual calculation would be:

**Taxable PIA value = account value − tax-free threshold**

**Annual PIA tax = taxable PIA value × annual PIA tax rate**

If the account value is below the tax-free threshold, no PIA tax would be due under the announced framework.

However, until the legislation and Budget figures are published, it would be misleading to put a euro tax bill beside a €5,000, €10,000 or €25,000 portfolio.

## A crucial detail: the proposed tax is based on account value

This is easy to miss.

The framework described publicly is for a flat annual charge on the **value of the account above the tax-free threshold**, rather than a tax only on realised profit.

That means the final rate and threshold matter enormously.

A low annual percentage applied to account value behaves very differently from a larger percentage applied only to gains when an investment is sold.

It also means provider fees will matter alongside tax. A seemingly small annual tax plus platform fees plus fund charges can compound over a long investment period.

Use Compound's [Investment Fee Calculator](/investment-fee-calculator/) to see how recurring percentage charges affect long-term values.

## Will providers handle the tax?

That is the intention.

Under the announced framework, participating financial providers are expected to calculate, report and pay the PIA tax to Revenue on the investor's behalf.

For many people, that administrative simplification could be as important as the headline tax rate.

The existing system can require an investor to understand which tax regime applies to a product, calculate liabilities, retain records and meet filing deadlines.

The PIA is intended to centralise more of that administration with the provider.

The final legislation will determine exactly what information an investor still needs to report personally.

## Can you withdraw the money whenever you want?

The announced framework contains **no minimum holding period or lock-in period**.

That makes the PIA structurally different from a pension.

However, "no lock-in" does not mean money invested in markets should automatically be treated as short-term cash.

If the value of an ETF or share portfolio falls 25% just before you need the money, the fact that you are legally allowed to withdraw it does not remove that loss.

Time horizon and risk still matter.

Read [Before You Invest in Ireland](/wealth/before-you-invest-ireland/) before deciding whether money belongs in investments at all.

## Personal Investment Account vs normal taxable investing vs pension

The PIA is not simply a replacement for every other investment account.

| Feature | Personal Investment Account | Normal taxable investing | PRSA / pension | Deposit savings |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main purpose | Flexible investing | Flexible investing | Retirement | Short-term savings / cash |
| Expected availability | 2027 | Available now | Available now | Available now |
| Access | No proposed lock-in | Generally flexible | Restricted by pension rules | Generally flexible, subject to product |
| Upfront income-tax relief | Not announced | No | Can apply to qualifying pension contributions within limits | No |
| Tax on investments | New annual PIA regime | Depends on asset/product | Investment income and gains not taxed as they arise inside a PRSA | Interest generally subject to deposit tax rules |
| Deemed disposal | Not under proposed PIA rules | Can apply to relevant funds/ETFs | Not in the same way inside the pension wrapper | Not applicable |
| Investment choice | Eligible shares, bonds, funds/ETFs | Broad, provider-dependent | Provider/fund-dependent | Cash products |
| Market risk | Yes | Yes | Yes | Normally much lower; deposit protection rules may apply |
| Contribution limit | Annual cap planned; amount unknown | No PIA-specific cap | Tax-relief limits and pension rules apply | Product-dependent |

A pension can still have a major advantage for retirement saving because qualifying contributions may receive **income-tax relief**, and the Pensions Authority states that investment income and capital gains are not taxed as they arise inside a PRSA.

The trade-off is access: a pension is designed for retirement.

The PIA is being designed as a more flexible investment wrapper.

That makes the real question less "PIA or pension?" and more **"what is this money for, and when might I need it?"**

See [Pension vs Investing in Ireland](/wealth/pension-vs-investing-ireland/) for that decision framework.

## How does the PIA compare with buying shares directly?

Outside the PIA, direct shares commonly fall under Capital Gains Tax rules.

Revenue states that the standard CGT rate is **33% for most gains**, and individuals have a **€1,270 annual personal exemption**.

Dividends have their own tax treatment.

Inside the proposed PIA, the existing tax rules applying to individual investments are expected to be replaced by the PIA's annual account-level regime.

That could make record keeping simpler, but whether it produces a lower or higher effective tax burden for a particular investor will depend heavily on:

- the tax-free threshold;
- the annual PIA tax rate;
- the size of the account;
- investment returns;
- how long the money remains invested;
- provider and fund fees; and
- the tax treatment that would otherwise have applied.

Until the Budget numbers are known, claims that the PIA is automatically "better" or "worse" than a normal brokerage account are premature.

## Can you transfer a PIA between providers?

The framework says investors should be able to move a PIA between providers on a **tax-neutral basis where possible**.

That is important for competition.

An investment wrapper is less useful if moving from an expensive provider to a cheaper one creates an immediate tax charge or forces the investor to restart the tax treatment.

The practical details still matter: whether investments can be moved in specie, whether a provider requires assets to be sold first, transfer fees, supported securities and how long a transfer takes.

When providers begin launching PIAs, Compound's [How to Choose an Investment Broker in Ireland](/wealth/choose-investment-broker-ireland/) framework will be updated to include PIA-specific comparisons.

## Who will be able to open one?

The framework reported so far provides for eligibility for:

- Irish residents;
- aged 18 or over;
- with a PPS number.

The published plan is for **one PIA per eligible individual**.

The Department of Finance has also acknowledged interest in investment accounts for children, but that is being considered separately for possible future legislation.

So parents should not currently assume the 2027 PIA will be available as a child investment account.

For the existing options, see [Saving and Investing for Your Child in Ireland](/wealth/saving-for-your-child-ireland/).

## What will Budget 2027 decide?

Three numbers.

### 1. The tax-free threshold

This determines how much account value can sit below the annual PIA tax charge.

A larger threshold would make the account more attractive to smaller investors. A smaller threshold would cause the annual charge to begin earlier.

### 2. The annual flat tax rate

This is arguably the most important unknown because it is expected to apply every year to account value above the threshold.

Even a small recurring percentage matters when applied over decades.

### 3. The annual contribution limit

This will determine how quickly an investor can build assets inside the wrapper.

A low annual cap would make the PIA primarily a small-to-medium saver product. A high cap would allow a much larger portion of household investment wealth to move inside the structure over time.

Budget 2027 is scheduled for **6 October 2026**. Compound's [Budget 2027 Ireland tracker](/wealth/budget-2027-what-to-watch/) follows the wider tax and household measures.

## What should you do before the PIA launches?

There is no need to rush into a product that does not yet exist.

What you can do is get the underlying investment decisions right first.

### 1. Decide what the money is for

Retirement money, a house deposit needed in two years and wealth intended for 20 years are different problems.

### 2. Build your cash buffer

Money that may be needed during a market fall should not automatically be moved into investments simply because a new tax wrapper exists.

### 3. Understand investment risk

A PIA changes the tax wrapper. It does not change the volatility of the asset inside it.

### 4. Learn the basic investment structures

Understand shares, bonds, ETFs and funds before choosing between them.

### 5. Compare fees when providers launch

Do not look only at tax.

Platform charges, dealing fees, foreign-exchange costs, fund charges and transfer fees can materially affect long-term returns.

### 6. Wait for the official tax numbers

The threshold, annual tax rate and contribution cap are necessary before anyone can make a serious after-tax comparison.

## What the PIA does not solve

Even if the account succeeds in simplifying Irish investment taxation, the normal investing problems remain.

You can still:

- buy an unsuitable investment;
- pay excessive fees;
- hold an undiversified portfolio;
- panic during a market fall;
- invest money you need too soon;
- chase recent performance; or
- misunderstand the risk you are taking.

Tax efficiency is useful. It is not an investment strategy.

## What happens to deemed disposal outside the PIA?

For now, treat this as a separate issue.

The Government's PIA framework removes deemed disposal **inside the new account**.

It has not announced a general abolition of deemed disposal for investments held outside the PIA in Budget 2027.

The Department of Finance's wider work on the taxation of retail investment is expected to continue, with changes to the existing regime being considered beyond Budget 2027.

So an investor who continues to hold relevant ETFs or funds outside a PIA may remain subject to the existing rules.

## Personal Investment Account Ireland: frequently asked questions

### When will the Personal Investment Account launch in Ireland?

The Government intends for Personal Investment Accounts to become available in **2027**. The exact launch date and first providers have not yet been confirmed.

### Is the Irish Personal Investment Account available now?

No. As of **25 September 2026**, the framework has been announced but the accounts are not yet available.

### Will ETFs be allowed in a PIA?

Yes, the published framework includes suitable investment funds and ETFs among the types of assets expected to qualify. Final product eligibility rules will still matter.

### Will deemed disposal apply inside the PIA?

The announced framework says the existing deemed-disposal regime will **not** apply to investments held inside the Personal Investment Account.

### What will the PIA tax rate be?

It has not yet been announced. The Government says the flat annual rate, tax-free threshold and annual contribution limit will be announced in **Budget 2027 on 6 October 2026**.

### Is the PIA tax charged only when I sell?

Not under the framework described so far. The proposal is for an annual flat tax on account value above a tax-free threshold, with providers handling the tax administration. The final legislation will determine the exact mechanics.

### Can I put crypto in a Personal Investment Account?

No. Crypto assets are excluded under the announced framework.

### Can I withdraw money before retirement?

The proposed account has no minimum holding period or lock-in period. It is therefore structurally more flexible than a pension, although investments can still rise or fall in value.

### Is a PIA better than a pension?

They are designed for different purposes. Pensions can provide income-tax relief on qualifying contributions but restrict access because they are retirement products. The PIA is designed as a flexible investment account. The best fit depends on the purpose and time horizon of the money.

### Will a Personal Investment Account guarantee returns?

No. The PIA is an investment wrapper, not a guaranteed savings product. Returns depend on the investments held inside it.

## The three numbers to watch on 6 October

The framework is now reasonably clear.

The economics are not.

For most ordinary investors, the entire PIA proposition will turn on the interaction between:

**tax-free threshold × annual tax rate × contribution limit**

Those figures will tell us far more than the label on the account.
