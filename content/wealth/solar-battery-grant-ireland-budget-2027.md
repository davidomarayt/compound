A new **€600 grant for home battery storage** is expected to be announced in Budget 2027.

That will immediately make batteries look more attractive to households considering solar.

But the important question is not simply:

> **Can I get €600 off a battery?**

It is:

> **Does the battery save enough money each year to justify the remaining cost?**

That distinction matters because a battery can improve how much solar electricity you use at home without necessarily producing the best financial return.

For many Irish households, **solar panels can make financial sense without a battery**.

The new grant could narrow that gap.

It will not automatically eliminate it.

## What is being proposed?

[The Irish Times reported on 25 September](https://www.irishtimes.com/politics/2026/09/25/grant-of-600-for-solar-panel-batteries-set-to-be-announced-in-budget/) that households installing battery storage alongside solar panels are expected to qualify for a new **€600 grant**.

The reported support would be additional to the existing SEAI solar PV grant.

SEAI's current 2026 domestic solar PV grant is:

| Solar PV size | Grant |
| --- | ---: |
| First 2 kWp | €700 per kWp |
| Additional capacity to 4 kWp | €200 per kWp |
| Maximum grant | **€1,800** |

For a qualifying 4 kWp or larger system, that means the existing solar support can reach €1,800.

If the reported battery measure is introduced as expected, a household installing both solar and storage could potentially receive:

**€1,800 solar PV grant + €600 battery grant = €2,400 total support**

subject to the final eligibility rules.

Those rules have not yet been published.

## Why Government wants more batteries in homes

Solar generation and household electricity demand do not always line up.

Panels often produce strongly around the middle of the day.

Many homes use more electricity:

- early in the morning;
- after work;
- during the evening;
- and after sunset.

Without a battery, surplus solar electricity can be exported to the grid.

With a battery, some of that energy can be stored and used later.

That can increase the percentage of your own solar generation that you consume inside the home.

It can also potentially allow households to charge a battery cheaply at night and discharge it during expensive daytime or peak-rate periods.

Whether that arbitrage is worthwhile depends on the real tariff spread and battery losses. Our **[Electricity Tariffs in Ireland guide](/wealth/electricity-tariffs-ireland/)** explains the current smart, EV and dynamic tariff structures behind that calculation.

That flexibility is one reason batteries are increasingly being paired with solar systems.

The Irish Times reported that around **half of current solar installations now include battery storage**.

## Batteries are still expensive

The same report noted that home battery systems typically cost **more than €4,000**.

So even with a €600 grant, the household is still making a substantial additional investment.

Consider a simple example:

| Battery cost | Grant | Net battery cost |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| €4,000 | €600 | €3,400 |
| €4,500 | €600 | €3,900 |
| €5,000 | €600 | €4,400 |
| €6,000 | €600 | €5,400 |

The grant therefore improves the economics.

But it does not transform a €5,000 battery into a cheap upgrade.

The more useful calculation is how much additional annual value the battery creates.

## What annual saving would justify a battery?

Take a battery costing **€4,500 before grant**.

After a €600 grant:

**Net cost = €3,900**

To recover that cost through additional savings alone:

| Target simple payback | Additional annual saving required |
| --- | ---: |
| 5 years | **€780/year** |
| 7 years | **€557/year** |
| 10 years | **€390/year** |
| 12 years | **€325/year** |

That is the key figure.

If the battery only improves your annual electricity position by €200, a €3,900 net investment has a simple payback of nearly **19.5 years**.

If it improves your position by €500 a year, the simple payback falls to about **7.8 years**.

The grant helps in both cases.

But your electricity usage still determines whether the battery is attractive.

## Why export payments matter so much

A common mistake is to treat surplus solar electricity as worthless.

It is not.

Irish households with eligible microgeneration can receive payment for electricity exported to the grid.

That means every kWh put into a battery has an opportunity cost:

**you are giving up the export payment you could otherwise have received.**

Suppose:

- export rate = 18c/kWh;
- electricity import price = 30c/kWh;
- battery round-trip efficiency = 90%.

If you export 1 kWh, you receive:

**18c**

If you store 1 kWh in the battery, only about 0.9 kWh may come back after losses.

Using that later to avoid buying electricity at 30c/kWh is worth:

**0.9 × 30c = 27c**

So the incremental value of storing rather than exporting that 1 kWh is roughly:

**27c - 18c = 9c**

That is much smaller than the full 30c retail electricity price.

This is why batteries can increase self-consumption without necessarily paying back quickly.

## The €600 grant changes the calculation — but not the underlying equation

In our existing analysis, **[Do you actually need a battery with solar panels in Ireland?](/wealth/solar-panels-vs-battery-ireland/)**, we modelled a €5,000 battery.

Under one illustrative household scenario, the battery increased annual energy value by around **€149 per year** from solar shifting alone.

A €600 grant would reduce that battery's effective upfront cost from:

**€5,000 → €4,400**

That improves the economics.

But if the battery is only creating €149 of extra annual value, the grant does not suddenly make the investment financially compelling.

The simple payback on the battery portion would still be close to:

**€4,400 ÷ €149 ≈ 29.5 years**

That is a deliberately simplified example, and many households can do better.

The point is that **a grant reduces capital cost; it does not change how much each stored kWh is worth**.

## When a battery becomes much more attractive

Battery economics improve when the difference between cheap and expensive electricity becomes larger.

There are several situations where this can happen.

### 1. You export a lot of solar but buy expensive electricity later

A household that exports heavily during the day and imports heavily during expensive evening periods has more opportunity to shift electricity through a battery.

The larger the gap between export value and avoided import cost, the more valuable storage becomes.

### 2. You have a strong night-rate tariff

Some smart tariffs offer much cheaper electricity overnight.

That can create a second use for the battery:

**charge it cheaply from the grid at night and use that electricity later when rates are higher.**

This means the battery is no longer relying solely on excess solar for its return.

A battery that cycles during both winter and summer can produce more annual value than one that is mainly used to store summer solar.

### 3. Your household uses a lot of electricity in the evening

Homes with high evening demand may be able to use more stored energy.

That can include households with:

- electric cooking;
- home offices;
- gaming or entertainment loads;
- electric showers;
- heat pumps;
- or larger families.

### 4. Your export rate is relatively low

If your electricity supplier pays a low export rate, giving up that export becomes less costly.

That improves the economics of storing the electricity instead.

### 5. Your import rate is high

The more expensive each imported kWh is, the more valuable it becomes to avoid buying one.

The relationship is simple:

**battery value rises when avoided import cost rises relative to export value.**

## EV owners need to be particularly careful

An EV sounds like an obvious reason to buy a large battery.

Sometimes it is.

Sometimes it is the opposite.

If your EV can charge overnight on a very cheap tariff, it may make little financial sense to:

1. store daytime solar in an expensive home battery;
2. then use that stored energy to charge the EV;
3. when the car could simply charge directly from the grid overnight at a much lower rate.

For example, if you receive 18c/kWh for exported solar but can charge your EV overnight at 8c/kWh, exporting the solar and buying cheap night electricity can actually leave you financially better off.

## A battery does something solar panels cannot

There is still a practical benefit that simple payback does not fully capture.

Solar panels generate electricity.

A battery gives you **control over when electricity is used**.

That flexibility can become valuable as smart tariffs become more sophisticated.

A battery may allow a household to:

- avoid expensive peak-rate periods;
- charge during very cheap night-rate windows;
- increase solar self-consumption;
- reduce exposure to future tariff changes;
- coordinate with an EV;
- and potentially provide limited backup capability, depending on the system.

Those benefits can matter even when the simple financial return is not the absolute best available investment.

But households should separate those lifestyle and resilience benefits from pure payback.

## What about the Government's €900 savings figure?

The Department of Energy has calculated that a household with a battery installed alongside a **4 kWp solar PV system** could generate savings of up to around **€900 per year**, according to The Irish Times.

That is useful as an indication of potential system-level savings.

But it should not be read as meaning:

**the battery itself saves €900 per year.**

A large portion of the savings from a solar-plus-battery system may come from the solar panels themselves.

The correct battery decision is therefore based on:

**solar + battery savings minus solar-only savings**

rather than comparing the whole system with a home that has no solar at all.

That incremental calculation is the one that tells you whether the battery earns its additional cost.

## What would the grant do to payback?

Consider this simplified battery-only comparison:

| Battery price | Annual additional value | Payback before grant | Payback after €600 grant |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| €4,000 | €300 | 13.3 years | 11.3 years |
| €4,500 | €400 | 11.3 years | 9.8 years |
| €5,000 | €500 | 10.0 years | 8.8 years |
| €5,000 | €300 | 16.7 years | 14.7 years |

The €600 grant can remove roughly **one to two years** from simple payback in many mid-range examples.

That is meaningful.

But it is not enough to rescue a poorly matched battery system.

## Should you wait until Budget 2027 before ordering?

If you are considering a battery right now, waiting for the final rules is sensible.

The grant has been reported, but the important details are still unknown.

We do not yet have confirmed information on:

- exact start date;
- whether existing solar homes can apply;
- whether the battery must be installed with new solar;
- minimum or maximum battery capacity;
- approved installer requirements;
- whether the grant will be paid directly or retrospectively;
- whether installations contracted before Budget Day qualify;
- or whether there will be household eligibility restrictions.

Those details can materially affect whether someone receives the grant.

Until the measure is formally announced, households should avoid assuming that any particular installation will qualify.

## What about the existing €1,800 solar grant?

SEAI currently offers a maximum domestic solar PV grant of **€1,800**.

The home must generally:

- have an MPRN;
- have been built and occupied before 2021;
- not previously have received solar PV funding at that MPRN;
- and have grant approval before works begin.

SEAI also states that grant approval should be in place before the installation proceeds.

The reported battery grant is expected to sit alongside this existing support rather than replace it.

## The best way to decide

Do not start with the grant.

Start with your electricity data.

Look at:

- annual household consumption;
- daytime versus evening usage;
- current import tariff;
- night-rate tariff;
- export payment;
- estimated annual solar generation;
- battery usable capacity;
- battery round-trip efficiency;
- installed battery cost after grant;
- and expected number of useful cycles per year.

Then compare:

**solar only**

with:

**solar + battery**

The battery should be judged on the **incremental value it adds**.

That is the number that matters.

## So is the €600 battery grant a good deal?

It will make batteries more attractive.

For households already close to the point where storage makes sense, €600 could be enough to move the decision in favour of a battery.

It may be particularly useful for homes with:

- high evening electricity use;
- large daytime solar exports;
- expensive peak tariffs;
- access to cheap off-peak charging;
- or a battery that can be used productively throughout the year.

For households with low electricity use, strong export payments and limited evening demand, solar panels without a battery may still produce a better financial return.

The grant should therefore be viewed as **a reduction in the hurdle**, not proof that everyone should install storage.
