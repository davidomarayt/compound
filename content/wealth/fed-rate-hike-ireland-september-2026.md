<p><em>News analysis · 22 September 2026. This article explains the possible Irish transmission channels from a US interest-rate decision; it does not assume a particular future path for exchange rates, markets or Irish lending rates.</em></p>

The US Federal Reserve has raised its main policy rate by **0.25 percentage points**, taking the federal funds target range to **3.75%–4.00%**.

The Federal Open Market Committee approved the move unanimously on 16 September, saying US inflation remained elevated and that the increase was intended to support a return to its 2% inflation goal. [Federal Reserve statement](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260916a.htm).

For someone in Ireland, the obvious question is:

> **Does a US rate rise make my mortgage more expensive?**

Not directly.

But the decision can still matter through financial markets, currencies and the value of US investments held by Irish savers.

## The Fed does not set Irish mortgage rates

The Federal Reserve sets monetary policy for the United States.

Ireland is part of the euro area, so the central bank that matters directly for euro-area monetary policy is the **European Central Bank**.

That distinction is particularly clear for Irish tracker mortgages.

The Central Bank of Ireland explains that a tracker mortgage typically follows a publicly available rate set by the ECB, commonly the ECB's main refinancing operations rate. [Central Bank of Ireland](https://www.centralbank.ie/consumer-hub/explainers/what-is-the-tracker-mortgage-examination).

The CCPC similarly describes Irish tracker mortgages as a fixed margin above an ECB rate. [CCPC mortgage guide](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/buying-a-home/about-mortgages/understanding-mortgages).

So a Federal Reserve decision does **not** mechanically add 0.25 percentage points to an Irish mortgage.

## The ECB has also raised rates

The timing makes this particularly interesting.

Six days before the Federal Reserve decision, the ECB raised its own three key interest rates by **25 basis points**.

From 16 September, the ECB deposit facility rate is 2.50%, the main refinancing operations rate is 2.65% and the marginal lending facility rate is 2.90%. [ECB monetary-policy decision](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2026/html/ecb.mp260910~314e508016.en.html).

For Irish borrowers, that ECB decision has the more direct monetary-policy connection.

For fixed and variable mortgage customers, however, the amount actually charged still depends on the lender, the mortgage product, funding costs, competition and the terms of the loan.

## Why should an Irish investor care about the Fed?

Because a large share of many Irish investment portfolios ultimately sits in US assets.

A global equity fund, S&P 500 fund or Nasdaq-heavy portfolio can contain substantial exposure to US companies.

A Federal Reserve rate decision can influence:

- US government bond yields;
- borrowing costs for companies;
- investor expectations;
- valuations placed on shares;
- the US dollar;
- global risk appetite.

None of those relationships is perfectly mechanical.

A rate rise does not guarantee that US shares fall, that the dollar rises or that any particular asset class will move in one direction.

Markets also react to what investors had expected **before** the announcement.

## The euro-dollar exchange rate creates another layer

For an Irish investor measuring wealth in euro, a US investment has two moving parts:

1. the investment's value in US dollars; and
2. the exchange rate between the dollar and the euro.

Imagine that a US share is unchanged in dollar terms.

If the dollar strengthens against the euro, its euro value can rise.

If the dollar weakens, the opposite can happen.

That means Irish investors can experience gains or losses from currency movements even when the underlying US investment itself has barely moved.

The Fed's decisions are one factor that can influence currency markets, but they are far from the only one.

## Does this mean Irish savings rates should rise?

Again, not directly because of the Fed.

An Irish bank does not normally change deposit rates simply because the US Federal Reserve changed its policy rate.

Irish deposit pricing is much more closely connected to euro-area interest rates, each bank's funding needs, competition and commercial decisions.

The more useful comparison for an Irish saver is therefore between the rates actually available here, inflation, access to the money and the risk level involved.

## Why did the Fed raise rates?

The Federal Reserve said economic activity was expanding at a solid pace while inflation remained elevated.

Its September projections put median **PCE inflation at 3.7% for 2026**, falling to 2.3% in 2027 and 2.1% in 2028. These are projections from Federal Reserve participants, not guaranteed outcomes. [Federal Reserve projections](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcprojtabl20260916.htm).

The Fed also stressed that future decisions would depend on incoming information and the evolving economic outlook.

In other words, one rate increase does not establish a guaranteed path for the next several meetings.

## What should someone in Ireland actually watch?

For an Irish household, it helps to separate the channels.

**If you have an Irish mortgage:** watch the ECB, your lender and the specific terms of your mortgage.

**If you have cash savings:** watch Irish and euro-area deposit rates rather than assuming US rates will be passed through here.

**If you own US shares or global funds:** the Fed is more relevant because it can affect US markets, bond yields and the dollar.

**If you run or invest in an Irish business with significant US exposure:** financing conditions and currency movements can also matter.

The key point is that the Federal Reserve matters globally without being Ireland's central bank.

A US rate rise can ripple into Ireland.

It just does not arrive as a simple one-for-one change to an Irish mortgage or savings account.
