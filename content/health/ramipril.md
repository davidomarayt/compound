## At a glance

Ramipril is an ACE inhibitor prescribed for several cardiovascular indications. A clinician may use it to lower blood pressure, manage heart failure or reduce risk in particular situations. [HSE guide](https://www2.hse.ie/medicines/ramipril/)

## The Science Behind the Drug

### Why an ACE inhibitor can change potassium and creatinine

Ramipril is converted to ramiprilat, which inhibits angiotensin-converting enzyme. Less angiotensin II means less vasoconstriction and altered aldosterone release. ACE also breaks down bradykinin, so inhibiting it can contribute to a persistent dry cough. Changes in kidney blood flow and aldosterone help explain why creatinine and potassium are monitored. [NHS SPS source](https://sps.nhs.uk/monitorings/ace-inhibitors-and-angiotensin-ii-receptor-blockers-monitoring/)

## What the evidence measures

Blood pressure readings assess one intended effect. Creatinine and potassium help assess safety. Long-term clinical benefits depend on the condition studied and cannot be inferred from a single blood pressure reading.

## A blood-test review in two lines

| What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Creatinine or eGFR | helps assess kidney function |
| Potassium | helps assess the effect of changes in kidney handling and aldosterone |


## What can cause a change in the next blood result?

Illness, dehydration or other medicines can affect the same measurements. Record changes and dates rather than attributing the whole result to ramipril by itself.

## When to ask for help or a medication review

Tongue or throat swelling is an emergency. Check anti-inflammatory painkillers, potassium products and pregnancy plans with your care team. Ask for an individual plan for illness or dehydration. [HSE patient guidance](https://www2.hse.ie/medicines/ramipril/)
