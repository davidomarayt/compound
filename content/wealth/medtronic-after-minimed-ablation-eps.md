**Medtronic, the Galway-headquartered medical device group, has finished separating its diabetes business and says its growth outlook is unchanged. The numbers show a business increasingly carried by one franchise, cardiac ablation, while a smaller share count helps the earnings arithmetic. Investors need to know which of those supports is durable.**

For the second quarter of fiscal 2027, ended 30 October 2026, Medtronic reported **revenue of $9.364 billion, up 4.5%**, or **6.5% on an organic basis**. Organic growth excludes currency, recent acquisitions and revenue reported as “Other”, which included the diabetes business until it left the group on 15 October.

Medtronic reiterated full-year guidance of **7.25% to 7.75% organic revenue growth** and **non-GAAP diluted EPS of $5.94 to $6.00**, saying it would absorb the separation.

For the maintained quarterly record, see [Medtronic’s stock profile](/stocks/mdt/). This article focuses on growth concentration and the earnings bridge after the split.

## Electrophysiology supplied most of the growth

On Medtronic’s organic basis, the reportable segments grew from $8.164 billion to $8.698 billion, an increase of **$534 million**. Electrophysiology Therapies alone rose from $1.825 billion to $2.163 billion, or **$338 million**.

That means electrophysiology accounted for about **63% of the quarter’s organic growth** while making up roughly a quarter of segment revenue, Compound’s calculations. Medtronic says its Cardiac Ablation Solutions business grew **58%**, including 80% in the United States, and gained seven points of share. Those are company figures for a sub-unit and are not separately reconciled in the release.

The rest of the portfolio was steadier but slower. Interventional cardiology was flat organically, neuromodulation fell 2.2%, and surgical and endoscopy grew 3.2%. Cranial and spinal technologies, up 6.1%, and acute care and monitoring, up 8.5%, were the other bright spots.

This concentration is a strength while pulsed-field ablation is taking share. It is also the main risk. Excluding electrophysiology, organic segment growth was about **3.1%**, Compound’s calculation. If competition or pricing slows that franchise, the group’s growth rate would drift towards that level.

## Adjusted profit lagged revenue

GAAP earnings jumped to **$2.20 per share**, but that includes a **$1.983 billion gain** on the MiniMed separation. Medtronic’s own non-GAAP measure is more useful here, and it went the other way.

Non-GAAP operating margin fell **70 basis points to 23.4%**, and non-GAAP diluted EPS fell to **$1.34 from $1.36**. Non-GAAP pre-tax income actually rose about 1.7% to $2.133 billion, Compound’s calculation. The decline came mainly from tax: the non-GAAP effective rate rose to **19.9% from 16.4%**.

Investors should keep two adjustments in view. Amortisation of intangibles, excluded from non-GAAP results, rose to $626 million and included $206 million of accelerated amortisation in Neuroscience. A further $236 million of tax charges, mostly a valuation allowance on deferred tax assets transferred with the diabetes business, was also excluded.

## A smaller share count helps the guidance

The separation took the form of an exchange offer. Shareholders swapped Medtronic shares for MiniMed shares, and Medtronic accepted **55,032,401 of its own shares** in return for its remaining **252.8 million MiniMed shares**. The offer was heavily oversubscribed, with only about 18.3% of tendered shares accepted.

Those 55 million shares equal roughly **4.3% of the quarter’s 1.271 billion diluted weighted shares**, Compound’s calculation. Because the swap closed in mid-October, it barely affected second-quarter EPS. It will affect the whole second half.

That matters for the guidance. Medtronic loses the diabetes business’s profit, but it also divides the remaining profit by fewer shares. The company does not quantify either effect, so we cannot say whether the share reduction fully offsets the lost earnings. Yet it shows that keeping EPS guidance unchanged is partly arithmetic and not only operating strength.

## The second-half bar is higher

Medtronic earned **$2.79 of non-GAAP EPS in the first half**. Reaching $5.94 to $6.00 requires **$3.15 to $3.21 in the second half**, about 13% to 15% more than the first half, Compound’s calculation. Fiscal 2027 also has a 53rd week, which fell in the first quarter, so that extra selling time will not help the second half.

Cash generation gives some room. First-half free cash flow, as Medtronic defines it, was **$2.621 billion**, up from $1.041 billion, helped by smaller working-capital outflows. Medtronic paid $1.842 billion in dividends, repurchased $446 million of shares and spent $1.234 billion on acquisitions.

## What would confirm or weaken the thesis

The thesis strengthens if electrophysiology keeps growing strongly while interventional cardiology and neuromodulation return to growth, and if the non-GAAP tax rate settles closer to last year’s level.

It weakens if ablation growth slows sharply, if second-half EPS needs further tax or below-the-line help, or if acquisitions such as the planned Contego Medical deal add cost without lifting organic growth.

**Medtronic’s post-MiniMed growth is real, but it is narrow. Most of this quarter’s organic gain came from cardiac ablation, adjusted EPS slipped on a higher tax rate, and a 55-million-share reduction will flatter per-share comparisons in the second half.** This analysis makes no price target.
