<p><strong>The average Irish commute does not look enormous when expressed as a single journey: 29.1 minutes.</strong> But repeat it twice a day, five days a week, for most of a working year and the number changes completely.</p>

<p>On that basis, the average commute adds up to roughly <strong>233 hours a year</strong>.</p>

<p>That is the equivalent of almost <strong>10 complete 24-hour days</strong> — or about <strong>29 eight-hour working days</strong> — spent simply getting to and from work.</p>

<p>And that is based on the national average from Census 2022. For people dealing with some of Ireland's longest commutes or worsening M50 congestion, the total can be considerably higher.</p>

<h2>The commute looks different when you measure it annually</h2>

<p>The Central Statistics Office found that the average journey to work increased from <strong>26.6 minutes in 2011 to 29.1 minutes in 2022</strong>.</p>

<p>To put that into perspective, imagine someone commuting to a workplace five days a week for 48 weeks of the year.</p>

<p>A commute is not necessarily wasted time. Some people read, listen to podcasts, walk or cycle. Others genuinely enjoy the transition between home and work.</p>

<p>But the calculation shows why a seemingly small daily journey can become a significant part of your life when repeated hundreds of times.</p>

<h2>Where you live makes a major difference</h2>

<p>The national average also hides large regional differences.</p>

<p>According to the CSO, <strong>Meath had the longest average commute in the country at 35 minutes</strong> in Census 2022. Kildare, Wicklow and Fingal were close behind at around 34 minutes.</p>

<p>At 35 minutes each way, a five-day commuter could spend around <strong>280 hours a year travelling to and from work</strong> under the same 48-week assumption.</p>

<p>That is nearly 12 full days of the year.</p>

<p>Long-distance commuting is not confined to drivers either. The CSO found that train, DART and Luas users had an average journey time of 52 minutes, while bus commuters averaged 45 minutes.</p>

<h2>And congestion can add another 5 to 10 days</h2>

<p>A 2026 Trinity College Dublin survey gives a more current picture of what is happening on the M50.</p>

<p>Researchers surveyed almost 2,000 motorway users in spring 2026. <strong>85% said their journey times had increased over the previous year</strong>, and more than half said they were spending an additional 15 to 30 minutes on each trip.</p>

<p>That additional delay alone is substantial.</p>

<p>If someone experiences an extra 15 minutes in each direction on 240 working days, that is another <strong>120 hours a year</strong> — five full days.</p>

<p>At 30 extra minutes each way, it becomes <strong>240 hours</strong>, or another 10 full days.</p>

<p>That does not mean every M50 user is losing exactly that amount. The Trinity survey was a voluntary survey of motorway users, not a nationally representative commuting sample. But it shows how rapidly repeated delays can accumulate.</p>

<h2>The cost is not only time</h2>

<p>The Trinity research also found clear wellbeing effects. <strong>42% of respondents said congestion was increasing their stress levels</strong>, while 34% reported exhaustion linked to time spent in traffic.</p>

<p>More than half said they used the M50 because other options were not viable.</p>

<p>That last point matters. Advice about commuting can easily become unrealistic. Moving home, changing jobs, cycling or taking public transport may simply not be practical for many people.</p>

<p>The useful question is therefore not always “how do I eliminate my commute?” It may be: <strong>can I reduce its frequency, cost or unpredictability?</strong></p>

<h2>One remote-working day can add up</h2>

<p>For someone with a 35-minute commute each way, working from home one day per week would avoid around 70 minutes of travel that day.</p>

<p>Across 48 weeks, that is roughly <strong>56 hours a year</strong> — more than two full days of time.</p>

<p>Two remote-working days would roughly double that figure.</p>

<p>Of course, remote work is not possible in every job, and the value of working from home depends on the person, the role and the home environment. But when employers and workers discuss hybrid arrangements, the time saved from commuting is a real part of the equation.</p>

<p>For the Irish rules around requesting flexible or remote work, see our guide to <a href="/life/remote-working-ireland-rights-request/">remote working in Ireland</a>.</p>

<h2>The financial cost compounds too</h2>

<p>Time is only one side of commuting.</p>

<p>A driving commute also includes fuel or electricity, servicing, tyres, depreciation, tolls and potentially parking. A longer journey can therefore create a double cost: more money spent travelling and less discretionary time outside work.</p>

<p>If you drive, Compound's <a href="/fuel-cost-calculator/">Fuel Cost Calculator</a> can estimate what your regular journey costs over a week, month and year.</p>

<p>This is where the annual view becomes useful. An extra €5 a day may not feel significant. Over 240 commuting days it is €1,200. The same principle applies to time.</p>

<h2>What is your commute worth to you?</h2>

<p>There is no universal “good” commute length.</p>

<p>A longer commute might make sense for a better job, cheaper housing, family reasons or a lifestyle you prefer. A 45-minute train journey where you can read may feel very different from 45 minutes of stop-start traffic.</p>

<p>But it is worth calculating the trade-off honestly.</p>

<p>If a job pays €5,000 more but adds five hours of commuting each week, that is around 240 extra hours over 48 working weeks. Before tax and travel expenses, the extra gross salary works out at roughly €20.83 for each additional commuting hour.</p>

<p>That does not make the job a bad choice. It simply makes the trade-off visible.</p>

<h2>A small daily number can become a large life number</h2>

<p>Most people do not experience their commute as “10 days a year”. They experience it as half an hour this morning and half an hour this evening.</p>

<p>That is exactly why it is easy to underestimate.</p>

<p>The broader lesson applies beyond commuting: repeated small demands can consume surprisingly large amounts of time when viewed over a year.</p>

<p>For people with flexibility, even reducing a commute by one day a week or shifting travel outside peak congestion can create meaningful time. For those without that flexibility, better transport infrastructure and more reliable alternatives become quality-of-life issues, not merely transport policy.</p>

<p>The daily commute may be unavoidable. Its true annual cost should not be invisible.</p>
