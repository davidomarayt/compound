**Keysight ended fiscal 2026 with orders growing faster than revenue and revenue growing faster than the wider test-and-measure market. AI infrastructure is a genuine demand driver, but acquisitions and a one-off tariff benefit make the headline acceleration look cleaner than the underlying economics.**

In the quarter ended 31 October, Keysight reported **$2.339 billion of orders, up 53%**, and **$1.994 billion of revenue, up 41%**. On the company’s core basis—which removes currency effects and acquired or divested businesses—orders rose 49% and revenue rose **36%**.

That distinction is central. Acquisitions helped, yet the four-percentage-point gap between reported and core quarterly revenue growth shows that most of the increase came from the existing business. The investment question is whether AI-related testing demand can keep orders ahead of a much larger revenue base after integration benefits and exceptional items fade.

For the maintained quarterly record, see [Keysight’s stock profile](/stocks/keys/). This article focuses on the growth quality and capacity economics behind the latest results.

## Demand is broad, but AI infrastructure is the sharpest edge

Communications Solutions Group revenue rose **51% to $1.496 billion**. Within it, commercial communications grew 73% to $1.145 billion, led by triple-digit growth in wireline applications tied to AI infrastructure. Aerospace, defence and government revenue increased 7%, while Electronic Industrial Solutions Group revenue rose 16% to $498 million.

Keysight sells the instruments and software used to design, simulate and validate electronics and communications systems. That gives it exposure to the complexity of faster networks and data-centre components rather than only the number of servers shipped. More complex links can require more testing steps, which can increase demand even when unit volumes are less spectacular.

The order-to-revenue ratio was about **1.17 in Q4** and **1.14 for the year**, calculated from reported figures. Ratios above one indicate that orders exceeded recognised sales during those periods, helping create record backlog. They do not guarantee future revenue: delivery schedules can change and customers can sometimes modify or cancel orders.

## Acquisitions are contributing more than revenue

Acquired businesses added approximately **$370 million of fiscal-year revenue**. Management says it achieved the full $100 million run-rate synergy target and lifted the acquired operations’ non-GAAP operating margin from 11% in the first quarter to 32% in the fourth.

That is meaningful operating work. It suggests integration is improving the profit contribution, not merely adding sales. It also raises the comparison bar for fiscal 2027: once a business has been owned for a full year, its revenue stops appearing as acquisition growth, and the first round of cost synergies cannot be repeated indefinitely.

Investors should therefore keep separating reported, core and acquired growth. Fiscal-year revenue rose **33%**, while core revenue rose **26%**. The gap matters, but it does not erase a strong organic result.

## Margins improved—with one benefit that will not recur

Q4 non-GAAP gross margin reached **69.0%** and operating margin reached **35.8%**, an increase of 950 basis points. For the year, non-GAAP operating margin was 32.7%.

Some of that improvement is structural: higher volume, mix and acquisition synergies. Some is not. One-time refunds related to US import tariffs added about **110 basis points to full-year gross margin, 100 basis points to operating margin and $0.31 to earnings per share**. Management does not expect that benefit in fiscal 2027.

The correct starting point for next year is therefore below the unadjusted 2026 exit economics. If core growth and integration gains continue, margins can still improve; treating the refund as permanent would overstate the baseline.

## Cash conversion is funding a bigger capacity bet

Keysight generated **$1.886 billion of operating cash flow** and spent $139 million on capital expenditure, leaving about **$1.747 billion of simple free cash flow**, or **24.4% of revenue**. It also spent roughly $669 million on share repurchases.

Management’s fiscal 2027 base case calls for about 20% revenue growth and roughly **$275 million of capital expenditure**. That capex plan is almost double the 2026 amount—a Compound calculation—and is intended to expand capacity for current demand.

This is a productive problem if backlog converts: investing $136 million more could support a much larger revenue opportunity. It becomes a risk if customers have over-ordered scarce test capacity or if AI infrastructure spending slows after facilities are expanded. The balance sheet offers flexibility, with $2.916 billion of cash against $2.509 billion of current and long-term debt, but returns still depend on utilisation.

## What would confirm the thesis?

The strongest evidence would be core orders remaining above revenue, backlog converting on time and operating margin holding up after the tariff refund disappears. Capacity additions should lift shipments without causing inventory or receivables to grow faster than sales.

The case would weaken if cancellations rise, core growth falls well below reported growth, or new capacity depresses cash conversion. A widening gap between non-GAAP and GAAP profitability would also deserve scrutiny.

**Keysight’s acceleration is not just acquisition arithmetic: core growth, order coverage and integration margins all improved. The next test is whether those orders become high-return revenue after the one-off benefit fades and the capacity bill arrives.** This analysis makes no price target.
