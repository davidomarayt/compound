**Could an Irish first-time buyer build up a mortgage deposit inside a Personal Investment Account (PIA), invest in an S&P 500 fund, and later use that money to buy a home?** Under the current legislative proposal, the account would permit withdrawals without a general statutory lock-in. But that is only half the story.

The mortgage lender has **two different questions**. First: **can you demonstrate that you own enough accessible money for your deposit?** Second: **can you consistently afford the mortgage payments?** An investment balance, a regular monthly contribution and a reliable savings history are different pieces of evidence. Having €40,000 in shares does not necessarily mean a bank will treat it as €40,000 of cash ready for a contract deposit.

An even more interesting distinction is **regular investing versus frequent trading**. Someone who automatically invests €750 a month in a diversified fund may present a very different financial history from someone making repeated speculative trades, using leverage or regularly funding losses. But **there is no published blanket Irish mortgage rule saying that stock trading is gambling or that every PIA investment is unacceptable**.

This guide separates the **published lender requirements** from the **reasonable questions an underwriter might ask**. It does not invent unpublished rules for PIAs, which are not available yet.

**As of 21 November 2026:** The Finance (No. 2) Bill 2026 calls the proposed account an *Individual Investment Account (IIA)*. "PIA" is the familiar Personal Investment Account name. It is **not enacted law**. The Government target of **1 July 2027** depends on legislation and providers being ready. No Irish bank has an established, published 2027 PIA-specific mortgage underwriting policy that we can verify.

## The short answer: can a PIA be used towards a mortgage deposit?

**Potentially, yes.** The current Finance Bill does not impose a minimum investment holding period or a special Irish tax charge just for taking money out of a qualifying PIA. The proposed rules permit liquidating eligible assets and withdrawing money. However:

- **Your lender must be satisfied** about the deposit's amount, ownership, source and availability.
- **Investments fluctuate in value.** A portfolio worth €40,000 when you receive approval in principle might be worth €30,000 when you find a house.
- **Selling is not the same as instant access.** A fund trade, settlement, account withdrawal and transfer to a bank may each take time under the provider's eventual arrangements.
- **Withdrawals do not replenish PIA contribution room.** Under draft section 747L(2)(d), the annual **€12,000 cap counts money paid in without deducting withdrawals**. If you contributed €9,000 and withdrew €9,000 in the same tax year, your remaining contribution room would still be €3,000, not €12,000.
- **Mortgage approval involves affordability as well as a deposit.** An investment gain is not automatically regular salary or a replacement for proving repayment capacity.

In other words: a future PIA could be **one source of deposit money**, but it is not a mortgage-deposit guarantee, a bank savings product or an alternative to the mortgage lender's own checks.

For the legal proposal, see [Finance (No. 2) Bill 2026 as amended in Select Committee, section 27, proposed sections 747L and 747P](https://data.oireachtas.ie/ie/oireachtas/bill/2026/98/eng/ver_a/b98a26d.pdf). For the conditional launch, see [Revenue's account-development notice](https://www.revenue.ie/ga/corporate/press-office/press-releases/2026/pr-100726-irish-investment-account.aspx).

## What do Irish mortgage lenders actually look for?

This is where the real evidence starts. Irish banks are not just checking whether a customer has enough money in one account.

**The Central Bank's standard mortgage limits** generally require at least a **10% deposit** for first-time and subsequent owner-occupier buyers, with loan-to-income limits of **four times gross income for first-time buyers** and **3.5 times for subsequent buyers**, subject to the regulatory allowances and exceptions. Those are borrowing limits, **not promises that a lender will approve an applicant**. Lenders also assess repayment capacity and their own credit criteria. [Source: Central Bank mortgage measures](https://www.centralbank.ie/financial-system/financial-stability/macro-prudential-policy/mortgage-measures).

The [Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC)](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/buying-a-home/about-mortgages/understanding-mortgages/applying-for-a-mortgage) explains that lenders examine **income, spending, debt, account management, regular saving and evidence that borrowers can sustain repayments**. The CCPC notes that a regular saving habit over at least six months is helpful, alongside rent and other commitments. AIB likewise says lenders want to see money managed well and continued, regular saving. [AIB's explanation](https://www.aib.ie/help-and-guidance/mortgages-faq/what-lenders-look-for-in-bank-statements-for-mortgage).

### Evidence from Bank of Ireland's actual application form

Bank of Ireland's **January 2026 mortgage application form** makes a distinction that matters for PIA investors:

| What the application asks about | What it tells us | What it **does not** prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Savings accounts | It asks for balances, monthly regular savings and the source of non-regular deposits. | That buying an ETF must be treated identically to a cash standing order. |
| Other assets, including **shares**, bonds and share options | An applicant can disclose these assets with their institution and account balance. | That volatile shares will count as fully available cash at the quoted market value. |
| Proof of deposit funding | It asks about the source of funds, with supporting documents where needed. | That an app screenshot alone will satisfy every lender. |
| Account statements | Its checklist requests six months of non-BOI current-account statements and 12 months of savings statements showing the build-up. | That every Irish lender uses exactly the same period or documentation. |

These details are on **pages 4 and 11** of the bank's [January 2026 application form](https://personalbanking.bankofireland.com/app/uploads/6695131-Mortgage-Application-Form-Jan-2026-FA.pdf). AIB separately requires records of accounts, savings and debts, including statements for accounts held outside AIB. [AIB mortgage document guidance](https://aib.ie/help-and-guidance/mortgages-faq/first-time-buyer-documentation-need-to-supply).

**What this means:** There is strong evidence that lenders already know how to **ask about investments and shares**. There is **not yet evidence** that Bank of Ireland, AIB or another lender will automatically accept an unliquidated PIA balance as the final deposit, or count a €750 monthly fund purchase exactly as a €750 monthly savings deposit. It would be misleading to claim either.

## Three people putting aside €750 a month: cash, S&P 500 ETF, or active trading

Consider three hypothetical first-time buyers with the same PAYE salary, ordinary household expenses, no missed loan payments and €750 available after their bills each month. They keep this pattern for **48 months**, making **€36,000 of contributions** each.

The example isolates the saving or investing **behaviour**. Market returns, deposit interest, taxes, fees and transaction costs are left unspecified, so **€36,000 is the amount contributed, not a claim about the final account value**. For a future PIA, €750 a month is €9,000 a year, within the currently proposed €12,000 annual cap if it becomes law.

| Applicant | What the €750 monthly payment does | What the lender could reasonably ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A — cash saver** | Goes to a separate bank savings account. The nominal contribution history is easy to trace. | Can the borrower sustain the savings while paying rent and bills? What interest or withdrawals are shown? |
| **B — long-term fund investor** | Buys units of a diversified, qualifying S&P 500-tracking UCITS ETF regularly and does not frequently trade out of it. | Can the applicant show the monthly funding trail and investment statements? What is the current value? When will the deposit be turned into accessible cash? |
| **C — frequent trader** | Moves €750 into a brokerage account but regularly buys and sells, with potentially large gains, losses or extra deposits. | Are substantial losses or unexpected transfers reducing affordability? Is any borrowing or leverage involved? Is the deposit value dependable and properly documented? |

**These are editorial illustrations, not published bank underwriting categories or approval predictions.** All three applicants might be approved or refused depending on their overall circumstances.

### Applicant A: the monthly cash saver

This person has a clear, repeatable deposit-building record and does not face share-price risk on that money. The CCPC's guidance on regular savings is directly relevant. The trade-off is that cash savings may earn less than investments over long periods, and inflation may erode purchasing power. Cash is still subject to account terms and, where relevant, deposit interest tax.

### Applicant B: the long-term S&P 500 investor

A monthly purchase and hold strategy is **not the same behaviour** as attempting to profit from frequent short-term price movements. Regular transfers, dated purchase confirmations and a long holding history can help explain where the money came from and that the applicant is consistently allocating surplus income.

But an **S&P 500 ETF is still an equity investment**: the index tracks major US-listed companies, and its value can fall sharply. A fund quoted in euros may still carry exposure to foreign currencies, depending on its structure and hedging. It is **not a guaranteed deposit**.

Also, not every product labelled an "S&P 500 ETF" will necessarily be eligible in an eventual PIA. The fund's legal structure, UCITS status, share class and the provider's available investment list matter. See [our PIA investment eligibility guide](/pia/investment-eligibility/) for the ISIN-level checks.

The crucial unknown is how a lender would treat those **regular investments as evidence of demonstrated repayment ability**. They show discipline and available surplus, but published guidance emphasises **savings habits and affordability**. Ask the lender or broker whether recurring investment contributions are accepted as part of that assessment rather than assuming they are equivalent to cash savings.

### Applicant C: the frequent trader

Frequent trades are not automatically reckless; some people trade within disciplined, funded strategies. Nevertheless, a statement showing **repeated large losses, unstable balances, transfers from an overdraft or use of borrowed money** may raise questions about disposable income and financial resilience.

The **real distinction is not the number of transactions alone**. It is whether the behaviour compromises the borrower's documented deposit or ability to repay. An ordinary investment sale can occur for sensible reasons. A pattern of speculative, loss-funded activity is a different concern.

A further distinction: products such as **CFDs, leveraged derivatives and many complex trading instruments are not included in the intended PIA investment menu** under the draft rules. Do not present high-leverage day trading as something an ordinary future PIA will necessarily allow.

## Would a bank view trading as gambling?

**We found no official Irish lender policy saying that buying and selling listed shares, using a regulated brokerage or investing in a PIA is automatically classified as gambling.**

There *is* real lender guidance on **gambling transactions**. Bank of Ireland's [first-time-buyer FAQ](https://personalbanking.bankofireland.com/borrow/mortgages/first-time-buyer/) says an isolated bet does not automatically make an applicant ineligible, while **a consistent gambling history could trigger concerns and a discussion**. The [CCPC's mortgage application guide](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/buying-a-home/about-mortgages/understanding-mortgages/applying-for-a-mortgage) also identifies excessive online gambling as a transaction pattern lenders may consider.

That is evidence about **gambling**, not evidence that broker transfers are assigned a gambling transaction code or treated in exactly the same way.

What we can say responsibly is that a lender may ask **additional questions** about any financial behaviour that creates volatile assets, repeated losses, high spending, debt or uncertain repayment capacity. That is an **inference from general lending and affordability principles**, not a published "day trading equals gambling" test.

| Behaviour | Supported distinction |
| --- | --- |
| Monthly purchase of a diversified fund with a long-term goal | A recognisable investing pattern, but still exposed to market risk; bank treatment of monthly purchases must be confirmed. |
| Occasional sale or rebalancing of investments | Not automatically an indication of gambling or poor money management. |
| Frequent fully funded share trades | Trading activity; outcome depends on financial effect and lender-specific checks, not an automatic adverse label. |
| Repeated losses funded by credit or inability to pay bills | Can damage financial resilience and prompt affordability questions, irrespective of whether the activity is labelled trading or gambling. |
| Frequent betting payments to gambling merchants | Banks have specific publicly documented concerns about sustained gambling activity. |

A good article should not shame lawful investing or create an invented bank blacklist. **Follow the evidence, examine the cash flows, and distinguish investment risks from actual betting transactions.**

## The deposit problem that matters even if the bank is comfortable with your investments

Suppose you want to buy a **€400,000 principal home**. Under the standard 10% deposit rule, you need **€40,000 for the mortgage deposit**. You also need additional money for solicitor's fees, stamp duty, valuation, survey and moving expenses, so a €40,000 total cash pot would leave no headroom for those costs. [See our home-buying budget guide](/wealth/buying-home-budget-deposit-ireland/).

Imagine your investment portfolio is worth **€40,000** when you are ready to bid. The figures below are simple **instantaneous market-shock scenarios**, not historical forecasts:

| Market move before the purchase | Value of €40,000 portfolio | Deposit shortfall against €40,000 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| No change | €40,000 | €0 |
| 10% fall | €36,000 | €4,000 |
| **25% fall** | **€30,000** | **€10,000** |
| 35% fall | €26,000 | €14,000 |

Even if you had demonstrated four years of consistent investing, the 25% fall would leave you with **€30,000**, or only **7.5% of the €400,000 price**, before dealing costs and any other funding. The lender could require the missing deposit to be made up, a different purchase budget, or a reassessment.

**No Irish bank can make a falling ETF price into a guaranteed deposit simply because the investing behaviour was disciplined.** This is *market-timing risk*, and it is separate from the underwriter's assessment of affordability.

The [CCPC's savings-versus-investing guidance](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/saving-and-investments/the-difference-between-saving-and-investing) specifically uses a **house deposit** as an example of a near-term objective that may be better suited to saving than investing. That does not make equity investing universally unsuitable for every future homeowner. It means the length and flexibility of your home-buying timeline are central.

### A practical timeline for a home deposit

These are **editorial planning prompts**, not bank requirements or personalised advice:

| How soon you expect to need the deposit | A question worth asking |
| --- | --- |
| Buying in the next 0–12 months | Can you afford any fall in market value or withdrawal delay? Would accessible cash better protect the purchase? |
| Buying in 1–3 years | If markets decline, could you delay the purchase or replace the shortfall from safe savings? |
| Buying in 3–5 years | How gradually should you reduce risk as your target date becomes firmer? |
| More than 5 years away, with a flexible date | What investment risk can you realistically tolerate, and when will you move the necessary amount into accessible cash? |

The closer you are to **bidding, signing contracts or drawing down**, the less time there is for a risky portfolio to recover from a loss.

## How to prove the source of your PIA deposit to a bank

Mortgage applications routinely ask applicants to establish where deposit funds originated. If savings were built by **selling investments**, prepare a continuous documentary trail rather than assuming a portfolio screenshot will suffice.

A practical **investment-to-deposit evidence pack** could include:

1. **Bank statements showing the original transfers** from salary or personal savings into the regulated broker or future PIA provider.
2. **Investment account statements** showing your name, the provider, contributions, holdings and transaction history.
3. **Trade confirmations or contract notes** for the sale of shares or fund units to raise the deposit.
4. **Settlement and cash-balance evidence** showing that the sale proceeds became available for withdrawal.
5. **Withdrawal confirmation and receiving bank statement** tracing proceeds from the investment provider to an accessible account.
6. **An explanation of any unusual large credits**, gifts, third-party funding or transfers, with additional documents if requested.
7. **Evidence for both applicants** if a couple is buying jointly and either is contributing money from an individually owned account.

These are **suggested records**, not a claim that every lender requests every item. Confirm your own lender's actual checklist, preferred format and timing. Bank of Ireland's 2026 form specifically requests details of deposit funding and may seek supporting evidence; AIB requests statements for relevant accounts outside AIB.

**Important distinction:** The **source of funds** is where your deposit came from. **Repayment capacity** is whether income and sustainable spending allow you to afford future mortgage payments. Your investment record may contribute useful evidence to the first question without automatically satisfying the second.

## How would you withdraw money from the proposed PIA to pay a deposit?

Under the draft PIA system, a practical path would likely be:

1. Decide how much of the deposit must be moved out of investments, including a buffer for fees and other purchase costs.
2. Sell the appropriate eligible holdings in the PIA, subject to market conditions, any product restrictions and provider dealing costs.
3. Allow the trade to settle, then request withdrawal using the provider's supported process.
4. Transfer the cleared money to the receiving bank account and retain the full statement trail.
5. Confirm with your lender and solicitor that the money is **accessible and acceptable** before committing to legally binding deadlines.

This is a **plausible operational sequence**, not a commitment that a particular 2027 provider will support a specified settlement timetable.

The proposed PIA regime would not impose an ordinary Irish CGT or fund exit-tax charge merely because a qualifying investment inside the account was sold and the proceeds withdrawn. But it is **not entirely tax-free**: under the Bill an annual **1% account-value tax applies to the portion of the average daily PIA value above €50,000**, with separate consequences for excess contributions and potential foreign taxes or fees.

Also, a withdrawal would **not reset the €12,000 annual contribution allowance**. That matters if you later change your mind about buying, or want to put the same money straight back into your PIA. See [how the PIA daily-value tax is modelled](/pia/daily-valuation/) and [our full PIA calculator](/pia-calculator/).

## Does regular ETF investing demonstrate mortgage repayment capacity?

**It may help document a consistent allocation of income, but it is not a guaranteed substitute for lender-recognised regular savings.** The published [CCPC](https://www.ccpc.ie/manage-your-money/buying-a-home/about-mortgages/understanding-mortgages/applying-for-a-mortgage) and [AIB](https://www.aib.ie/help-and-guidance/mortgages-faq/what-lenders-look-for-in-bank-statements-for-mortgage) guidance specifically recognises regular saving and sound current-account management. Neither source supplies a universal formula translating monthly brokerage purchases into mortgage affordability.

If you invest €750 each month and can afford every bill, that may look very different from frequently transferring money to cover trading losses. **But the bank still needs to understand the cash flow**. If you are going to apply, ask the mortgage adviser or broker to clarify:

- Can regular transfers into a PIA or brokerage account be considered evidence of a sustained capacity to set money aside?
- Will the lender need to see **cash savings** separately from investments for a specified number of months?
- At what stage must the deposit be **liquidated into an accessible bank account**?
- How will the lender value a volatile fund or share portfolio before drawdown?
- What statements are needed to document the origin of sale proceeds?
- If funds are held in one partner's sole PIA, what evidence of transfer or contribution will be required for a joint mortgage?

These questions are more reliable than hoping a lender sees a bank-statement line marked "investment" and interprets it as a savings standing order.

## Should first-time buyers choose a PIA or a regular savings account?

The answer depends on the **job the money has to do**, not whether one account has more attractive tax treatment.

A PIA is intended for **holding eligible investments** under a simpler proposed tax system. It is **not designed as a guaranteed capital-protection product** and does not remove investment risk. A deposit savings account, by contrast, prioritises **certainty and access** rather than equity-market growth. Deposit-protection terms also differ from protections for investment holdings; check the provider and product.

It can be reasonable to have **both**: cash set aside for a likely home purchase and long-term investments for goals with a more flexible horizon. If your house-purchase date becomes more certain, revisit the level of money exposed to share-price risk.

There is a further question for people who already invest outside a PIA: **moving existing securities into a future PIA is not automatically the same as transferring a PIA between providers**. Under the published draft, the contribution route is generally money; selling outside assets first could crystallise ordinary Irish tax, depending on the instrument. Our [invest now versus wait for the PIA comparison](/pia/invest-now-or-wait/) and [PIA investment eligibility guide](/pia/investment-eligibility/) explain those separate issues.

## Mortgage deposit and PIA: frequently asked questions

### Can a bank accept money withdrawn from an investment account as a house deposit?

**Potentially**, where the borrower can evidence ownership, source and access to the funds, and the mortgage lender is satisfied. Shares are explicitly listed in Bank of Ireland's January 2026 application under **other assets**, but that is not an assurance that every invested asset counts at full market value as immediately usable deposit cash.

### If I invest €750 a month instead of saving €750, will the bank see that as savings?

**Not necessarily in the same way.** It demonstrates a regular transfer and may help show disposable income, but published lender guidance tends to discuss **regular saving**. Ask your lender or broker what it will accept as evidence of repayment capacity and whether it needs a distinct cash savings record.

### Does day trading make you ineligible for a mortgage in Ireland?

**There is no verified blanket lender rule to that effect.** Frequent or speculative trading may create questions about losses, risk and the consistency of your finances. It is different from documented gambling transactions, about which Bank of Ireland and the CCPC publish specific guidance.

### Will a mortgage lender classify Trading 212, Revolut or an S&P 500 ETF purchase as gambling?

No basis has been found to claim that **ordinary regulated brokerage transfers** or fund purchases are automatically treated as gambling. The lender may still examine statements and decide whether the transactions affect affordability. A specific provider's future PIA eligibility or product menu should not be assumed.

### Can I withdraw the full amount from an Irish PIA for a house deposit?

The proposed framework does not impose a general holding lock-in or separate withdrawal tax. The amount available in practice would depend on **real market value, sale proceeds, product dealing conditions, provider fees and processing times**. The account is not currently live.

### If I withdraw €12,000 can I deposit €12,000 again later that year?

**Not under the currently proposed contribution rule** if you have already used the full annual allowance. Contributions are counted **gross**, without deducting withdrawals. Read [the PIA rules guide](/wealth/personal-investment-account-ireland/) before planning to withdraw and redeposit.

### Will an S&P 500 ETF always be allowed inside a PIA?

No. Eligibility depends on the **exact fund structure and share class** and on the provider's offered investments. For an ETF, check its UCITS status, domicile, ISIN and future account provider's own list. [PIA eligibility guide](/pia/investment-eligibility/).

### Is a €40,000 PIA balance enough for a €400,000 home?

It equals the **standard 10% minimum mortgage deposit** only while it remains worth €40,000, and assumes the purchase falls within that rule. You still need additional funds for transaction costs and the lender must approve affordability. If the portfolio falls 25%, it is worth €30,000, leaving a **€10,000 deposit shortfall** before other costs.

### Does PIA investing replace my need to show six months of good bank statements?

No. Mortgage lenders still assess income, outgoings, borrowings and account management. Document requests vary by lender and circumstances. Bank of Ireland's January 2026 form requests six months of non-BOI current-account statements and twelve months of savings history for relevant accounts.

### Can a couple each withdraw from a separate PIA for a joint mortgage deposit?

The draft PIA accounts are **individually owned**, not joint. Two qualifying adults could potentially combine legitimate funds for a joint purchase, subject to lender/solicitor verification, funding ownership and any tax or legal issues. See [PIAs for couples in Ireland](/wealth/pia-for-couples-ireland/) for the separate contribution limits and ownership rules.

## A practical plan before relying on a PIA to buy a home

If you intend to use invested funds for a mortgage deposit, first work out the **real cost of buying**, including the deposit, stamp duty and professional fees. Use the [House Deposit Calculator](/house-deposit-calculator/) and [Home Buying Costs Calculator](/house-buying-costs-calculator/) rather than treating the minimum 10% as the entire cash requirement.

Next, check both **your ability to repay** and **how you will evidence the deposit**. Keep enough accessible money to cover the home-buying timetable, and consider how a sharp market fall could affect your plans.

The core distinction is simple: **disciplined long-term investing, active trading and gambling are not identical financial behaviours.** Irish lenders can review all three in their broader assessment of risk and affordability. The important questions are what your records show, whether you can meet repayments, and whether your deposit will actually be available when you need it.
