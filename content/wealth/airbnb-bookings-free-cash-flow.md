**Airbnb’s marketplace accelerated in the third quarter, but its cash did not keep pace. The investment question is whether stronger bookings can translate into faster per-share cash growth after stock compensation and buybacks.**

For the three months ended 30 September 2026, Airbnb reported **$27.7 billion of gross booking value, up 21%**, and **$4.87 billion of revenue, up 19%**. Nights and seats booked increased 16% to 155 million. Those are healthy demand figures rather than a purely price-led result.

Quarterly free cash flow, however, increased from $1.35 billion to **$1.43 billion**, or about 6%. Its margin fell from 33% to 29%. Over the trailing twelve months, free cash flow rose about 8% to $4.90 billion while its margin slipped one percentage point to 35%.

For Airbnb’s maintained financial record, see the [Airbnb stock profile](/stocks/abnb/). This article asks whether the marketplace’s renewed momentum is reaching shareholders efficiently.

## Demand is doing more than pricing

Gross booking value grew faster than nights booked because the average daily rate rose 4% to $178.83. That price-and-mix contribution matters, but volume supplied most of the growth.

The result also appeared across regions. Airbnb’s shareholder letter said nights booked grew by double digits in every region, with Asia Pacific the fastest. The company highlighted more first-time bookers and more users booking through the app. Those are management observations, not independent proof of customer loyalty, but they point to a broader demand base than a single-market rebound.

Revenue grew slightly slower than gross booking value. This can happen because of timing, the mix of services and changes in the relationship between booking value and fees. Investors should not assume that every dollar of marketplace growth will produce the same revenue—or profit—over time.

## Profit rose faster than cash

Net income increased to **$1.89 billion from $1.37 billion**, while adjusted EBITDA rose to $2.51 billion from $2.05 billion. The adjusted EBITDA margin reached 52% for the quarter.

That is strong operating leverage. Yet quarterly operating cash flow rose only 11% to $1.51 billion, and company-defined free cash flow rose about 6% after capital expenditure. Free cash flow is a non-GAAP measure here, calculated as operating cash flow less purchases of property and equipment.

One quarter is not enough to diagnose a structural cash-conversion problem. Airbnb collects and remits large amounts on behalf of guests and hosts, so timing can move working capital. Taxes and other settlement items can also make profit and cash diverge. The trailing-twelve-month comparison is therefore more useful—and it still shows free-cash-flow growth lagging revenue.

The next test is straightforward: if bookings and revenue remain in the mid-to-high teens, cash growth should eventually close some of the gap without relying on favourable working-capital timing.

## Stock compensation changes the buyback test

Stock-based compensation was **$462 million** in the quarter, up from $399 million, and $1.36 billion for the first nine months. It is added back in the cash-flow statement because it is non-cash in the period, but it is not costless to existing owners. Issuing shares can dilute their claim on the business.

Airbnb repurchased $700 million of shares during the quarter and authorised an additional $12 billion programme. Management says its fully diluted share count has fallen by approximately 10% since repurchases began in the third quarter of 2022, driven by more than $17 billion of repurchases and cash used for employee tax obligations.

That reduction is important: these buybacks have done more than merely disguise all dilution. But the capital required was substantial, and a falling share count alone does not establish that repurchases created value. The price paid determines how much future cash flow each remaining shareholder gains.

The cleanest scorecard combines three items: stock compensation, cash spent on repurchases and the actual diluted share count. Cash flow per diluted share should improve faster than aggregate cash flow if capital allocation is working well.

## The balance sheet provides room—but not a free pass

Airbnb ended the quarter with **$12.65 billion of cash and other liquid assets**. That gives it flexibility to invest, make acquisitions or repurchase shares without relying on new debt.

Management expects fourth-quarter revenue of $3.22 billion to $3.28 billion, representing 16%–18% growth, including an estimated two-percentage-point foreign-exchange tailwind. It also expects full-year revenue growth in the high teens and an adjusted EBITDA margin of about 36%.

Those are forecasts, not achieved results. The quarter’s 52% adjusted EBITDA margin is seasonally strong and should not be treated as the annual run rate. Management also expects fourth-quarter margin to be slightly lower year over year, partly because of marketing timing.

## What would confirm the thesis?

The strongest outcome would combine continued double-digit nights growth with trailing free-cash-flow growth moving closer to revenue growth. Stock compensation should moderate as a percentage of revenue, while diluted shares keep falling without buybacks consuming an ever-larger share of cash.

The counter-case is that booking growth remains impressive but cash conversion keeps slipping, marketing or product investment absorbs more of the revenue gain, and stock compensation forces Airbnb to spend heavily just to deliver modest per-share progress.

**Airbnb’s demand acceleration looks real. The unresolved question is how efficiently that growth becomes cash for each remaining share.** Bookings are the starting point; cash conversion and the net share count are the better measures of whether the buyback is earning its keep.
