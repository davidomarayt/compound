**Investing in Ireland 101 · Chapter 3 of 9**

[← Pension vs investing](/wealth/pension-vs-investing-ireland/) · [Course hub](/wealth/how-to-start-investing-in-ireland/) · [Next: Investment tax →](/wealth/investment-tax-ireland/)

A share, an ETF and an investment fund can all give you exposure to companies.

But they solve different problems.

The biggest beginner mistake is choosing from the label alone.

An ETF can be diversified or concentrated. A fund can be cheap or expensive. A share can be an excellent company and still be a poor portfolio if it is your only holding.

In Ireland there is another complication: **tax treatment can differ materially depending on what you own and how the product is structured**.

## What is a direct share?

A share is a small ownership interest in a company.

If you buy shares in one company, your return depends heavily on that company's:

- profits;
- cash flow;
- competition;
- balance sheet;
- management;
- industry;
- valuation;
- and future expectations.

The upside is direct ownership and a relatively simple economic structure.

The downside is concentration.

A great company can still have a falling share price if expectations were too high. A profitable company can cut its dividend. A dominant business can be disrupted.

Owning ten companies is more diversified than owning one, but it can still be highly concentrated if they are all in the same sector or country.

## What is an ETF?

An exchange-traded fund is a pooled investment that trades on an exchange.

One ETF can hold:

- hundreds of global companies;
- only US technology companies;
- government bonds;
- corporate bonds;
- commodities;
- a single country;
- a narrow theme;
- or a leveraged strategy.

That is why “ETF” does not mean “diversified”.

The **index or mandate** matters more than the acronym.

A broad global equity ETF and a leveraged semiconductor ETF are both ETFs but have very different risk profiles.

## What is an investment fund?

A fund pools money from investors and invests it according to a stated mandate.

Funds can be:

- actively managed;
- index-tracking;
- equity-focused;
- bond-focused;
- multi-asset;
- property-related;
- or designed for a particular risk level.

Some are exchange traded. Others are bought and sold directly through a provider.

The important questions are:

1. What does the fund own?
2. How diversified is it?
3. What does it cost?
4. What legal structure is it?
5. What tax regime applies in Ireland?

## Shares vs ETFs vs funds

| Feature | Direct share | ETF | Non-exchange-traded fund |
|---|---|---|---|
| What you own | Interest in one company | Units in a pooled vehicle | Units in a pooled vehicle |
| Diversification | Depends on how many shares you own | Can be very broad or very narrow | Can be very broad or very narrow |
| Trading | Exchange | Exchange | Usually provider/fund dealing process |
| Ongoing fund fee | None at company level | Usually yes | Usually yes |
| Company-specific risk | High if concentrated | Lower in broad funds | Lower in broad funds |
| Irish tax | Often CGT + dividend rules for ordinary shares | Depends on structure/domicile; fund regime may apply | Fund regime may apply |

The tax row is deliberately cautious because product classification matters.

## Why a broad fund can reduce avoidable risk

Suppose your entire portfolio is one Irish company.

A company-specific problem can damage almost all of your portfolio.

Now imagine a broad fund holding hundreds or thousands of companies across countries and industries.

One corporate failure becomes a small part of the total portfolio.

The broad fund can still fall because markets fall together. Diversification does not remove market risk.

It reduces the amount of **single-company risk you were never paid to need**.

## The Irish tax question changes the comparison

Imported investing advice often stops at:

> “Buy the cheapest broad ETF.”

For an Irish resident, that can be incomplete.

Revenue confirmed that from **1 January 2026** the tax rate for individuals on Irish-domiciled investment funds and equivalent offshore investment funds in relevant countries was reduced from 41% to **38%**.

Where the investment-fund regime applies, the eight-year deemed-disposal rule can also apply.

Direct shares, by contrast, are commonly taxed under Capital Gains Tax rules on disposal, with dividend income taxed separately.

That means two investments with similar market exposure can have different after-tax outcomes.

Read [Investment Tax in Ireland](/wealth/investment-tax-ireland/) before choosing a product solely on its pre-tax return or fee.

## Accumulating vs distributing funds

A **distributing** fund pays investment income out to the investor.

An **accumulating** fund generally reinvests income inside the fund.

Accumulating does not automatically mean “tax deferred until sale” for an Irish investor. If the relevant investment-fund rules apply, the fund can still be subject to the Irish exit-tax and deemed-disposal regime.

This is a common point of confusion because tax treatment in other countries can be very different.

## Active versus passive funds

A passive fund generally aims to track an index.

An active fund generally gives a manager discretion to choose securities.

The useful comparison is not ideological. It is measurable:

- mandate;
- diversification;
- tracking quality;
- ongoing cost;
- turnover;
- risk;
- tax treatment;
- and evidence that the service justifies the fee.

A higher fee creates a higher hurdle because the portfolio must earn more before the investor receives the same net return.

## What to check before buying any ETF or fund

### 1. The index or mandate

Do not stop at the product name.

Look at the actual holdings and weighting method.

A “global” fund can still have a large US weighting. A “technology” fund may be concentrated in a handful of companies.

### 2. The domicile

The legal domicile can affect taxation and investor protections.

For Irish tax, do not assume that two ETFs tracking the same index are treated identically.

### 3. The legal structure

Check whether the product is an ETF, UCITS fund, investment trust, company or another structure.

A ticker symbol is not a tax category.

### 4. Total cost

Look beyond the headline ongoing charge.

Potential costs include:

- fund expense ratio;
- broker commission;
- platform fee;
- bid–ask spread;
- foreign-exchange conversion;
- custody charge;
- and transfer fees.

### 5. Tracking difference

An index fund's published fee is not the entire story.

What matters to the investor is how closely the fund's actual performance follows the index after costs, taxes and implementation.

### 6. Liquidity

Large, widely traded ETFs often have tighter dealing spreads than small niche products.

For long-term investors this may be less important than tax and annual cost, but it is still part of the real purchase price.

## A direct-share example

Suppose you invest €10,000 into one company.

If the company falls 50%, your portfolio is €5,000.

Now suppose €10,000 is spread equally across 100 companies and one of them goes to zero while the other 99 are unchanged.

The direct effect of that one failure is roughly 1% of the portfolio.

Real portfolios are not equally weighted and companies move together, but the example shows what diversification is trying to achieve.

## Does diversification mean owning more funds?

Not necessarily.

Owning five funds that all hold the same mega-cap US companies can create the appearance of diversification without much additional diversification underneath.

Look through the labels.

Ask what the portfolio actually owns.

## When direct shares can make sense

Direct shares can be appropriate for investors who:

- understand company-specific risk;
- are willing to research businesses;
- can build enough diversification;
- understand the tax and record-keeping requirements;
- and are not relying on one stock to fund an essential goal.

That does not make direct stock selection superior or inferior. It makes it a different job.

## When a broad fund can make sense

A broad fund can simplify diversification by packaging many holdings into one investment.

But the Irish investor still needs to check:

- the product's legal structure;
- tax treatment;
- ongoing fee;
- platform costs;
- and whether the underlying exposure actually matches the goal.

## Do not choose the product before the portfolio

A useful sequence is:

**Goal → time horizon → wrapper → asset allocation → product.**

Many beginners reverse that sequence:

**Product → excitement → tax surprise → portfolio problem.**

Chapter 4 deals with the tax layer in detail.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are ETFs better than shares in Ireland?

They solve different problems. A broad ETF can provide diversification more easily than a small number of direct shares, but Irish tax treatment and product structure must be checked.

### Are all ETFs subject to deemed disposal?

Do not assume all products with “ETF” in the name have identical tax treatment. Revenue's ETF guidance makes clear that the applicable treatment depends on the underlying legal and tax classification.

### Is an accumulating ETF tax free until I sell?

Not necessarily. Where Ireland's investment-fund regime applies, deemed disposal can create a taxable event after eight years even if you have not sold.

### How many shares are enough to diversify?

There is no magic number. Diversification depends on sectors, countries, position sizes and how correlated the businesses are, not just the count of tickers.
