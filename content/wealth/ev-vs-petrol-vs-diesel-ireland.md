Choosing between an EV, petrol car and diesel car in Ireland looks simple until you price the **whole decision**.

An electric car can cost much less per kilometre when it is charged cheaply at home.

A diesel can still be very efficient on long motorway journeys.

A petrol car can make more financial sense for a low-mileage driver — especially when the alternative is spending a large amount simply to change cars.

So the useful question is not:

> **Which fuel is cheapest?**

It is:

> **Which car will cost me least from today until I sell it?**

<div class="cs-intro">
<p class="cs-kicker">Quick answer</p>
<p><strong>For a driver who can charge mostly at home, an EV will usually have the lowest energy cost per kilometre.</strong> In our worked 15,000 km example using September 2026 fuel prices, the EV costs about €671 a year for electricity, versus about €1,515 for diesel and €1,822 for petrol.</p>
<p>But that does <strong>not</strong> automatically make the EV the cheapest car to own. Purchase price, depreciation, finance, insurance, motor tax, charger installation, servicing and the value of the car you already own can outweigh several years of energy savings.</p>
</div>

This guide reflects Irish rules and prices checked on **4 October 2026**.

**Budget 2027 is due on 6 October 2026**, so fuel-tax measures, EV supports or other motoring measures may change shortly after publication. Compound will update the guide when the final measures are known rather than creating a new URL.

There is no universal winner because two people driving the same annual kilometres can have completely different costs.

The most important dividing line for an EV is often **whether you can charge at home**.

## What fuel and electricity cost in Ireland right now

AA Ireland's September 2026 national average pump prices were:

- **petrol: 186.86 cent per litre**
- **diesel: 194.20 cent per litre**

Those are a snapshot, not a five-year forecast.

Fuel prices can move quickly because of wholesale oil prices, refining, currency, tax and temporary government measures.

Electricity is different because there is no single "EV charging price".

You might pay:

- a low overnight home rate;
- a standard household rate;
- a destination-charging rate;
- a public AC rate;
- or a much higher rapid/DC charging rate.

That makes an EV comparison highly sensitive to **where the electricity comes from**.

## Worked example: 15,000 km a year

To make the comparison concrete, assume one driver covers **15,000 km a year**.

These are deliberately transparent assumptions rather than claims that every car will achieve the same result.

### Petrol assumption

- real-world consumption: **6.5 L/100 km**
- September 2026 fuel price: **€1.8686/L**

Annual fuel used:

**15,000 ÷ 100 × 6.5 = 975 litres**

Annual petrol cost:

**975 × €1.8686 = about €1,822**

Cost per 100 km:

**about €12.15**

### Diesel assumption

- real-world consumption: **5.2 L/100 km**
- September 2026 fuel price: **€1.9420/L**

Annual fuel used:

**15,000 ÷ 100 × 5.2 = 780 litres**

Annual diesel cost:

**780 × €1.9420 = about €1,515**

Cost per 100 km:

**about €10.10**

### EV assumption

Assume:

- real-world vehicle efficiency: **17.5 kWh/100 km**
- charging losses: **10%**
- 80% of charging at home at **€0.15/kWh**
- 20% public charging at **€0.55/kWh**

The weighted electricity price is:

**(80% × €0.15) + (20% × €0.55) = €0.23/kWh**

After allowing for 10% charging loss, the grid needs to supply about:

**19.44 kWh per 100 km**

Annual electricity cost:

**15,000 ÷ 100 × 19.44 × €0.23 = about €671**

Cost per 100 km:

**about €4.47**

Under those assumptions, the EV saves approximately:

- **€844 a year versus diesel**
- **€1,151 a year versus petrol**

Over five years, before considering changes in prices, that is roughly:

- **€4,220 versus diesel**
- **€5,755 versus petrol**

Those numbers are meaningful.

But they also show why the **purchase-price gap** matters so much.

If changing into the EV costs you €12,000 more than the alternative car, a €1,151 annual energy saving does not magically erase that extra capital cost.

## The most important EV calculation: the price gap

Suppose the EV you want costs **€8,000 more** after grants, discounts and trade-in than the petrol alternative.

If your annual energy saving is **€1,151**, then the simple energy-only payback is:

**€8,000 ÷ €1,151 = about 7 years**

That ignores:

- finance interest;
- depreciation;
- motor tax;
- servicing;
- insurance;
- charger installation;
- repairs;
- resale value;
- and changing fuel/electricity prices.

The point is not that seven years is good or bad.

The point is that an EV purchase should be evaluated against the **extra cost of choosing it**, not against the full purchase price.

## Do not replace a good car just to save fuel

This is one of the biggest financial traps in motoring.

Imagine you already own a reliable petrol car worth €10,000.

A newer EV would cost €30,000 after support.

You are not comparing:

**€30,000 EV versus €30,000 petrol.**

You are comparing:

**keep the €10,000 car versus spend roughly €20,000 net to change.**

Even if the EV saves €1,200 a year in energy, it could take many years for fuel savings alone to justify the cash required to change.

That does not mean you should never upgrade.

You may value:

- safety;
- reliability;
- comfort;
- lower emissions;
- newer technology;
- warranty;
- or simply wanting a new car.

But those are separate reasons.

A lower running cost should not be presented as proof that changing car is automatically a saving.

## When an EV makes the strongest financial case

An EV becomes particularly compelling when several of these are true:

- you can **charge at home**;
- a large share of charging can happen on a cheap overnight tariff;
- you drive a reasonable annual distance;
- the EV purchase price is close to the petrol/diesel alternative;
- you expect to keep the car for several years;
- you are buying new and qualify for support;
- you can avoid expensive finance;
- and the EV has adequate range for your normal journeys.

A driver doing 20,000–25,000 km a year with cheap home charging has much more opportunity to recover an upfront premium than someone doing 6,000 km.

## When petrol can still be the rational choice

Petrol often deserves more credit in low-mileage comparisons.

A petrol car may make financial sense where:

- annual mileage is low;
- most journeys are short or mixed;
- the petrol version is materially cheaper to buy;
- you cannot charge at home;
- you already own a reliable petrol car;
- or you value rapid refuelling without planning charging stops.

At low annual mileage, the difference between €4 and €12 per 100 km sounds large but produces a smaller annual euro amount.

At 5,000 km a year, an €8-per-100-km difference is only about:

**€400 a year.**

That can be overwhelmed by depreciation or finance very easily.

## When diesel still makes sense

Diesel remains strongest where its traditional advantages are actually being used.

That generally means:

- high annual mileage;
- frequent long journeys;
- sustained motorway driving;
- good real-world fuel economy;
- and a purchase price that makes sense.

A diesel doing repeated long runs can deliver excellent litres-per-100-km figures.

But modern diesel economics are not just fuel economy.

Depending on the vehicle, maintenance and repair exposure can include components such as:

- diesel particulate filters;
- exhaust-gas recirculation systems;
- AdBlue / SCR systems;
- turbochargers;
- and other emissions-control hardware.

That does not mean every diesel is unreliable.

It means a driver doing mostly short urban trips may be paying for diesel efficiency without using the car in the conditions where diesel is strongest.

## The home-charging advantage

Home charging changes the EV equation more than almost anything else.

SEAI currently offers an **Electric Vehicle Home Charger Grant of up to €300** toward qualifying purchase and installation costs.

The grant does not mean the charger will cost €300.

Your final cost depends on:

- charger model;
- electrical work;
- cable route;
- distance from the distribution board;
- earthing / protection requirements;
- installation complexity;
- and whether other upgrades are needed.

But once a home charger is installed, the car can become a large flexible household load.

That can allow charging to be scheduled into cheap night or smart-tariff windows.

Use Compound's [Electricity Tariffs in Ireland guide](/wealth/electricity-tariffs-ireland/) before assuming the cheapest advertised EV rate applies to every kWh you use.

## What if you cannot charge at home?

This is where the answer can change sharply.

If an EV owner relies heavily on public rapid charging, the energy-cost advantage can narrow substantially.

Using the same EV efficiency from our example:

- 17.5 kWh/100 km at the battery;
- 10% charging losses;
- about 19.44 kWh bought per 100 km.

At **€0.55/kWh**, electricity alone would cost about:

**€10.69 per 100 km.**

That is very close to the **€10.10 per 100 km** diesel example.

Public-charging convenience, subscriptions, charger location and pricing therefore matter.

An EV can still be the right car without home charging, but the claim that it is automatically dramatically cheaper to fuel becomes much weaker.

## EV grants in Ireland in 2026

SEAI currently provides a maximum **€3,500 grant** for a qualifying new privately purchased M1 battery-electric passenger car.

For private M1 cars, the current scheme applies to qualifying cars within the stated price limits, with cars above **€50,000 full price** not receiving the grant.

The dealer applies for the grant and deducts it from the agreed price.

Important distinctions:

- the grant is for **new qualifying BEVs**;
- it is not a general grant for used EVs;
- plug-in hybrids are not treated as BEVs for the purchase grant;
- and scheme conditions can change.

The separate **ICE2EV scrappage pilot launched in 2026 is already closed to new applications** after reaching its target. Do not include the former €5,000 scrappage amount in a current purchase calculation unless you already had an approved application.

## VRT can favour a new EV — but check the actual car

Revenue currently provides VRT relief for qualifying battery-electric vehicles registered before **31 December 2026**.

The relief can be worth up to **€5,000**.

For passenger cars:

- an OMSP up to €40,000 can receive relief up to the €5,000 maximum;
- relief tapers above €40,000;
- and vehicles above €50,000 OMSP do not receive the EV VRT relief.

That is separate from the SEAI purchase grant.

For petrol and diesel Category A passenger vehicles, VRT is based on the applicable CO2 rate multiplied by the car's Open Market Selling Price, with the NOx charge then added.

Revenue's current CO2-based VRT rates range from **7%** for the lowest-emission band up to **41%** for vehicles above 190 g/km.

The NOx charge applies to Category A vehicles excluding electrics.

This is one reason a simple "list price before tax" comparison is not useful for an Irish buyer.

Compare the **actual on-the-road cash price** you have been quoted.

## Motor tax

A battery EV currently sits at **€120 annual motor tax**.

Petrol and diesel private cars can be taxed under different systems depending on first-registration date.

For newer cars, the rate is based on CO2 emissions.

Older cars may fall under earlier CO2 bands or engine-capacity rules.

Therefore, do not use a generic "petrol tax" or "diesel tax" number.

Check the exact vehicle registration or emissions band on Motor Tax Online.

Over five years, even a few hundred euro difference per year can become material — but it is still usually smaller than depreciation.

## Servicing: EV advantage, but not zero maintenance

A battery EV removes many combustion-engine service items.

There is no:

- engine oil;
- oil filter;
- spark plug;
- timing belt in the combustion-engine sense;
- exhaust system;
- or diesel particulate filter.

That can reduce routine servicing complexity.

But an EV still needs:

- tyres;
- brakes;
- suspension;
- cabin filters;
- air-conditioning maintenance;
- wipers;
- wheel alignment;
- fluids;
- inspection;
- and eventually wear-item replacement.

Regenerative braking can reduce friction-brake use, but brakes can still corrode or require servicing.

Tyres can also be an important cost because EVs are often relatively heavy and can deliver high torque.

Do not treat "fewer moving parts" as "no maintenance".

## Diesel servicing and short journeys

Diesel engines can be excellent at sustained long-distance work.

Repeated short trips can be a worse fit.

Modern diesel emissions systems are designed around operating conditions that may include reaching sufficient temperature for proper regeneration and emissions control.

A driver who does 30,000 motorway kilometres a year is using diesel very differently from someone driving 5 km to work and back.

That is why the best fuel type is partly about **journey pattern**, not only annual kilometres.

## Depreciation can overwhelm every other number

For many cars, depreciation is the biggest ownership cost.

A car losing €12,000 in value over three years has effectively cost:

**€4,000 a year in depreciation**

before fuel, insurance, tax, servicing or finance.

That dwarfs a few hundred euro difference in annual fuel cost.

EV resale values can move as:

- new-EV prices change;
- battery technology improves;
- incentives change;
- manufacturers discount new stock;
- consumer demand changes;
- and used buyers reassess range expectations.

Petrol and diesel cars have their own residual-value risks as tax, emissions rules and buyer preferences evolve.

No one can know the exact resale value today.

The answer is to **stress-test it**.

Do not make the EV win only because you assume optimistic EV resale and pessimistic petrol/diesel resale — or vice versa.

## Finance can erase running-cost savings

Suppose one car saves €900 a year in fuel but costs €10,000 more.

If that €10,000 is financed, you are not only paying the extra capital cost.

You are also paying interest on it.

A low monthly PCP payment can make the more expensive vehicle look affordable because a large amount is deferred into the final balloon.

For a proper comparison, use the **same deposit and ownership outcome**.

Compound's [Car Finance Calculator](/car-finance-calculator/) compares personal loan, Hire Purchase and PCP while keeping the cash price, deposit and final ownership position visible.

Our [PCP vs HP vs Car Loan guide](/wealth/pcp-vs-hp-vs-car-loan-ireland/) explains why monthly payment alone is a poor way to compare cars.

## What about insurance?

There is no reliable rule that says one fuel type is always cheaper to insure.

Premiums depend on factors such as:

- driver profile;
- address;
- licence history;
- claims history;
- vehicle value;
- repair cost;
- performance;
- theft risk;
- parts availability;
- and insurer pricing.

A high-performance EV can be more expensive to insure than a modest petrol car.

A mainstream EV may not be.

Get actual quotes before buying if the insurance difference could alter the economics.

## Range and motorway driving

EV range is not a fixed number.

It changes with:

- motorway speed;
- temperature;
- heating and air-conditioning;
- wind;
- rain;
- tyre pressure;
- load;
- wheel and tyre specification;
- and battery temperature.

A driver whose normal day is 60 km and who charges at home has a very different range problem from someone covering 500 km several times a week.

Do not buy range you never use simply because the largest battery looks safer.

But do not buy a car whose realistic winter motorway range forces charging stops you know you will resent.

## Ireland's charging network is expanding

The Department of Transport's draft National EV Charging Infrastructure Strategy 2026–2028 describes home charging as the primary charging method for most EV users while planning further neighbourhood, destination and en-route infrastructure.

That direction matters.

Public charging should improve over the ownership life of a car.

But a purchase decision should still be based on the **network you can realistically use now**, not only promised future chargers.

Check the routes you actually drive.

## Used EV versus used petrol or diesel

The used market changes the calculation because the first owner may already have absorbed a large amount of depreciation.

A used EV can therefore offer attractive running costs without the full new-car price premium.

But inspect the right things.

For a used EV, consider:

- battery state of health where reliable data are available;
- remaining battery warranty;
- real-world range;
- DC fast-charging capability;
- charging-port standard;
- service history;
- tyre condition;
- accident history;
- and whether charging suits your home and routes.

For a used petrol or diesel car, engine, gearbox, clutch, emissions systems, turbocharger and maintenance history can matter more than a small difference in quoted fuel economy.

The cheapest used car is not necessarily the one with the lowest asking price.

## Battery degradation

EV batteries do degrade over time.

But "battery degradation" should not be treated as synonymous with "battery failure".

A gradual reduction in usable capacity may simply mean the car has less range than when new.

For a used buyer, the important question is whether the remaining real-world range is adequate for the intended use and whether the battery is still covered by a manufacturer warranty.

Battery replacement is potentially expensive, so unusual battery faults need proper diagnosis.

That is different from assuming every older EV will require a new battery.

## Petrol, diesel or EV for commuting?

A regular commute can be ideal for an EV where:

- the daily distance is comfortably within range;
- the car can charge at home overnight;
- and the annual mileage is high enough for energy savings to matter.

Petrol can work well where the commute is short and the buyer wants a cheaper or simpler entry price.

Diesel can be attractive where the commute is long, especially when much of it is motorway.

The journey pattern matters more than the label on the fuel flap.

## What if you have solar panels?

Solar can reduce the amount of electricity bought from the grid to charge an EV.

But solar charging is not literally free.

A solar kWh used in the car has an opportunity cost if it could otherwise:

- power another household load;
- charge a battery;
- or be exported for payment.

If your overnight import rate is very cheap while your export rate is attractive, charging the EV overnight and exporting daytime solar can sometimes make more financial sense than automatically diverting every solar kWh into the car.

Use the [Solar + EV + Battery Optimiser](/solar-ev-battery-optimiser/) if you are trying to coordinate the three.

## The three numbers to calculate before buying

Before choosing the drivetrain, calculate these three numbers.

### 1. Net cost to change

**New car cash price − value received for your current car − grants / genuine discounts**

This tells you how much additional capital you are committing.

### 2. Annual running-cost difference

Include:

- fuel / electricity;
- motor tax;
- insurance;
- routine servicing;
- likely maintenance;
- charger cost where relevant.

### 3. Expected value when you sell

This is uncertain, so use a range.

Run:

- pessimistic;
- central;
- optimistic.

If your conclusion changes completely with a small resale-value change, the decision is not robust.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an EV cheaper than petrol in Ireland?

On energy cost alone, an EV charged mainly at home is usually much cheaper per kilometre than a comparable petrol car. Overall ownership cost can still be higher if the EV costs materially more to buy, finance or depreciates more than expected.

### Is an EV cheaper than diesel?

Often on energy cost, particularly with cheap home charging. Diesel can remain competitive for high-mileage motorway drivers, especially if the diesel car is materially cheaper to buy or the EV would rely heavily on public rapid charging.

### How much does it cost to charge an EV in Ireland?

There is no single rate. Multiply the electricity drawn from the meter by the price per kWh. Include charging losses and use a blended rate if you charge partly at home and partly on public chargers.

### What is the EV grant in Ireland in 2026?

SEAI currently offers a maximum €3,500 grant for a qualifying new privately purchased M1 battery EV, subject to scheme and price conditions.

### Is there a grant for a used EV?

The standard SEAI private M1 purchase grant applies to qualifying new BEVs, not second-hand cars.

### What is the EV home charger grant?

SEAI currently offers up to €300 toward the purchase and installation of a qualifying home charge point, subject to the scheme conditions.

### How much is EV motor tax in Ireland?

The current annual motor tax for a battery EV is €120.

### Should I change my petrol car for an EV to save money?

Not automatically. Compare the net cost of changing car with the annual running-cost saving. Keeping a reliable car you already own can be cheaper than buying a new EV even when the EV is much cheaper per kilometre.

### Is diesel still worth buying in Ireland?

It can be for high-mileage drivers who do frequent long motorway journeys and buy the car at the right price. It is less compelling where most use is short-distance urban driving.

### Is public EV charging cheaper than diesel?

Not always. At high rapid-charging prices, an efficient EV's cost per 100 km can approach or even exceed that of an efficient diesel. Home charging is often what creates the largest EV energy-cost advantage.

## The bottom line

For most Irish drivers, the comparison can be reduced to four questions:

1. **How much will it cost me to change car?**
2. **How many kilometres do I actually drive?**
3. **Can I charge an EV cheaply at home?**
4. **What will each car probably be worth when I sell it?**

If you have home charging, drive meaningful annual mileage and can buy the EV without paying a large premium, the EV can be very difficult for petrol or diesel to beat on running costs.

If you drive very little, cannot charge at home, or already own a reliable paid-off car, changing purely for fuel savings can be financially weak.

The best comparison is therefore not EV versus petrol versus diesel in isolation.

It is:

> **the exact EV you can buy, versus the exact petrol or diesel alternative, versus keeping the car you already own.**

Use the [EV Charging Cost Calculator](/ev-charging-cost-calculator/) and [Fuel Cost Calculator](/fuel-cost-calculator/) to establish the energy difference, then use the [Lifetime Cost Calculator](/lifetime-cost-calculator/) and [Car Finance Calculator](/car-finance-calculator/) to test the full ownership decision.
