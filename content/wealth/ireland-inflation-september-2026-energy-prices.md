<p><em>News analysis · 2 October 2026. The September figures are a flash estimate of Ireland's Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP) and are subject to revision. The CSO's official September Consumer Price Index release is due on 8 October 2026.</em></p>

Ireland's inflation rate has moved sharply higher again.

The Central Statistics Office estimates that the **Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices rose by 3.9% in the 12 months to September 2026**, up from **3.4% in August**.

The clearest driver is energy.

Energy prices are estimated to have risen:

- **2.9% in September alone**;
- **15.3% over the past 12 months**.

Food prices, by contrast, were estimated to be unchanged in September and only **0.3% higher than a year earlier**.

Services were up **4.1% year-on-year**, while inflation excluding energy and unprocessed food was **2.8%**.

The important point is that the 3.9% figure is an average price index.

It does **not** mean every household's total spending has risen by exactly 3.9%.

A household that spends heavily on electricity, heating oil, petrol or diesel can feel a much bigger squeeze than one whose spending is concentrated in areas where prices have barely moved.

For that household-by-household picture, see Compound's recent analysis: **[Your inflation rate may be higher than the national average](/news/personal-inflation-rate-ireland-households-2026/)**.

## What does 3.9% inflation actually mean?

Inflation is easiest to understand in euro terms.

If a basket of goods and services cost **€1,000 a year ago**, a 3.9% rise would put the equivalent cost at approximately:

**€1,039**

If a household's spending basket were €3,000 per month and moved exactly in line with 3.9% inflation, that would be equivalent to about:

**€117 more per month**

or roughly:

**€1,404 more over a year**.

That is only an illustration. Real households buy different things in different proportions, so their personal inflation rate may be higher or lower.

## Energy is the real story

A 15.3% annual increase in the HICP energy category is much more significant than the overall 3.9% headline.

It does not mean every electricity or gas bill has risen by exactly 15.3%. The energy component covers a broader basket and households are on different tariffs.

But it shows where a large part of the current inflation pressure is coming from.

For a simple illustration, if a household had €200 per month of energy-related spending and that spending rose by 15.3%, the equivalent increase would be about:

**€30.60 per month**

or roughly **€367 a year**.

Again, that is an example rather than a forecast for a typical Irish household.

## What does this mean for a €50,000 salary?

Inflation does not reduce the number printed on a payslip.

It reduces what that income can buy if wages do not rise at the same pace.

At 3.9% inflation, maintaining the same gross nominal purchasing power as a €50,000 salary would require roughly:

**€51,950**

That is an increase of **€1,950**.

Another way to look at it: a €50,000 income after a year of 3.9% inflation has purchasing power equivalent to roughly **€48,123 in the previous year's prices**, before allowing for tax, pay changes or the household's actual spending pattern.

This is why wage growth and inflation need to be considered together.

A 3% pay rise can still leave someone worse off in real terms if the prices relevant to them are rising faster.

## What does 3.9% inflation do to cash savings?

Cash can lose purchasing power even when the account balance does not fall.

Take **€20,000** held for one year.

If prices rose by 3.9% and the money earned no interest, its purchasing power at the end of the year would be equivalent to roughly **€19,249 in today's money**.

## Could higher inflation affect mortgage rates?

Potentially — but not mechanically.

The European Central Bank's target is **2% inflation over the medium term**.

At its September meeting, the ECB raised its three key interest rates by **0.25 percentage points**, taking the deposit facility rate to **2.50%**, the main refinancing rate to **2.65%** and the marginal lending facility to **2.90%**.

The ECB has said future decisions will remain data-dependent.

Ireland's 3.9% HICP reading does not determine ECB policy on its own. The ECB sets rates for the entire euro area and looks at the inflation outlook, underlying inflation and monetary-policy transmission across the currency union.

But renewed inflation pressure — particularly if energy feeds through into other prices and wages — is relevant to the rate outlook.

For mortgage holders, the practical distinction is between:

- **fixed-rate borrowers**, whose payment normally stays unchanged until the fixed period ends;
- **tracker borrowers**, whose rate moves with the ECB refinancing rate plus the contractual margin;
- **variable-rate borrowers**, where the lender sets the rate.

## Why this does not contradict the 3.4% household inflation article

Compound published a separate inflation analysis earlier this week showing how different household types experienced different inflation rates, including younger households and renters.

That article used the CSO's household-characteristics analysis for the year to **June 2026**.

Today's 3.9% figure is different.

It is the **latest September 2026 flash HICP estimate for the economy as a whole**.

The two pieces answer different questions:

- the household analysis asks **who is being hit hardest?**
- the September release asks **how quickly prices are rising now?**

Together, they show why one headline inflation number never tells the entire story.

## What should households watch next?

The next key Irish release is the CSO's full **September Consumer Price Index on 8 October**.

That will provide more detailed category data and confirm the official CPI reading for the month.

For households, the most important areas to watch are:

1. whether energy inflation remains elevated;
2. whether higher energy costs begin feeding into other goods and services;
3. how wages compare with price growth;
4. what happens to ECB interest rates;
5. whether Budget 2027 contains measures aimed at energy or broader cost-of-living pressures.

## Bottom line

Ireland's latest inflation data points to a renewed cost-of-living squeeze.

The headline HICP rate has risen to **3.9%**, but the bigger warning sign is the **15.3% annual rise in energy prices**.

Food inflation remains much more subdued, while services are still rising at **4.1%**.

For households, the effect depends on what they spend money on, how quickly their income is rising and whether they hold cash, investments or debt.

The 3.9% number matters.

But for many households, the energy number may matter more.
