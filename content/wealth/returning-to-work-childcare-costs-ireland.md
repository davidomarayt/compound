A parent earns **€45,000 a year**.

Childcare costs **€320 a week**.

Is returning to work financially worth it?

At first glance, the calculation looks obvious:

**salary minus childcare**

But that is the wrong comparison.

A gross salary is not spendable household cash. Childcare may be subsidised. Returning to work creates costs beyond childcare. Staying at home can also change the household's tax position. And a year outside paid employment can affect pension contributions and future earnings in ways that do not appear on this month's bank statement.

A better question is:

> **How much additional household value does paid work create after tax, childcare and work-related costs — and what am I giving up if I look only at this year's cash flow?**

The [Childcare Cost vs Returning to Work Calculator](/childcare-return-to-work-calculator/) is designed around that question.

## The short answer

There is no universal salary at which returning to work suddenly becomes "worth it".

The result depends on:

- gross salary;
- PAYE, USC and PRSI;
- pension contributions;
- number of children in paid childcare;
- childcare hours;
- provider fees;
- National Childcare Scheme subsidy;
- commuting;
- meals, clothing and other work costs;
- whether you are married or in a civil partnership and jointly assessed;
- employer pension contributions;
- and how long the expensive childcare period lasts.

For some households, paid work creates a large immediate cash gain.

For others — particularly with two or three children simultaneously in full-time childcare — the short-term gain can become surprisingly small.

But **short-term household cash is not the same thing as long-term financial value**.

That distinction matters.

## Start with take-home pay, not gross salary

Take a €45,000 gross salary.

Under the calculator's simplified 2026 single-person PAYE assumptions, with no employee pension contribution, estimated annual take-home pay is approximately:

**€37,010**

That is the amount available before childcare, commuting and other work-related costs.

The article's examples use the same simplified tax engine as the [Childcare Return-to-Work Calculator](/childcare-return-to-work-calculator/) so the figures can be reproduced there.

Your actual payroll result may differ because of:

- marital status;
- jointly assessed tax bands;
- additional tax credits;
- pension deductions;
- benefits;
- medical-card USC rules;
- different PRSI treatment;
- or other personal circumstances.

For a more isolated salary calculation, use the [Take-Home Pay Calculator](/take-home-pay-calculator/).

## The National Childcare Scheme changes the calculation materially

The National Childcare Scheme has two broad forms of support:

- a **universal subsidy**;
- an **income-assessed subsidy**.

The universal subsidy is currently:

**€2.14 per hour**

for up to:

**45 hours per week**

for eligible children using participating childcare.

The HSE describes it as available regardless of family income.

[HSE: childcare options and NCS](https://www2.hse.ie/babies-children/parenting-advice/services-supports/childcare-options-free-pre-school-scheme/).

Income-assessed support can be higher.

From September 2026, the Government increased the relevant NCS income thresholds:

- lower threshold: **€34,000**;
- upper threshold: **€68,000**.

The actual award depends on reckonable household income and circumstances, including child age and family composition.

[Department of Children: 2026 NCS threshold changes](https://www.gov.ie/en/department-of-children-disability-and-equality/press-releases/minister-foley-publishes-two-new-action-plans-for-the-early-learning-and-care-and-school-age-childcare-sector/).

That is why the calculator does **not** try to guess your income-assessed award.

If you have an NCS award, enter the actual hourly rate shown to you.

## Worked example 1: €45,000 salary, one child

Assume:

| Input | Example |
| --- | ---: |
| Gross salary | €45,000 |
| Estimated take-home pay | €37,010 |
| Children in paid childcare | 1 |
| Childcare hours | 40 hours/week |
| Gross childcare fee | €8/hour |
| NCS subsidy entered | €2.14/hour |
| Childcare weeks | 48 |
| Commuting | €60/week |
| Other work costs | €30/week |

These are **illustrative assumptions**, not national average childcare prices.

### Gross childcare cost

**40 hours × €8 × 48 weeks = €15,360**

### Universal NCS support modelled

**40 × €2.14 × 48 = €4,108.80**

### Net childcare cost

**€15,360 - €4,108.80 = €11,251.20**

### Commuting and other work costs

**€90 × 48 = €4,320**

### Additional household cash from working

**€37,010 - €11,251 - €4,320 ≈ €21,439**

Under those assumptions, returning to work adds roughly:

**€21,439 a year**

of household cash after the modelled costs.

Across 37.5 paid hours a week for 48 weeks, that is approximately:

**€11.91 of household financial gain per paid working hour**.

That is not the employee's wage rate.

It is the modelled amount left for the household after the costs specifically entered.

## The same salary with two children looks very different

Now keep everything the same but put **two children** into the same 40-hour childcare arrangement.

Gross childcare:

**€30,720**

Universal NCS support modelled:

**€8,217.60**

Net childcare:

**€22,502.40**

Work-related costs remain:

**€4,320**

Estimated household cash gain:

**€37,010 - €22,502 - €4,320 ≈ €10,188**

The gross salary is still €45,000.

But the modelled additional household cash has fallen from about:

**€21,439**

with one child to:

**€10,188**

with two.

Effective household cash gain per paid hour falls to approximately:

**€5.66/hour**.

This is why families can reach very different conclusions even on identical salaries.

The number of children simultaneously requiring paid care can dominate the short-term calculation.

## Three children can push the short-term cash result below zero

Using the same assumptions for three children:

- estimated take-home: €37,010;
- net childcare after the modelled universal NCS support: about €33,754;
- work costs: €4,320.

The simplified result becomes approximately:

**-€1,063 a year**.

In other words, under those exact assumptions, the entered salary would not fully cover the entered childcare and work costs after tax.

That is not a claim that a parent with three children "should not work".

It demonstrates why a household needs to model the **actual** number of children, fees, subsidies and work costs rather than relying on salary alone.

An income-assessed NCS subsidy could also change the result materially.

## The childcare years are temporary

A common error is to project today's childcare burden indefinitely.

Childcare requirements change as children age.

One child may:

- enter ECCE;
- begin primary school;
- need only before- or after-school care;
- stop needing formal paid care altogether.

Two children who overlap in full-time childcare for two years may not create the same cost for the next ten years.

A useful household calculation therefore has at least two layers:

### Today's cash-flow test

What does returning to work add **this year**?

### Multi-year test

How does the result change when one child moves out of full-time care?

A return-to-work decision that looks marginal for 18 months can look completely different over five or ten years.

## Do not charge all childcare to one parent's salary

This is one of the most misleading ways to frame the decision.

Suppose one parent earns €45,000 and the other earns €70,000.

The family pays €20,000 net childcare.

It is tempting to say:

> "The lower earner works for €45,000 and childcare costs €20,000, so half the salary is gone."

But childcare is a **household cost created by the household's working arrangement**.

There is no economic rule saying the entire childcare bill belongs to the lower earner.

A better approach is to compare:

**household finances if both work**

with:

**household finances if one does not work**.

That captures tax, childcare and work expenses on the same basis.

## Married couples need to consider the household tax position

This is especially important in Ireland.

For 2026, Revenue's standard-rate band for a married couple or civil partners with one income is:

**€53,000**.

Where both have taxable income, the standard-rate band can increase by the lower of:

- **€35,000**;
- or the lower earner's income.

The additional band cannot simply be transferred in full to the other spouse.

[Revenue: tax rate bands](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/calculating-your-income-tax/tax-rate-band.aspx).

So when a second person enters paid employment, the household tax calculation can change.

The current Compound childcare calculator deliberately uses simplified single-person payroll assumptions rather than pretending it can reconstruct every married couple's Revenue allocation.

For a high-stakes household decision, use your actual Tax Credit Certificates or Revenue figures alongside the childcare calculation.

## The Home Carer Tax Credit can also change

For jointly assessed married couples or civil partners, Revenue's Home Carer Tax Credit is worth up to:

**€1,950 in 2026**.

The full credit is available where the home carer's qualifying income is below €7,200, then tapers, and is unavailable once that income reaches €11,100 in 2026.

[Revenue: Home Carer Tax Credit](https://www.revenue.ie/en/personal-tax-credits-reliefs-and-exemptions/health-and-age/home-carer-credit/home-carer-tax-credit-rates.aspx).

Revenue also notes that a couple cannot use the increased two-income standard-rate band in the same year that they claim the Home Carer Tax Credit.

That creates another reason why:

**returning worker's take-home pay minus childcare**

can be an incomplete household calculation.

The other spouse's tax position can change too.

## Part-time work is not automatically the middle-ground winner

Suppose working three days rather than five cuts childcare by 40%.

That sounds attractive.

But the salary may also fall by 40%.

The result depends on:

- whether childcare can actually be purchased for fewer days;
- whether the provider charges for a full-time place regardless;
- NCS hours;
- tax;
- commute frequency;
- pension contributions;
- and the salary structure.

A four-day week can produce very different economics from a three-day week.

The correct comparison is to run each arrangement as a separate scenario.

## Commuting can be bigger than it looks

Assume commuting costs:

**€12 per working day**

for fuel, parking, tolls or public transport.

Five days a week for 48 weeks:

**€12 × 5 × 48 = €2,880 a year**

That is paid from after-tax income.

If hybrid work reduces commuting to two days a week:

**€12 × 2 × 48 = €1,152**

Difference:

**€1,728 a year**.

Hybrid working can therefore alter the childcare-return calculation even if salary is unchanged.

## Add the costs that only exist because you work

Depending on the household, these may include:

- commuting;
- parking;
- tolls;
- additional meals;
- professional clothing;
- childcare;
- extra cleaning;
- after-school care;
- school-holiday cover;
- convenience spending caused by time pressure.

Not every household has all of these costs.

Do not invent them.

But do not ignore real costs simply because they are spread across several bank-card transactions.

The calculator includes a general weekly work-cost field and an annual additional-cost field for this reason.

## Then remove costs that would exist anyway

The opposite mistake is just as common.

A household may count:

- the entire second car;
- all food;
- the full mortgage;
- every child's activity;

as a "cost of working".

That overstates the incremental cost.

The correct question is:

> **Would this cost exist, or be materially different, if I did not return to paid work?**

Only the difference belongs in the calculation.

## Pension contributions are real compensation

Imagine the €45,000 job comes with a 5% employer pension contribution.

That is:

**€2,250 a year**

being contributed to retirement savings by the employer.

Over five years, even with a completely flat salary and ignoring investment growth:

**€11,250**

would have been contributed.

If the employee also contributes and receives tax relief where applicable, the retirement effect can be larger.

The immediate childcare calculation does not capture that value unless you add it separately.

Use the [Pension Projection Calculator](/pension-projection-calculator/) if you want to examine the long-term effect of pension contributions.

## MyFutureFund can matter too

Where an employee is eligible for MyFutureFund and does not have a qualifying payroll pension arrangement, returning to employment can also create employer and State retirement contributions.

In 2026–2028, the scheduled MyFutureFund contribution structure is:

- employee: 1.5%;
- employer: 1.5%;
- State: 0.5%.

Those rates rise over time.

The immediate household cash calculation and the retirement-savings calculation are therefore not identical.

For the structural differences, see [MyFutureFund vs a private pension in Ireland](/wealth/myfuturefund-vs-private-pension-ireland/).

## Future salary is harder to quantify — but should not be assumed to be zero

Suppose a job pays €45,000 today and salary rises by an illustrative 3% per year.

After five annual increases:

**€45,000 × 1.03⁵ ≈ €52,167**

That is not a forecast.

It simply demonstrates that the relevant comparison may not be:

> €45,000 now versus €0 now.

A longer absence from a career can change:

- accumulated experience;
- progression;
- promotion opportunities;
- employer pension contributions;
- future salary path.

Those effects vary enormously by occupation and employer, so they should not be assigned one universal euro value.

But assigning them a value of **zero** is also an assumption.

## Irish research shows childcare costs affect work decisions

This is not merely a household-budget theory.

ESRI and Pobal research using Growing Up in Ireland data found that higher childcare costs were associated with mothers working fewer paid hours.

The study estimated that a 10% increase in childcare cost was associated with about a **30-minute reduction in weekly paid work** among mothers in the studied population.

The cost data were historical — largely from 2011 — so the euro figures in that older research should not be treated as current childcare prices.

The behavioural finding is the relevant point.

[ESRI: Maternal employment and the cost of childcare in Ireland](https://www.esri.ie/publications/maternal-employment-and-the-cost-of-childcare-in-ireland).

More recent ESRI research published in 2025 modelled the National Childcare Scheme and found that childcare subsidies could modestly increase maternal labour-market participation while also shifting families from informal to formal childcare.

[ESRI: childcare subsidies and labour supply](https://www.esri.ie/publications/will-childcare-subsidies-increase-the-labour-supply-of-mothers-in-ireland-0).

## Caring responsibilities still show up clearly in Irish employment data

CSO's **Women in the Labour Market 2024–2025** report found that women made up:

**67.1% of people in part-time employment**

in Q4 2025.

Among women working part time, the largest reported reason was:

**looking after children or incapacitated adults — 29.7%**.

For men working part time, the corresponding figure was 7.0%.

[CSO: Women in the Labour Market 2024–2025](https://www.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-wlm/womeninthelabourmarket2024-2025/keyfindings/).

These statistics describe population patterns.

They do not determine what arrangement is right for any individual family.

## The NCS threshold change matters in 2026

The September 2026 NCS changes are particularly relevant to this calculation.

The lower income threshold increased from:

**€26,000 → €34,000**

and the upper threshold increased from:

**€60,000 → €68,000**.

The Government also increased multiple-child discounts in the income assessment.

That means a family whose older calculation showed only the universal €2.14 subsidy may now qualify for a different income-assessed result.

Before making a decision from an old childcare quote, check the current NCS award.

## Core Funding fee caps also changed

From September 2026, new lower maximum fees apply to services participating in Core Funding.

The Department says the maximum parent-facing cost for a typical 45-hour full-day place falls further after the universal NCS subsidy is applied, with higher subsidies available for qualifying families.

The important practical point is not to use a generic national crèche price in your household calculation.

Use:

1. the fee from the provider you can realistically access;
2. the provider's NCS participation;
3. your actual subsidy award;
4. the number of weeks you genuinely need care.

## School holidays can break an otherwise neat calculation

A term-time arrangement may look affordable.

Then summer arrives.

Parents may need:

- summer camps;
- extra crèche weeks;
- childminder cover;
- annual leave;
- unpaid leave;
- family help.

A realistic annual calculation should include the **full year**, not one representative school-term month multiplied by twelve.

The calculator therefore asks for the number of paid childcare / working weeks.

## Childcare availability can matter as much as childcare price

A theoretical €8/hour place is not useful if there is no available place near home or work.

Similarly, an NCS subsidy only reduces the price of qualifying care actually accessible to the family.

The financial model should therefore use an available arrangement, not the cheapest childcare option found online.

## A one-year result can hide a five-year result

Consider a simplified family where the additional cash from working is only:

**€8,000 in year one**

because two children are simultaneously in childcare.

If one child leaves full-time care two years later, the household gain could rise sharply even if salary did not.

Meanwhile, the worker may also have accumulated:

- two more years of employment;
- salary progression;
- employer pension contributions;
- social-insurance contributions;
- professional experience.

The best decision horizon is therefore usually longer than the next twelve months.

## How to compare "stay home" versus "return to work"

Build two household scenarios.

### Scenario A — one parent does not return

Include:

- other household income;
- tax position;
- Home Carer Tax Credit if applicable;
- childcare still required for any reason;
- commuting avoided;
- work costs avoided;
- pension contributions forgone.

### Scenario B — parent returns

Include:

- after-tax salary;
- actual NCS award;
- net childcare;
- commute;
- work costs;
- pension contributions;
- changed household tax bands or credits.

Then calculate:

**Scenario B household resources - Scenario A household resources**

That is much closer to the true financial difference.

## A useful break-even calculation

Suppose returning to work creates:

- €11,251 net childcare;
- €4,320 commuting/work costs.

Total additional annual cash costs:

**€15,571**

You therefore need at least:

**€15,571 of additional after-tax household income**

to reach cash break-even under those assumptions.

The gross salary required to produce that take-home amount depends on the household tax situation.

That is why a universal answer such as:

> "You need to earn €30,000 for crèche to be worth it"

is unreliable.

## The most useful number is not "childcare as a percentage of salary"

Suppose net childcare is €11,251 and gross salary is €45,000.

You could say childcare equals 25% of gross salary.

But that is not particularly useful.

A better number is:

**additional household cash after tax, childcare and work costs**.

In our one-child example:

**about €21,439**

In our two-child example:

**about €10,188**

Those numbers describe the actual short-term trade-off much more clearly.

## The practical order to work through it

First, get your actual childcare quote.

Second, check your current NCS award — particularly after the September 2026 threshold changes.

Third, estimate real take-home pay.

Fourth, add only the commuting and work costs that genuinely arise because you work.

Fifth, check whether the household loses or changes any tax credit or rate-band entitlement.

Sixth, separately record employer pension contributions or MyFutureFund contributions.

Finally, repeat the calculation for the year when one child leaves full-time childcare.

Then use the [Childcare Cost vs Returning to Work Calculator](/childcare-return-to-work-calculator/) with the actual figures.

The question is not simply:

> Is my salary bigger than the crèche bill?

It is:

> **What does paid work add to our household now, how long will the childcare squeeze last, and what longer-term financial value sits outside this year's cash-flow calculation?**

That produces a much more useful answer.
