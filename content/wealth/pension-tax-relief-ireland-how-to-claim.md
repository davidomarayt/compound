Pension tax relief means the money you put into a pension is taken off your income before income tax is worked out. Revenue gives that relief at your marginal rate, which is the highest rate you pay, so a contribution from income taxed at 40% cuts your tax bill by 40% of the amount you put in ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/index.aspx)). If all of your income is taxed at 20%, the relief is 20%.

There are limits. Relief is capped at a percentage of your earnings that rises with your age, from 15% if you are under 30 to 40% at 60 or over, and only the first €115,000 of earnings counts ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/tax-relief-limits.aspx)). For most PAYE workers (PAYE is Pay As You Earn, the system where your employer deducts tax before you are paid) the relief arrives automatically through payroll. If you pay into a pension yourself, you claim it, and a lump sum paid before the pay-and-file deadline can be set against last year's income.

One note on who this is for. It covers private-sector employees in defined contribution schemes, sole traders, and anyone with a PRSA (Personal Retirement Savings Account, a personal pension you own and keep when you change jobs). It does not cover public sector or defined benefit scheme members, where the scheme promises a pension linked to your salary and the contribution and relief mechanics work differently. Nor does it cover very high earners close to the Standard Fund Threshold, the lifetime cap on the total pension fund that can get tax relief, which has its own set of rules.

## What does pension tax relief actually mean, and what is it worth at 20% and 40%?

Income tax in Ireland is charged at 20% and 40%. For 2026 a single person pays 20% on the first €44,000 of income and 40% on the balance ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/personal-tax-credits-reliefs-and-exemptions/tax-relief-charts/index.aspx)). Pension relief works by treating your contribution as if it were never earned, so it comes off the top slice of your income first.

That is why the relief is worth more to someone earning above the band. If part of your income is taxed at 40%, a contribution up to that amount saves you 40% in income tax. If you are entirely inside the 20% band, the saving is 20%. Revenue's wording is that "Income Tax relief is given at your 'marginal' (highest) tax rate" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/index.aspx)).

Here is the catch, and it matters for the sums you see on pension adverts. The relief is from income tax only. Revenue states that "There is no relief from Universal Social Charge (USC) or Pay Related Social Insurance (PRSI) for employee pension contributions" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/index.aspx)). USC is a separate levy on gross income, and PRSI is the contribution that funds the State Pension and other benefits. So the cut in your take-home pay is a bit bigger than the income tax saving alone suggests.

## How much can you put in? The age-related limits and the €115,000 earnings cap

Revenue caps the contributions that qualify for relief at a percentage of your earnings, based on your age ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/tax-relief-limits.aspx)):

- Under 30: 15%
- 30 to 39: 20%
- 40 to 49: 25%
- 50 to 54: 30%
- 55 to 59: 35%
- 60 or over: 40%

The earnings counted for the calculation are capped. "The maximum amount of earnings taken into account for calculating tax relief is €115,000 per year" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/tax-relief-limits.aspx)). Revenue's own example is an employee aged 42 earning €40,000 a year, who "can get tax relief on annual pension contributions up to €10,000" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/tax-relief-limits.aspx)).

If your employer also pays into your scheme, that does not use up your allowance. "Employer contributions to an employee's scheme are not taken into consideration when calculating the employee's earnings threshold" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/tax-relief-limits.aspx)). For a sole trader, the earnings figure is the profit from the business rather than turnover, and it is worth checking the number with whoever prepares your accounts.

You can pay in more than the limit, but the excess gets no relief that year.

## If you're a PAYE worker, is the relief automatic — and what if your employer doesn't do it?

If you are in a workplace pension scheme, the answer is usually yes. Revenue says: "Usually your employer deducts the contributions directly from your pay, and will give you the tax relief due" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/how-to-claim.aspx)). You do not need to do anything.

If your employer does not deduct the contributions, you claim the relief yourself through myAccount, Revenue's online service for PAYE workers ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/how-to-claim.aspx)). Sign in, go to "Review your tax for the previous 4 years" under PAYE Services, request a Statement of Liability, then complete the Income Tax Return. On the Tax Credits and Reliefs page, under "Your job", pick either AVCs or PRSAs and enter the policy details.

If your job has no pension scheme at all, your employer must give you access to at least one Standard PRSA ([Citizens Information](https://www.citizensinformation.ie/en/money-and-tax/personal-finance/pensions/personal-retirement-savings-accounts/)). Ask HR or payroll which provider they use.

## How do you claim relief on a PRSA or an AVC you paid yourself?

An AVC (Additional Voluntary Contribution) is an extra payment you make on top of your workplace scheme, and a PRSA you fund yourself is treated in a similar way. Both go in through the Income Tax Return, either in myAccount for a PAYE worker or on the Form 11, the self-assessment return, through ROS (Revenue Online Service) if you are self-employed ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/how-to-claim.aspx)).

Keep the paperwork. "To claim AVCs, a copy of the certificate, or detailed receipt, must be submitted when filing your Income Tax Return" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/how-to-claim.aspx)). Your provider issues this, and if you do not have it you can upload a document showing the payment date, amount, proof of payment, policy number and the names and addresses of you and the provider.

Enter your date of birth accurately on the return. That is what tells Revenue which age-related percentage you get.

## Can you still get relief for last year? The 31 October and ROS deadlines

This is the part most people miss. Revenue's guidance on PRSA AVCs says: "You may pay a once-off, or special, contribution after the end of a tax year. This must be done before the following 31 October" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/contributions-to-prsa-avc.aspx)). You can then choose to have the relief given in the earlier year, and that choice must also be made before 31 October ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/contributions-to-prsa-avc.aspx)).

For the 2025 tax year, "The Pay and File deadline for the 2025 Income Tax Return (Form 11) is Saturday 31 October 2026" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/self-assessment-and-self-employment/filing-your-tax-return/index.aspx)). If you both file and pay through ROS, "The deadline for paying and filing on ROS for the 2025 Income Tax Return (Form 11) is Wednesday 18 November 2026" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/self-assessment-and-self-employment/filing-your-tax-return/index.aspx)).

The ROS extension also moves the pension deadlines, and because this is the claim most people find hard to believe, here is Revenue's exact wording ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/relief/contributions-to-prsa-avc.aspx)):

> When you use the Revenue Online Service (ROS), the deadlines for paying contributions and making this choice are extended.

In practice this means a sole trader who has just worked out their 2025 profit, and sees a chunk of it taxed at 40%, can still make a pension payment now and set it against 2025. The same applies to a PAYE worker paying an AVC, as long as you stay inside your age-related limit for that year.

If you paid into a pension in earlier years and never claimed, you have four years to fix it. Revenue's example: "claims for 2022 must be made by 31 December 2026" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/personal-tax-credits-reliefs-and-exemptions/four-year-rule/index.aspx)). After that, "Claims made after this time cannot be repaid" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/personal-tax-credits-reliefs-and-exemptions/four-year-rule/index.aspx)).

## What about USC and PRSI — and what tax do you pay when you draw the money down?

As above, for an employee USC and PRSI are charged on your full pay before the pension contribution comes out. Only income tax is relieved.

Sole traders sit in a slightly different spot. You pay PRSI under Class S rather than the employee's Class A, and it is worked out on your profit through the Form 11 rather than taken through payroll. Revenue's no-relief wording above is written about employee contributions, so do not assume your pension payment lowers the USC or Class S PRSI lines on your own computation. Check them, or ask whoever prepares your return.

The relief is a deferral, not a gift. When you retire, "Personal pensions and occupational pensions are taxable sources of income. They are liable to Income Tax and Universal Social Charge (USC). They may also be liable to Pay Related Social Insurance (PRSI) in the same way as employment income" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/private/index.aspx)). Pension income is taxed under PAYE at the same rates and bands as any other income, and "Your pension provider will deduct the tax from each payment it makes to you" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/private/index.aspx)).

The sweetener is the retirement lump sum. "The tax free amount is a lifetime entitlement of €200,000 on retirement lump sums from all sources" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/private/retirement-lump-sums.aspx)). "The amount between €200,001 and €500,000 is taxable at the standard rate of tax (20%)", and "Any amount in excess of €500,000 is taxed under PAYE at the higher tax rate (40%)" ([Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/jobs-and-pensions/pension/private/retirement-lump-sums.aspx)).

So the bet you are making is that relief at 40% now, plus tax-free growth and a tax-free lump sum, beats paying tax on the pension later, when many people are on a lower rate. For most people on the higher rate that bet is a good one. For someone entirely on 20% it is closer, and the case rests more on the employer contribution and the lump sum than on the relief.

## What should you actually do this month?

One-third of workers in the State had no pension cover in 2025, and of those, almost half (49%) said they never got around to organising it or would do it at a future date ([CSO](https://www.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-pens/pensioncoverage2025/keyfindings/)). This month is a good time to be in the other half.

- **If you are in a workplace scheme**, check a payslip. The contribution should come off before tax. If the tax saving is not showing, ask payroll, or claim it in myAccount.
- **If you paid an AVC or PRSA yourself in 2025**, get the certificate from your provider and file the claim. You have until 31 October 2026, or 18 November 2026 if you file and pay on ROS.
- **If you are a sole trader doing your 2025 return**, look at how much profit sits above the 20% band. A contribution up to your age-related limit, paid before the deadline and elected against 2025, gets relief at 40% on that slice.
- **If you have no pension at all**, ask your employer which Standard PRSA they provide. A PRSA provider cannot insist on more than €300 a year as a minimum contribution ([Citizens Information](https://www.citizensinformation.ie/en/money-and-tax/personal-finance/pensions/personal-retirement-savings-accounts/)), and you can stop, start or change the amount without charge.
- **If you missed a claim from 2022**, it has to be in by 31 December 2026 or it is gone.

The relief is real, and the October window is genuinely useful. Just keep the arithmetic honest: the saving is on income tax only, it is only 40% on the income actually taxed at 40%, and the tax is deferred rather than forgiven.
