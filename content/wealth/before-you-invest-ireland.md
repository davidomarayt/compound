**Investing in Ireland 101 · Chapter 1 of 9**

[Course hub](/wealth/how-to-start-investing-in-ireland/) · [Next: Pension vs taxable investing →](/wealth/pension-vs-investing-ireland/)

Most investing mistakes happen before the first investment is bought.

The problem is usually not the lack of a clever stock pick. It is using money that had another job, taking risk over the wrong time horizon, or trying to invest while expensive debt and weak cash reserves make the plan fragile.

The CCPC's starting point is deliberately unglamorous: be clear about your goal, your time horizon and the amount of risk you are comfortable taking before choosing an investment.

That is the right order.

## The four questions to answer first

### 1. What is the money actually for?

“Build wealth” is not specific enough.

A house deposit needed in two years, money for a child in fifteen years and retirement savings for thirty years are three different problems. They should not automatically sit in the same account or take the same level of risk.

Write the goal as a sentence:

> I need approximately €___ for ___, no earlier than ___.

That one line gives you a target, a date and a minimum period the money can stay untouched.

### 2. What happens if an unexpected bill arrives next month?

Investing works best when you are not forced to sell.

If all available cash is invested and the boiler, car or roof suddenly needs money, the market gets to choose your selling date. A temporary market fall can then become a realised loss.

An emergency reserve is therefore part of the investment system, not a competing use of money.

There is no single official number that fits every household. A person with secure employment, two incomes and low fixed costs may need a different buffer from a self-employed household with one income and high monthly commitments.

Use the [Emergency Fund Calculator](/emergency-fund-calculator/) to turn your own essential spending into a cash target.

## Cash is not “failing to invest”

Cash has a job.

Money that may be needed soon should prioritise certainty and access. Long-term money can accept more short-term volatility because it has time to recover.

That distinction matters more than whether one savings account pays a few tenths of a percentage point more than another.

A useful way to divide money is by **time to use**, not by excitement:

| When the money may be needed | Main priority | What matters most |
|---|---|---|
| Days to months | Access and stability | Liquidity, deposit protection, no market risk |
| 1–3 years | Capital preservation | Avoiding a forced sale after a market fall |
| 3–7 years | Balance | Goal flexibility, risk tolerance, asset mix |
| 7+ years | Long-term growth | Diversification, fees, tax and staying invested |

These are planning bands, not guarantees. A diversified market portfolio can still be down after several years.

## Should you pay off debt before investing?

The answer depends on the debt.

Paying €1,000 off debt charging 20% interest avoids roughly €200 of annual interest before compounding and fees. That saving is known. A 20% investment return is not.

That does not mean every euro of a low-rate mortgage should be repaid before any investing begins. It means **expensive consumer debt deserves to be compared with investment returns on a risk-adjusted basis**.

Ask:

- What is the effective interest rate?
- Is it fixed or variable?
- Is there a penalty for early repayment?
- Would paying it down improve monthly cash flow?
- What investment return would be required, after tax and fees, to beat the guaranteed interest saving?

The higher and less predictable the debt cost, the harder it is to justify ignoring it.

## Time horizon changes what “risk” means

Risk is not just “the investment might fall”.

There are several risks:

**Market risk:** the value falls before you sell.

**Inflation risk:** cash preserves the euro amount but loses purchasing power.

**Liquidity risk:** you cannot get money out when you need it.

**Behaviour risk:** you panic and sell after a fall.

**Goal risk:** the portfolio does not deliver enough money by the date you need it.

A long investment horizon can reduce the importance of short-term market movements, but it does not make risk disappear.

## What would you do after a 30% fall?

This is one of the most useful questions a beginner can ask.

Imagine investing €20,000 and seeing €14,000 on the screen several months later.

Would you:

- keep contributing;
- do nothing;
- reduce risk;
- or sell everything?

There is no virtue in choosing the most aggressive answer. The purpose of the exercise is to choose a portfolio you can realistically hold.

A theoretically efficient portfolio that you abandon during the first major fall is not an efficient portfolio for you.

## The difference between capacity and tolerance

**Risk tolerance** is how emotionally comfortable you are with losses.

**Risk capacity** is how much loss your financial situation can absorb.

You can have high tolerance and low capacity. Someone may enjoy volatility but need the money for a deposit in eighteen months.

You can also have low tolerance and high capacity. Someone may have a thirty-year horizon but sleep badly during every market decline.

Both matter.

## A practical pre-investing checklist

Before opening a brokerage account, you should be able to answer these questions:

| Question | A strong answer sounds like |
|---|---|
| What is the goal? | “Retirement income from age 65” |
| When is the money needed? | “Not for at least 25 years” |
| What cash is separate? | “Emergency fund and known near-term spending” |
| Is expensive debt under control? | “Yes, or I have a deliberate repayment plan” |
| What loss could I tolerate? | “I understand a large temporary fall is possible” |
| How much can I contribute? | “An amount I can sustain every month” |
| What will trigger a review? | “Life changes, not daily headlines” |

If several answers are unclear, the next step is usually clarification, not investment selection.

## How much cash should stay outside investments?

Think in layers.

**Operating cash** covers normal monthly bills.

**Emergency cash** covers shocks.

**Known future spending** covers commitments such as tax bills, a car purchase, renovations or a house deposit.

Only after those jobs are separated does long-term investment capital become obvious.

The correct amount depends on your household. The [Emergency Fund Calculator](/emergency-fund-calculator/) gives you a starting point based on your own essential spending.

## What if you only have €100 available?

That is enough to build the habit.

The important early variable is not finding the “perfect” fund. It is creating a repeatable system.

At an illustrative 7% annual return, €100 invested at the end of every month would grow to about:

| Period | Contributions | Illustrative value |
|---|---:|---:|
| 10 years | €12,000 | €17,308 |
| 20 years | €24,000 | €52,093 |
| 30 years | €36,000 | €121,997 |

This is not a forecast. Markets do not return 7% smoothly and tax, fees and inflation can materially change the result.

Use the [Compound Interest Calculator](/compound-interest-calculator/) to test your own assumptions.

## The first decision is not “what should I buy?”

It is:

**Which money can genuinely stay invested?**

Once that is clear, the next decision is where that money should live. In Ireland, the tax treatment of a pension can be radically different from the tax treatment of an ordinary brokerage account.

That is Chapter 2.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need an emergency fund before investing?

There is no universal rule, but a separate cash reserve can reduce the risk that an unexpected expense forces you to sell investments at a bad time.

### Should I invest if I have a mortgage?

A mortgage does not automatically prevent investing. Compare the mortgage rate, repayment terms, pension benefits, tax treatment, time horizon and your need for liquidity.

### Is three years long enough to invest in shares?

Three years is a short horizon for assets that can fall sharply. If the goal date is fixed, capital preservation may matter more than expected return.

### What is the most important thing to know before investing?

Know the goal and the earliest date the money might be needed. That determines how much risk the plan can reasonably carry.
